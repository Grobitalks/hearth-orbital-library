NOTES TO BOETHIUS.<b>BOOK I.</b>

METRE 1. In order to elucidate the English text, I frequently quote the original Latin, usually from the text of T. Obbarius, Jena, 1843. See further in the Introduction.

3. <i>rendinge</i>, Lat. 'lacerae'; rather rent, or tattered. The sense 'rending' occurs in Ovid, Met. viii. 880.

6. <i>that is to seyn.</i> The words in italics are not in the original, but were added by Chaucer as explanatory. Throughout the treatise, I print all such passages in italics.

8. <i>werdes</i>, 'weirds,' fate.

'Gloria felicis olim uiridisque iuuentae
   Solantur maesti nunc mea fata senis.'

12. <i>slake</i>, better <i>slakke</i>; cf. Cant. Ta. E. 1849. <i>empted</i>, 'effeto.' MS. C. has <i>emty</i>.

13. <i>in yeres ... swete</i>: 'dulcibus annis.'

14. <i>y-cleped</i>, invoked; 'uocata,' sc. 'mors.' Cf. Troilus, iv. 503.

16. <i>naiteth</i>, refuseth; 'negat.' Icel. <i>neita</i>, to say nay.17. <i>lighte</i>, i.e. transitory; 'leuibus ... bonis.' The gloss 'sc. temporels' (in A) gives the right sense. <i>sc.</i> = scilicet, namely; the form <i>temporels</i> is the French plural.

18, 19. <i>But now</i>:

'Nunc quia fallacem mutauit nubila uultum,
   Protrahit ingratas impia uita moras.'

The translation <i>unagreable dwellinges</i> is an unhappy one.

22. <i>in stedefast degree</i>, in a secure position; 'stabili ... gradu.'

With regard to the last sentence, Mr. Stewart remarks, in his essay on Boethius, that Chaucer here 'actually reproduces the original Latin metre,' i.e. a hexameter and pentameter. The true M. E. pronunciation must, for this purpose, be entirely neglected; which amounts to saying that Chaucer must have been profoundly unconscious of any such intention.

PROSE 1. 2. <i>and markede</i>: 'querimoniamque lacrimabilem stili officio designarem.' Hence <i>markede</i> is 'wrote down'; and <i>pointel</i> refers to the <i>stilus</i>. Cf. Som. Tale, D 1742. <i>with office</i>, by the use (of).

6. <i>empted</i>, exhausted; 'inexhausti uigoris.' Of course the woman here described is <i>Philosophia</i>.9. <i>doutous</i>; 'statura discretionis ambiguae.'

12. <i>heef</i>, heaved; A. S. <i>h[=o]f</i>. In Layamon, <i>hof</i>, <i>haf</i>, <i>heaf</i>. I put <i>heef</i> for <i>hef</i>, because the <i>e</i> is long.

13. <i>so that</i>: 'respicientiumque hominum frustrabatur intuitum.'

14. <i>delye</i> (so in both MSS.) = <i>deli-[=e]</i>, O. F. <i>deliè</i> (see Cotgrave), delicate, thin, slender, from Lat. <i>delicatus</i>, with the usual loss of <i>c</i> between two vowels and before the accented syllable; Lat. 'tenuissimis filis.'

After <i>crafte</i> it would have been better to insert <i>and</i>; Lat. 'indissolubili_que_ materiâ.' But some MSS., including C., omit <i>que</i>.

18. <i>as it is wont</i>: 'ueluti fumosas imagines solet.'

21. <i>a Grekissh P</i>; i.e. [PI]. <i>a Grekissh T</i>; i.e. [THETA], not [TAU]; the Greek [theta] being pronounced as <i>t</i> in Latin. The reference is to [Greek: philosophia praktikê kai theôrêtikê]; in Latin, Philosophia Actiua et Contemplatiua; i. e. Practical (or Active) and Theoretical (or Contemplative) Philosophy. This is the same distinction as that between the <i>Vita Actiua</i> and <i>Vita Contemplatiua</i>, so common in medieval literature; see note (3) to the Sec. Non. Tale, G 87; and note to P. Plowman, B. vi. 251.

26. <i>corven</i>, cut, cut away pieces from; Lat. 'sciderant.'33. <i>cruel</i>, i. e. stern; 'toruis.'

34. <i>thise comune</i>: 'has scenicas meretriculas.'

39. <i>no-thing fructefyinge</i>; 'infructuosis.' Hence we may perhaps prefer to read <i>no-thing fructuous</i>, as in Caxton and Thynne.

41. <i>holden</i>: 'hominumque mentes assuefaciunt morbo, non liberant.'

45. <i>for-why</i>, because (very common); seldom interrogative.

47. <i>me</i>, from me; and, in fact, Caxton and Thynne read <i>from me</i> or <i>fro me</i>. The forms <i>Eleaticis</i>, &c. are due to the Lat. text--'Eleaticis atque Academicis studiis.' He should rather have said--'scoles of Elea and of the Academie.' The <i>Eleatici philosophi</i> were the followers of Zeno of Elea (Zeno Eleates, born about B. C. 488 at Elea (Velia) in Italy), and the favourite disciple of Parmenides (who is expressly mentioned in Book iii. pr. 12, l. 143). The Academic philosophers were followers of Plato.

49. <i>mermaidenes</i>; Lat. 'Sirenes,' Sirens; cf. N. P. Tale, B 4461, and note.

<i>til it be at the laste</i>; a false translation. Rather <i>unto destruction</i>; 'usque in exitium.' But, instead of <i>exitium</i>, MS. C. has <i>exitum</i>.

55. <i>plounged</i>, drowned; 'mersa.' Cf. <i>dreint</i>, Met. 2, l. 1.59. <i>ner</i>, nearer; comparative, not positive; 'propius.'

METRE 2. 2. <i>mintinge</i>, intending; 'tendit ... ire.' Still in use in Cambridgeshire.

8. <i>sterres of the cold moon</i>: 'gelidae sidera lunae.' I suppose this means the constellations seen by moonlight, but invisible in the day. The expression <i>sidus lunae</i>, the moon's bright form, occurs in Pliny, Nat. Hist. ii. 9. 6; but it is difficult to see how <i>sidera</i> can have the same sense, as some commentators say.

9. <i>recourses</i>, orbits; referring to the planets.

<i>y-flit</i>, moved or whirled along by their different spheres; alluding to the old Ptolemaic system of astronomy, which supposed that each planet was fastened to a revolving sphere, thus causing it to perform its orbit in a certain time, varying in the case of each.

<i>this man</i>: 'Comprensam [sc. stellam] in numeris uictor habebat.'

16. <i>highteth</i>, adorns; 'ornet.' Prob. from the sb. <i>hight</i>, <i>hiht</i> (A. S. <i>hyht</i>), joy, delight.

17. <i>fleteth</i>, flows (i.e. abounds); 'grauidis influat uuis.'

20. <i>empted</i>: 'Nunc iacet effeto lumine mentis.'22. <i>fool</i>, i.e. foolish, witless, senseless; 'stolidam.'

PROSE 2. 6. <i>armures</i>, i.e. defensive armour; 'arma.'

8. <i>in sikernesse</i>: 'inuicta te firmitate tuerentur.'

14. <i>litargie</i>; better <i>letargye</i>, i.e. lethargy. Cf. Troil. i. 730.

19. <i>yplyted</i>, pleated into a wrinkle; 'contracta in rugam ueste.'

METRE 3. 1. <i>discussed</i>, driven away; 'discussâ ... nocte.'

4. <i>clustred</i>; 'glomerantur'; or 'covered with clouds,' as Chaucer says.

5. <i>Chorus</i>, Corus, or Caurus, the north-west wind.

6. <i>ploungy</i>, stormy, rainy; 'nimbosis ... imbribus.'

8. <i>Borias</i>, Boreas, the north wind, from Thrace.

9. <i>caves</i>; better <i>cave</i>, as in Caxton and Thynne; Lat. 'antro.' <i>beteth</i>; 'uerberet'; hence Chaucer's gloss.

16. <i>quasi diceret non</i>, as if she would say no; as if she expected the answer no. This remark is often inserted by Chaucer.

19. <i>Plato</i>; B.C. 428-347. Before his time, Solon, Anaxagoras, and Pythagoras all met with opposition. The fate of Socrates is well known.

21. <i>The heritage</i>: 'Cuius hereditatem cum deinceps Epicureum uulgus ac Stoicum, ceterique pro sua quisque parte raptum ire molirentur, meque reclamantem renitentemque uelut in partem praedae detraherent, uestem, quam meis texueram manibus, disciderunt, abreptisque ab ea panniculis, totam me sibi cessisse credentes abiere.'

38. <i>Anaxogore</i>, Anaxagoras, a Greek philosopher (B.C. 500-428); exiled from Athens (B.C. 450).

39. <i>Zeno</i>; Zeno of Elea (see p. 420), born about B.C. 488, is said to have risked his life to defend his country. His fate is doubtful.40. <i>Senecciens</i>, apparently meant for 'the followers of Seneca.' The original has: 'at Canios, at Senecas, at Soranos ... scire potuisti.'

<i>Canios</i>, the Canii; i. e. men like Canius. The constancy and death of Julius Canius (or Canus) is related by Seneca, De Tranquillitate, cap. xiv. Cf. Pr. iv. 131, and note, p. 424.

41. <i>Sorans</i>, the Sorani; men like Soranus. Soranus is mentioned in Tacitus, Annal. xvi. 23. Caxton and Thynne read <i>Soranos</i>, as in the Latin text.

42. <i>unsolempne</i>, uncelebrated; 'incelebris.'

49. <i>it is to dispyse</i>, it (the host) is to be despised.

53. <i>ententif</i>, busy about seizing useless baggage as spoil.

<i>sarpulers</i>, sacks made of coarse canvas; in Caxton, <i>sarpleris</i>; 'sarcinulas.' Cotgrave has: '<i>Serpillere</i>, a Sarpler, or Sarp-cloth, a piece of course canvas to pack up things in.' Cf. mod. F. <i>serpillière</i>.

56. <i>palis</i>, also spelt <i>paleis</i> (O. F. <i>palis</i>), lit. a palisading, or a piece of strong paling, a rampart, used to translate Lat. <i>uallum</i>. When spelt <i>paleis</i>, it must not be confused with <i>paleis</i>, a palace.
