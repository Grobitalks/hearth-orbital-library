<i>Page</i> 153. "What needeth all this travail and turmoiling?"--Suggested by Spenser's fifteenth sonnet:--

"Ye tradefull Merchants that with weary toyle
Do seeke most pretious things to make your gain,
And both the Indias of their treasure spoile,
What needeth you to seeke so farre in vaine?
For loe! my Love doth in her selfe containe
All this worlds riches that may farre be found.
If Saphyres, loe! her eies be Saphyres plaine;
If Rubies, loe! hir lips be Rubies sound;
If Pearles, hir teeth be pearles, both pure and round;
If Yvorie, her forehead yvory weene;
If Gold, her locks are finest gold on ground;
If Silver, her faire hands are silver sheene:    But that which fairest is but few behold,
    Her mind, adornd with vertues manifold."

<i>Page</i> 154, l. 1. "And fortune's fate not fearing."--Oliphant boldly reads, for the sake of the rhyme, "And <i>fickle fortune scorning</i>."--in "England's Helicon" the text is the same as in the song-book.

<i>Page</i> 158, l. 5. "And when she saw that I was in her danger."--<i>Within one's danger</i> = to be in a person's power or control.

L. 16. "White <i>Iope</i>."--Campion must have had in his mind a passage of Propertius (ii. 28);--

"Sunt apud infernos tot millia formosarum:
  Pulchra sit in superis, si licet, una locis.
Vobiscum est <i>Iope</i>, vobiscum candida Tyro,
  Vobiscum Europe, nec proba Pasiphae."

See Hertzberg's note on that passage.

<i>Page</i> 162. "While that the sun."--Also printed in "England's Helicon," 1600.
