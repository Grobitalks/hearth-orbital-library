The Man Who Was Two Men

By RAY CUMMINGS

Author of "The Snow Girl," "Princess of the Atom," etc.

_Alone in a strange city, a young man "awoke"--not
  knowing his name or his past! What could he do?
    How could he start life again--and where?_

[Transcriber's Note: This etext was produced
   from Argosy, February 8 and 15, 1930.]<b>CHAPTER I.</b>

WHO WAS HE?

He felt himself more frightened than he had ever been before--more frightened than he had ever imagined any one could be. He stood on a street corner watching the line of passing vehicles. It seemed to be night, or at least early evening. It was a wide street, with low houses and stores. A trolley went by, pushing its way through the turmoil of automobiles. It was an overhead trolley.

He stood at the curb, gripping an iron post which held a street light. The sidewalk was crowded; people jostled him. He turned and gripped a man.

"Listen--where am I?"

The man evidently thought him drunk. The man said:

"You're at Main Street and Devoir. What's the matter? You sick? Been hitting it up, buddy?"

He realized that he was visibly trembling. "No!" he stammered. "No. I'm all right. Much obliged."

The man looked him over and turned away. He decided that he had better move along. A crowd might collect. He didn't want that--more than anything, he didn't want to attract attention.A crowd collecting around him, looking at him, asking questions. That would be horrible. No one must ask him any questions!

He started off briskly along what the man indicated was Main Street. He thought he had never seen this place before. It was an American city. He was sure of that. A small city. And the time was early evening.

He passed a jeweler's window. At the curb was an iron pole with a huge clock face at the top. The clock indicated eight twenty.

"Eight twenty in the evening," he thought. But was the clock going? He looked in the jeweler's window. A chronometer was there--it said eight twenty-two.

He wandered on with fear striking at him; but he told himself that he was thinking perfectly coherently. He wondered if this were summer or winter. Why, it must be summer, of course. It was a warm evening. He saw that he was dressed in a light gray suit. He murmured vaguely to himself, "Why, this is a tweed mixture." He put his hand to his head. He had on a straw hat. He saw that most of the other men passing were wearing straw hats.

* * * * *

A newsboy came past. "Paper! Paper, sir?"

He felt in his pocket and found coins. He drew them out, looked at them. And muttered, "Why, of course! They're American coins.""How much?" he asked.

"Two cents," said the boy.

He handed over the coins and took the paper. His fingers were shaking. The boy dashed away.

He told himself again, "I mustn't stand here like this." He glanced at the paper. The date line read Tarlington, Connecticut, June 2nd, 1930. He tried to puzzle out whether that frightened him or not. June 2nd, 1930? It meant nothing. Tarlington? It meant nothing. Or did it? He couldn't decide.

He thought, "What's the matter with me?"

What frightened him was that at the moment he could not think of his name or anything else that any one not insane should know about himself!

He was wandering on again. He came to a department store which was closed, with unlighted show windows. He walked over and stood in the entryway just off the sidewalk. He would be a trifle more secluded here. He stood looking vacantly into the window.

He thought, "I mustn't get frightened. I'll be all right in a minute."

His hands were gripped tightly in his pockets. It gave him an idea. He began searching his clothes.He found himself going into a panic, but he struggled to control it.

He thought, "I mustn't let people see me doing this."

He found a few small bills, a pen-knife, some coins. He searched his other pockets. There seemed to be nothing that would give him any clew. He stood thinking.

"What'll I do? What'll I do?" His impulse was to dash out in the street, shouting, "I don't know what to do!" But he thought, "That's the worst thing. I've got to control it. I'll be all right in a minute."

He took stock of himself. He felt strong and well. He wasn't hungry; he had evidently eaten recently. He was dripping wet all over with a cold sweat, but he thought, "That's only nervousness. I'm frightened--frightened as hell."

He wasn't crazy. He was sure of that. His head was absolutely clear. He remembered every detail of everything that had happened to him in the past few minutes. He remembered standing on the corner asking the man where he was. But before that it was a blank. As though standing there in that instant he had awakened from a dreamless sleep. A sleep that had lasted all his previous life. As though now--just now--his life were beginning.

And then he thought, "Why, I know. This is amnesia."It seemed to steady him. Amnesia! He knew the word. He knew exactly what it meant. He had often read of people with amnesia--their memory of past events taken from them so that all in an instant everything that had gone before was blank. But that wasn't insanity. There was nothing dangerous in a condition like that. People often went on for years, perfectly normal.

It steadied him. He managed a sickly laugh. He murmured aloud, "This is an awful fix. What will I do?"

There was a policeman standing in the middle of the street at the next crossing. Had he better go to that policeman and have himself taken to a hospital? The idea somehow was revolting.

He thought again, "I won't do that. I'll try to get over this."

As he stood there facing the unlighted plate glass window, a dim reflection of himself was visible. He studied it. What he saw was a tall, slender young man of perhaps twenty-two, dressed in a light gray tweed suit and a straw hat; a thin, smooth-shaven face, white, with staring eyes. A sensitive, boyish-looking face; it would have been handsome, perhaps, without the fear on it.

He thought, vaguely, "Is that me? It must be!"

* * * * *It was frightening to stare at the unfamiliar face that was his; but it fascinated him. He stared. He smiled. The reflection in the window smiled back at him. The face of a stranger! Yet there was about it something familiar--the vaguest hint of memory telling him it was his face.

With the paper folded under his arm he turned from the window. A good brisk walk might help him. He walked a block. He passed the corner where the policeman stood. The policeman's side glance made him shiver. A new thought struck him. The officer seemed to be regarding him suspiciously, as though he were a criminal. Was he a criminal? What was he? Whatever idea he had had of giving himself up to the authorities to be questioned was gone with this last and terrible thought. What was he?

Then it seemed that another vague hint of memory was coming. He groped for it. Something menacing; sinister. A feeling swept him, something so elusive he could not define it. A menace, threatening him, indefinable as the terror of a nightmare. A menace hanging over him, somehow diabolical. He stood bathed in cold sweat, fighting it, as though this were a nightmare, and he was struggling to awaken. The feeling passed presently, leaving him weak and shaken.

The lights of a theater showed at the end of the block ahead of him. He read the large electric sign:

ZANETTI'S REVUEHe stood again by the curb with clenched fists, staring. Zanetti's Revue! It seemed as though somewhere in his memory the words stirred something. Familiar words. But were they? He moved on as though fascinated. The terror was gone. He felt now a surge of something pleasant.

He thought again, "Something will strike me as familiar--something that will bring everything back." He came to the front of the theater. The lobby was crowded with the arriving audience. At the curb automobiles were stopping, discharging their passengers. It seemed a fashionable audience. A line of ticket buyers wound from the box office in a queue across the lobby.

He stood gazing at the large photographs, depicting scenes from the show. His heart was pounding. The fear for a moment seemed to have left him. Curiosity had taken its place, a vague but eager curiosity. He seemed to be searching for something.

And then he saw what perhaps unconsciously he had been seeking. He gulped and stood gaping. For an instant all his thoughts failed him. He was conscious only of his hands clenched into tight fists in the side pockets of his jacket.

What he saw was a line of short-skirted girls, with two principals in front of them. It was evidently a dance scene, or perhaps a song. The girls in the chorus were on tiptoe, with bare arms upraised, and a leg in the air. In front of them were a young woman and a young man. A small girl with very thick, short black hair; a very low-necked bodice, tight at the waist, with very short flaring skirt.A black ruff was around her neck, wide black ribbons at her wrists. A little black and white Columbine. Petite, beautiful, alluring. Lips painted into a Cupid's bow, mockingly smiling. A vivacious little Columbine with upflung arms, poised on tiptoe.

Beside her was her young man dancing partner. A tall, slender fellow with wide, baggy trousers; on his face a make-up which widened his mouth and gave him a foolish grin. He stood with ankles bent--stood on the sides of his wide flat shoes, with dangling arms and drooping shoulders. He was grinning fatuously. A very comical sort of fellow, this partner of the fairy-like black and white Columbine.

He thought vacantly, as he stood there staring at the photograph. "Why, why, that's--"

It was the face that had stared back at him from the plate glass window a few moments before. It was his own face!

* * * * *

Again his memory vaguely stirred. That was his picture! Whatever his name, whatever all the circumstances of his past, he knew at least that he belonged here in the back of this theater. And the audience was already assembling!

He turned away from the photograph. He thought, "I must get around to the back, to the stage door. I wonder where it is?I'll try to find some one who knows me. Why, that's foolish! Of course they'll know me! There'll be a friend there. I'll tell him--tell him how frightened I am."

He became suddenly aware of a girl pushing her way toward him across the lobby. A small girl in a trim blue serge suit, with a little black and white toque on her black hair.

He stared. He had never seen her before. Or had he? Then he realized, "Why, that's the girl of the photograph, the Columbine."

She came running up to him. She gripped him by the arm. She said softly, "Why, Chuck! Chuck, dear!"

Chuck! Was it familiar? Yes, it seemed to be. What he might have answered he never knew. A girl had come into the lobby from one of the arriving automobiles. A tall, slender blonde in a long satin evening wrap. A girl of extreme beauty, with the aura of wealth upon her. She saw him. Her eyes widened and she cried impulsively:

"Leslie! Leslie!"

He felt the little dark-haired girl pulling at him. "Chuck! Chuck, darlin'! Come!"

The other girl called once more:"Leslie!"

They were attracting attention in the lobby. People were staring. The girl holding him repeated appealingly, "Chuck, darlin'! Come!" She pushed him. He was confused. It seemed suddenly that every eye in the lobby was upon him.

He thought, "Why, yes, that's who I am." He murmured, "For God's sake, let's get out of here."

"Yes, yes, Chuck."

Over by the box office two men had joined the blond girl. They started hastily forward.

He felt again that wave of terror. Something diabolical was coming to him from the blond girl--or from one of the men with her--a sense of something fiendish.

He gasped, "Get me away!"

The little dark-haired girl plucked at him, pulling him forcibly away. They escaped to the crowded sidewalk. The terror seemed receding. They darted around to the side of the theater, down the cross street, and into a dimly lighted alley. Fire escapes were here, with dressing rooms opening onto them. In the heat of the summer evening, girls in their gaudy costumes were sitting on the fire escapes.The man at the stage door passed them into the theater.

<b>CHAPTER II.</b>

BACK STAGE.

A man said, "Good evening, Mr. Daniels."

"Evening," he responded.

Mr. Daniels! Chuck Daniels! Was that his name? Why, of course! He kept repeating it as though again it might slip away.

Chuck Daniels! Chuck Daniels!

The back of the theater was a bustle of activity. Girls were crowding down an interior iron staircase. It was all a confusion of unreality.

He kept telling himself, "I mustn't forget; I'm Chuck Daniels."

There seemed a hundred or more of the girls, in tights and with spangles with long shimmering capes hanging down the back, and huge feathered headdresses. A few principals in costumes stood about.

There was scenery piled against the high blank brick wall which was the back of the theater;a discarded piano with its case opened to expose the hammers; a hundred odds and ends of props; coils of rope and electric cables. There were ropes hanging vertically down from the dim recesses overhead. Near by was the back of the set occupying the stage. Electricians were wheeling flood-lights that way huge mechanisms with cables dragging after them like slithering snakes.

The girls were crowding toward the back of the painted wings. From out front the strains of an orchestra sounded. A man in shirt sleeves, his hair matted on his forehead with sweat, was rushing about, issuing low-voiced commands.

A bustle of muffled but orderly activity. Dim and confusing in the sheen of the many colored lights.

He thought, "Am I part of this? What will I do?"

The girl was leading him through the crowd. As they mounted an iron staircase to an upper level of dressing room doors, he turned on her. He said suddenly, "I'm frightened. I want to talk to you."

Her hand held his arm. "Chuck, dear, you--you're sick again."

"No," he said.

The fear on her face struck at him. A wave of compassion and tenderness, some sort of emotion for her, swept over him."No," he said. "Don't look like that. I'm all right, but--but--I got to talk to you."

They stood at a dressing room door. A small paper tag was tacked to it. On the tag was written, "Chuck Daniels of Chuck and Elsie."

The girl whispered, "Oh, Chuck, don't you remember?"

"No, I don't! That's just it, I don't!"

"But you must, Chuck, you must! The show's startin'. Think--think hard! You're Chuck Daniels. I'm Elsie Kane. We're 'Chuck and Elsie.' We go on in a few minutes. You got to remember, Chuck, darlin'." She shook him, pleading, "Think hard, honey. You been all right for so long."

He stood dazed. "Yes, I know I have.... Have I?"

"Yes, of course, darlin'."

She was tense, hurried, and there was stark fear on her face. For a moment he almost forgot his own fear. This girl--she seemed to love him. Did she love him? Did he love her? He almost thought so. He clung to her.

She was whispering, "Chuck, if I only had a few minutes to talk to you, I could straighten it out. But we can't talk now, we haven't time. We go on at nine ten. You got to be ready. It's our big chance, Chuck.This is only a dog town, but you know they'll all be watching us. It's our big chance. Don't fail me now, darlin'! Don't!"

She shoved him into the little room. It was unoccupied. Was she going to follow him in? Was this their room? Or was she going to leave him alone--alone, with vacant confusion?

She said: "I'm right next door. If you call I can hear you, but you got to hurry."

She looked at her wrist-watch. It was five minutes to nine.

"You'll have time, Chuck. Hurry!"

But he still clung to her. "I don't know what to do," he pleaded.

* * * * *

She stood watching him, white-faced and helpless, as he sank down into a chair before the dressing table. The little overhead lights shone brightly on its triple mirrors. He saw his face there, full-faced and side-faced--rumpled, curly brown hair, staring blue eyes; frightened eyes. He fought with it; and then as he sat there with elbows on the table and hands ruffling his hair, a memory came. Two words; he seized upon them, gripped them.He turned to the girl: "Elsie, I remember! Elsie, listen; you know what we are? We're Adagio Hoofers, that's what we are! Adagio Hoofers!"

"Oh, Chuck!" She flung herself on the floor beside his chair, with her arms sprawled across his lap. "Chuck, you do remember!" She gazed up, and he saw that her eyes were filled with tears.

His hands went down to caress her dark, bobbed curls. "Sure, I remember. We're Adagio Hoofers. I'm the one who doped that phrase. I remember it!"

His mind flung back into that frightening emptiness of the past. But it wasn't quite empty now.

"You remember Ed Williams, Elsie? He liked that catch line when I told him."

Little light spots of memory in the dark void. Ed Williams, the press agent, with his black cigar. Williams had said, somewhere back there in the void: "Sure, that sounds fine. I'll feature it, Chuck. Listen, kid, you'll hoof your way onto the front page of every daily in the city."

That was Ed Williams. Chuck remembered him now clearly. And then there was a piano. A piano in a bedroom somewhere. He tried to grip the thought of the piano. He remembered being seated at it, playing. Yes, now he had it! A bedroom in New York. In his boarding house. He was writing a song, and now the name of the song came to him. No, not one song--two songs.He fondled the girl's dark curls. He had frightened her. He mustn't do that. Her eyes were overflowing with tears. She dashed them away with the back of her hand.

"Oh, Chuck--"

He said awkwardly: "Quit it, Elsie, I'm all right."

She reached up and gripped his arm. "You're sure?"

"Sure I am."

"Then what comes first? Think--think hard."

He thought: "The Hosiery Number." He said aloud: "Why, we're goin' out to dance to the Hosiery Number."

"Oh," she said. An infinite relief was in her tone and on her face. "Yes. An' then what?"

He thought intensely: "And then what?"

It came to him--they would change the scene for the Egyptian set, but he would still be wearing those incongruous, asinine trousers. And then he remembered his sweetheart song in the tenement scene."I'm all right, Elsie. Listen." He burst into the song, humming it at first, then singing it while she stared at him, and encouraged: "That's it, honey! Go on! You got it!"

"Why a girl like you should love a man like me is something I can't understand."

He went through the verse; he swung into the chorus:

"Just you, wonderful you."

"That's it, honey! Go on! You got it!"

He ended the chorus tenderly, dreamily:

"And so you are in love with just me."

She flung off his caressing hand and leaped to her feet.

"Honey, that's fine. Oh, Chuck, you're all right now! You can remember everything, can't you?"

"Yes," he said, "all about the show." He added, haltingly: "But this afternoon--what did I do--I mean, where was I?"She said swiftly: "We mustn't talk about that now--we haven't time. You ran away from me, Chuck. You were supposed to meet me at the restaurant."

He said: "Did I? I mean, did I run away from you?" He added: "I know where we are. This is Tarlington, Connecticut."

* * * * *

Other memories struck him. This little city some forty or fifty miles from New York. The show was opening here to-night. This was the first performance. Zanetti's Revue, trying itself up here on the dog, and then it would head back for Broadway.

He realized that he was thinking aloud. She interrupted him. "That's it, Chuck! You got it all now. We'll talk about it later."

As he stood up she clung to him again. She was so small beside him; her head barely came to his shoulder. He put an arm around her.

She said strangely: "Chuck, can you remember everything?"

It seemed as though something horribly important to her were hanging upon his answer.

He said: "I don't know what you mean, everything.""I mean, think back, Chuck. A few months ago."

A few months? Years? His past? It was all a wilderness of confusion.

She said, even more strangely: "This is summer. Can you remember last winter? I mean, what you did before last winter?"

Winter. He tried to remember what winter would be like. Why, that would mean snow, wouldn't it? He remembered vaguely that he had seen snow somewhere, some time.

He groped at her last words. "What you mean? What I did?"

She said: "This is Tarlington, Connecticut. We've never been here before, have we, Chuck?" She was smiling. A strange smile; she seemed to be pleading with him.

He said vaguely: "No."

There was a rap on the door. A boy's voice called: "Ten minutes! Ten minutes!"

"Right!" shouted Chuck.

Elsie dropped him. "My word, we're up against it. Hurry it, Chuck!"

"Sure I will."It seemed as though his words, his speech, even his mode of thought were changing. A normality coming to him. He recalled that wild surge of terror he had felt on the street. And felt again in the theater lobby. Those people calling him "Leslie." He was not afraid of that terror now.

He said with a grin: "Listen, kid, you make it snappy. If I can find those damned pants, I'll be ready in three minutes."

"They're hangin' over there, Chuck. There they are. We got your stuff out this afternoon, don't you remember?"

He saw his costumes hanging in the wardrobe. On the dressing table his make-up box was ready.

"Sure I do. Beat it, kid."

She dashed out and slammed the door. He heard her voice come back: "Fast as you can! Ye gods! If we hold up the show--"

"Don't worry," he called, and flung himself before the dressing table again. He put on his make-up with a swift, sure touch. This was more than memory; it was instinct. He told himself that he wasn't frightened now. He was thinking only one thing: "Ye gods! If we hold up the show--"

He was ready in a few minutes. He stood in the center of the floor. Adagio Hoofer. The young man of the photograph outside in the lobby.He stood balancing himself on the sides of his wide, flat shoes. His sailor pants flopped about his ankles. He hitched his belt up over his chest. He buttoned his shirt and pulled his necktie awry. He stood grinning with the foolish grin. He was ready.

He heard Elsie's voice: "Right, Chuck?" And the call-boy's voice: "Chuck and Elsie! Stage!"

He hurried out onto the iron balcony. The little black and white Columbine stood there.

"Gee, Chuck, you look great."

"So do you."

He put his arm around the dainty, fairy-like figure. The white powder, thick on her neck and arms, came off on his hand. He stooped suddenly and kissed her.

For an instant she clung to him. "Oh, Chuck, darlin'." Then she said: "Come on, come on, kid."

They hurried down the iron staircase to the back of the stage. A man shoved them toward the wings. He growled: "Say, you two, you had me scared!"

"Rats," said Elsie. "Listen, big boy, when Chuck and Elsie hold up a show, you'll have somethin' to write home about."They stood in the wings. The line of hosiery and garter girls were out front. The house was packed. From where they stood they could see the nearer boxes on the opposite side. Elsie whispered: "You nervous, honey?"

"No," he said. "We're goin' to knock 'em dead, Elsie."

The orchestra ended the chorus and swung into the eccentric dance. Chuck's mind was clear. Knock 'em dead, that's all he had to do. It would be easy.

She twitched him: "That's us. Here we go!" On tiptoe she floated away from him, out into the floodlight.

He stood the proper interval. He bent his ankles and got up on the sides of his shoes. He murmured: "Strut your stuff, Chuck." He adjusted his foolish grin. He waddled out onto the stage to join Elsie, with the hosiery girls behind them and the sea of upturned faces across the footlights in front.<b>CHAPTER III.</b>

THE GIRL IN THE BOX.

Chuck swung into his loose-jointed dance. He caught his inspiration from the laugh that went up from the audience at his appearance. Why, this was a cinch! They'd put it over big. The orchestra leader smiled at him as he went past. Elsie flitted like a little black and white bird around him. His own grotesqueness was a contrast to her fairy-like grace. He remembered how the dance director drummed that into him.

Memories were coming back to him fast. The dim auditorium, with its thousands of eyes upon him, was a blur, an unreality. The only things real were on the lighted stage. The hosiery girls had now vanished into the wings. He and Elsie were alone in the spotlight. Memories coming fast. He remembered the scores of rehearsals. The patient drumming of all this into him and Elsie, so that now all their movements were instinctive. He seized her. He whirled her over his head. He heard her murmur: "Great stuff, Chuck." He dropped her upon one tiptoe, and stood grinning as she flitted away from him.

The dance came to an end. He stood with Elsie, hidden in the wings, listening to the applause. The orchestra leader held his baton poised, ready for the encore. The applause was moderately hearty. He heard the claque of ushers at the back of the auditorium keeping it going.

"Let's go, Elsie," he whispered.The orchestra started the encore. He and Elsie went through it. They took their bows, running out, in again, then out again, while the claque sustained the applause. Four bows. The applause was thinning down. He came running to the wings with Elsie's slight figure poised on his shoulder.

She murmured: "Plenty, honey. Plenty. Let 'em alone. We knocked 'em dead."

They stood in the wings while the show went on. A man in shirt sleeves came up to them. He was vaguely familiar, but Chuck couldn't think of his name.

"Well," said Chuck, "we knocked 'em over, didn't we?"

"You'll get by," said the man, and wandered away.

As they stood there in the wings, diagonally across the theater, one of the stage boxes was visible. Chuck suddenly noticed that in it were the three people who had made the scene in the lobby outside. The tall blond girl and her two men companions.

And as he saw them, the terror swept Chuck again; but this time he fought rationally to control it. He stood tense, with clenched fists fighting it, trying to analyze it. An instinct of terror. A feeling that here was something diabolical. Gruesome. Something concerning him and the three people in the stage box.He searched them with his gaze. The terror came like an aura from them. From all three of them? He could not at first determine. The blond girl sat there now, gracefully resplendent in her low-cut evening gown. A girl a few years older than himself, perhaps; beautiful.

Surely there was nothing malignant about her!

The two men sat behind her. In the shadows of the draped loge Chuck could only make them out as dim, black-suited figures with white shirt fronts. One seemed a young fellow. The other was a man much older, a heavy-set figure. Momentarily, as he moved in his chair, the reflected glow of light from the stage caught his face: a heavy-jowled, smooth-shaved face, with a square, thick jaw; massive eyebrows; a shaggy mane of gray-black hair. An evil face. Diabolical.

The word stabbed at Chuck. <i>Diabolical.</i> And then another word. <i>Gruesome.</i> Words like stabs of light coming from the darkness of his past. Here was the terror. He knew it now. This man--

Yet it was so incongruous. Chuck gripped his cold, moist fists and tried to smile. The show went on. There seemed no place for terror here. This was comedy, gayety, laughter. Chuck fought the specter of terror back, and tried to keep his mind on the stage.

* * * * *The stage was a scene in a department store. The comedian--Chuck suddenly remembered his name, Sax Hotchkiss--was buying hosiery from the models who paraded before him and his fat wife. The wife was torn between desire to have the hosiery and to keep the models from her husband's eager, admiring gaze. The audience was roaring with laughter at his antics. It was a risqué scene, but funny, and here in the sticks it was going over big, no doubt of that.

"Great stuff," Elsie whispered, "they're eatin' it up."

Chuck murmured suddenly: "That girl who called me 'Leslie' is out front. See her there in the stage box?"

"Yes, I see her." The frightened look came to Elsie again. "Yes, I see her."

He said: "Who do you suppose she is? Why should--"

A roar of laughter from the audience drowned him. A thousand questions were flooding into his mind, but suddenly he felt a reticence. This girl, Elsie, for all their apparent intimacy, still seemed a stranger. His memory gave him now vague scenes of the past in which she figured, but it was all confused.

The audience was laughing again. The comedian on the stage was saying: "But listen, mamma, how can we buy what we don't see?"

Chuck drifted into his vague conflicting thoughts. This girl, Elsie--He seemed to feel that she knew more than she had told him. Hiding something?He suddenly recalled her words: "Do you remember what you did before last winter?"

The question seized on him now like something ominous. What had he done before last winter? His thoughts whirled away, groping. What was in his hidden past? Something criminal, perhaps. Was that it? Was that the terror? Then as though it might be an illuminating thought, he suddenly recalled his involuntary shudder when the policeman had looked his way back there in the street when he was groping with complete blankness. That policeman's look had frightened him. Why? Why should he be subconsciously afraid of a policeman? Was this girl Elsie trying to shield him? Had he done something criminal? He tried to laugh. What nonsense! But was that why she was frightened?

Elsie was whispering now: "I'm goin' to try an' find out who they are. You go back to your dressing room."

She was leading him from the wings. He thought suddenly: "She's always leading me. She leads me around as if I was a child. What's the matter with me anyway?"

His memory was coming back now so that he felt almost normal; but there were so many holes in it--so many things he knew he ought to know but did not!

"We've got half an hour, Chuck. The Nile scene is next. Better go to your room, Chuck. You got half an hour."They were at the back of the theater. He said suddenly: "Elsie--I've got to have a decent, quiet talk with you. I'm--you're playin' me for something! You're not on the level with me--or are you?"
