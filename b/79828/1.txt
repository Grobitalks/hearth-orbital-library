Chuck started. Went pale. The physician saw it, and gripped him by the arm. "That's just what I mean, boy! You're brave enough--my God, I can appreciate what you're fighting! And West knows it. They departed. Chuck and Elsie went through the stage door. It was just eight o'clock. The place was a hustle and bustle of confusion. The nervousness back stage was at once apparent. It permeated every one, from the suave, debonair Zanetti with his fiercely upturned mustache down through the principals and chorus girls to the lowliest call boy. The stage hands cursed unreasonably when a rope got tangled. To-night, of all nights, ropes were tangling frequently. The electricians cursed the lights. Out front the orchestra leader gazed over the musicians and wondered if they would go dumb and forget how to read music. To-night, of all nights!

The doorman said, "Good you're on time. Zanetti's raisin' hell. An' you missin' the rehearsal this afternoon."

"Not raising hell with us," said Elsie. "Man, he wouldn't dare speak to us. We ain't takin' nothin' from nobody to-night. Come on, Chuck, let's get upstairs."

They reached Chuck's dressing room.

"I wonder if they're out front, Elsie?"

"They?""The Wests."

"Forget 'em." She gazed at him anxiously, then turned and left the room. He donned his costume and put on his make-up. He grinned at himself in the mirror, but the grin was wrong. It didn't match his staring eyes. What was the surprise Dr. Adams had for him? No, he mustn't think of things like that. He was Chuck Daniels, afraid of nothing. But his father, maybe his mother also, and Gloria, would be out front.... No! Those were Leslie West's people!

* * * * *

Alone here in the dressing room, he was conscious of the thoughts and the fears which were the Leslie West in him. He was Leslie West! He thought, "God, I'm two people! It's horrible."

"Nervous, Chuck?" It was Elsie's voice from the room next door. "Don't be nervous, honey--we'll be the hit of the show." He heard her laugh--but it was a high-pitched, almost hysterical laugh.

At nine o'clock she was ready, and she came in. A little black and white Columbine. He thought he had never seen her look so alluring.

She eyed him. "You all right, Chuck? I mean, not nervous over the show?"

"No.""Then come on, let's go."

The hosiery girls were crowding back stage. Zanetti was striding up and down, furious at something; and then smiling, waving his fat hands.

The hosiery girls were herded off to the wings. They stood at the side where the stage was visible. Beyond it the left-hand box showed. The Wests were there! Elsie tried to stand in front of Chuck, but he saw them.

Gloria was resplendent, with calm, haughty dignity in the front of the box. The reflected light from the footlights gleamed on the satin-white smoothness of her neck and arms. Behind her, in a position not so prominent but still perfectly visible, sat Mrs. West. Leslie's mother. All Chuck's flooding memories told him it was so. Cold and haughty. Disdainful. Her gray-black hair rose in a wave from her forehead. Her ample bosom was wide with lace, and jeweled with a brooch and a rope of pearls.

As Chuck stood watching, she raised her lorgnette and surveyed the audience with a cold, haughty stare. Behind her and Gloria in the dim seclusion of the loge showed the white shirt fronts of Dr. West and Gloria's brother Leamington.

He was horribly nervous at first. His knees quaked as he danced on the sides of his shoes; but the nervousness presently passed. It was going big. Chuck and Elsie! Going big. He stood with Elsie, watching the stage. The comedian and his fat wife were buying hosiery. Chuck could see his mother here from where he stood in the wings. He could feel her shrinking at the coarse jokes.

"But listen, Momma, how can we buy what we don't see?"And that other, worse crack, "Momma, listen, I got to measure these stockings, don't I? You can't go by the marked sizes."

He could feel his mother shrinking at the coarseness of it. Putting herself through this to appeal to him. And Gloria was appealing, too. Could a man with any sense of decency and honor break his word to a girl whom he had promised to marry? She sat there so beautiful, cultured, refined. That was it: refined. So different from this tawdry tinsel of the stage. Was this all the decent side of life appealing to him; trying to win him from the coarseness, vulgarity?

He was very silent as he and Elsie went back upstairs. Elsie noticed the change. She was white and tense, but she said nothing. She left him, to change her costume for the Egyptian scene. She dashed the powder over him and laughed. And then something his father said last night echoed in his mind, "You're a rich man's son, Leslie. Don't get mixed up with a chorus girl."

He danced his loose-jointed, eccentric dance, Elsie writhed in a circle around him. Her slender white arms were like sinuous snakes. The music was exotic, sensuous. But cleverly mingled with a jazz rhythm. He seized her. He whirled her aloft. His hand was gripping her bare leg. The thought flashed to him, "Gloria is out there." Gloria shrinking. He strove to get hold of himself. At nine fifty he was ready for their last scene in the show: the Tenement Scene. He was dressed now as a poor but romantic young man of the tenements. His shirt, smeared with the dirt of honest toil, was open at the throat, exposing his bronzed neck. He was made up to look as handsome as he could. Elsie said that he was handsome.

He stared at himself in his dressing room mirror. He tried to appraise his face, strongly masculine but sensitive, almost poetical; romantic blue eyes, curly brown hair, brushed smoothly back. In this tenement scene would Gloria see him as the romantic young toiler, seated all day in the factory at his soulless machine, but with his sensitive soul dreaming his dreams, and now come home to his tenement singing his song of those dreams? * * * * *

He heard the applause ring out. This theater-full of people didn't think it was banal. It gripped them. They could feel what it meant to have a mother who was a dear old pal.

He was singing again, the second verse now. OUT OF THE PAST.

In Chuck's dressing room he sat with his arms around his mother. "Easy, moms! Don't cry! Here, Elsie, you take care of her. I got things to do, moms. Here's the girl who's goin' to be your new daughter. You sit with Elsie, an' listen, please don't cry. I got to talk to Dr. Adams--"

He turned to the physician and the detective. "You bet I'll give you the dope now."

With swift vehement words he poured it out. It was almost the brief history of his life. Not one life, but two! Chuck Daniels and Leslie West, strangely merged!

Chuck was born in the little town of Sea Cove, Delaware, an only child. His father was a famous vaudevillean. Charles "Happy" Daniels.

"An' he <i>was</i> happy," Chuck explained. "Always smilin', laughin' at life--wasn't he, moms? Gee, I remember him so well. An' then, right at the height of his success, he was paralyzed. I remember it. I was eight, ten years old maybe."

The dreams of further greatness for the stricken vaudevillean had gone into his son, Chuck. He idolized his son.Taught him music, stage dance steps, eccentric dancing--the crippled father sitting, singing and beating time while the boy danced. And he taught the boy to love and revere his mother.

Old Happy Daniels dreamed of the coming years when his boy would be a headliner. And then, when Chuck was about sixteen, his father died.

Dr. Adams put in: "That was about the time that Dr. West got hold of you?"

"Yes, sir. A month or so after father died. I was only a kid. Mother an' I, alone--"

Chuck's mother was sitting with Elsie, a frightened, confused little woman, dazed by all this. She murmured:

"I never knew where you went, Chuck, dear. I couldn't find you; I never knew how to try to find you."

"How did this West get you?" McGuire demanded. "An' what did he want of you? That's what we want to hear."

"I was sixteen. An overgrown, delicate boy. There was a picture of me with father, published in the Sea Cove newspaper. It was captioned, '<i>Happy Daniels's Son Training for Broadway</i>.' Dr. West saw it--he told me that's how he heard of me; an' he wanted me--"'Chuck faltered. He sat, groping. "Oh, I thought I had it all clear in my mind."

"You have, lad," the doctor urged.

"Yes, I have--all the Chuck Daniels part. The Leslie West in me is fading. I'm not afraid of him now. I thought I had it all. It's vivid as a dream, but the Leslie West part, like a dream, gets away from me. Dr. West was living in Royville, not far from Sea Cove. He saw that newspaper picture of me. I can remember clearly that I was out walking one night, not far from home--"

"The night you disappeared!" murmured his mother.

"Yes, moms. I was abducted. You know Sea Cove isn't much of a place. The road was dark--I guess the automobile had followed me from home. It drove up to me and stopped. Men seized me. I was taken to Dr. West's home in Royville. Dr. West confronted me. I'd never seen him before. He told me he was a doctor--a brain specialist. He said I was sick an' he would help me. He forcibly gave me a hypodermic. Up here, in my arm. His man Timmons held me. It was over in a minute. It was a drug, and it knocked me unconscious. An' when I came to myself, I was Leslie West."

In the silence of the dressing room the strains of music from the stage floated dimly up.

* * * * *The doctor said at last: "You were Leslie West? You'd forgotten Chuck Daniels?"

"Yes, it seems so. I was Leslie West. I couldn't remember anything at first. Then I remembered a lot of confused things of past years as Leslie West. My father told me--" Chuck started. "I don't mean that! It's the Leslie West still in me that made me say that! I mean Dr. West told me I was his son. He said I'd been sick, had had amnesia, which was why I couldn't remember things. I got better, but the past of Leslie West was always confused. It's all dim now--too dim for me to put it into words. Then, after about six months I guess I must have had a little glimmer of Chuck Daniels."

McGuire said tensely: "The drug was wearin' off?"

"Yes, I know now that's what it was. But Dr. West told me it was another amnesia attack coming on. And he said I had delusions; that the injections would cure me."

He told them of the next injection; and others, at intervals of six months, keeping the Chuck Daniels in him submerged. Meanwhile he was living his life as Leslie West. The family moved almost at once from Royville to Tarlington, Connecticut. Chuck grew up as Leslie West, a delicate, morbid youth, fearing, but respecting his father: loving Mrs. West as his mother, but very strangely. They saw that love for a mother--or at least the sense of obligation--was very strong in him. An instinct, not of Leslie West, but from Chuck Daniels. And they played upon it shamelessly."I understand it now," said Chuck. "She, Mrs. West, pretended she was delicate. Her 'heart was bad.' You had to do always what she wanted or her heart might kill her."

"What did she want?" McGuire demanded. "They're all in it together? All of 'em, framin' you for some game of their own?"

"Yes, I guess so. They wanted me to marry Gloria Bates. We met her in Tarlington when I was about nineteen. They picked her for me, but I didn't love her. I never wanted to marry her. She knew it--I'm sure she knew it, put like all the rest of 'em, she was forcin' me."

"Bunch of crooks," McGuire ejaculated. "That's clear enough. But what have they got to gain?"

Chuck said: "I don't know. That's what we'll have to find out."

The doctor prompted: "Six months ago, when it was time for one of the injections--"

"I quarreled with my--with Dr. West," Chuck finished. "It was about Gloria. They had the date for the wedding set. I told them all that I wouldn't go through with it. We quarreled. I must have run away, got to New York, met Elsie--"

"I think I can explain this part of it," Dr. Adams interrupted. "That drug put into you the personality of Leslie West.The memories were implanted vaguely--by suggestion, perhaps. Maybe otherwise. Such a thing has been done before. Legends of it permeate the history of medicine. But this is no time for a medical lecture.

"This thing is fiendish; certainly illegal. West is a criminal. We must bring him to justice."

"We will!" McGuire declared.

The doctor went on: "Substitution of personality! The personality of Leslie West was introduced, and Chuck Daniels was submerged. But every six months, as the influence of the injection began to wear off, the real Chuck Daniels tried to assert himself.

"A horrible strain upon the nervous system. And upon the mind. It brought on--at these six-month periods--a pseudo-amnesia, when the mind would give way under the strain, trying to adjust itself to the warring memories. An emotional disturbance, resulting in temporary blankness. But if, when this emotional disturbance came on, the drug were again injected, the mind was again temporarily relieved of its fight. Leslie West was again uppermost. A condition that would last another six months. Do you follow me, Chuck?"

"Yes, sir. I think so."

* * * * *"I want you to understand this thoroughly. When you do, the last danger of the Leslie West breaking out in you will be gone. A temporary blankness. The past stayed blank, save that you finally recalled your name Chuck Daniels, and had within you the subconscious influence of your real boyhood. You took to the stage. Your talent for it--your real father's training--came uppermost."

"An' his love for his real mother," Elsie put in. "He wrote his mother song, you know; you heard him sing it to-night."

The doctor nodded. "Yes. His subconscious longing for his mother. This brings us to yesterday. The show by chance took him to Tarlington. Familiar scenes. The influence of Leslie West was stimulated. The after-effects of the stoppage of any drug comes in periodic waves. That is well known. A drug addict may be cured--his drug entirely cut off--but his greatest suffering comes after several months, at definite periodic intervals.

"It was like that yesterday. Another time when the drug was due, had arrived. You went blank yesterday in Tarlington. It did not last long. The sight of Elsie--and of Dr. West and Gloria in the theater lobby--brought back your memories. Chuck Daniels and Leslie West were about equal within you, each struggling for mastery! The strains of the orchestra playing the finale were audible.

"The show's about over," said Chuck.

At the corner they hailed a swiftly passing taxi and crowded in. It was a short run up Broadway to Chuck's and Elsie's boarding house. The West limousine, for which they had been looking, had not been seen. As they turned into the cross street, McGuire said suddenly:

They mounted the stairs. Mrs. Peters was usually in bed at this hour, and most of the lodgers had not come in yet. The house was dim and silent. They came to the upper hall.

"This is my room, moms. Elsie, if she gets nervous or anything, you better sleep in this room. Yours opens on the fire escape. But this one is OK.You can lock yourselves in--but there's not a blessed thing to be nervous over."

Chuck's bedroom door was partly open--no one bothered to lock doors at Mrs. Peters's. They entered the bedroom, which was dimly illumined from the street outside its front windows.

THE ABDUCTION.

The thing took Chuck wholly by surprise. He jumped sidewise; but he was unarmed; and he saw his assailant's leveled weapon.

"Throw up your hands, Leslie!" It was Timmons. "Up with 'em! I mean it--I got orders to shoot this time. Quiet, you two--want me to kill him?"

Elsie and Mrs. Daniels were standing stricken with fright. They had cried out; but the bedroom windows were closed. The men in the taxi two stories down could not have heard them.

Chuck's hands went up. "I have no gun, Timmons; easy." He felt suddenly cold and calm; but his heart was pounding in his throat.

"Tell these women to get back." Timmons's free hand waved them.

"Over there, Elsie. Moms, don't get excited--it's all right--I know this man." Chuck could see better now in the dim room. He stood tense against the wall, hands over his head with Timmons's revolver leveled at him.

Timmons rasped, "Sure it's all right." He came forward; his left hand felt of Chuck's pockets. "We're going out. Leave the women here. You two, get me straight! You stand where you are.A sound out of you, even after we're gone, an' I'll shoot a chunk of lead into him. That mean anything?"

"Yes," Elsie gasped.

"Back up," Timmons commanded Chuck. "Open that door! Soft--you're goin' first. Take it slow--I'll tell you which way."

He backed Chuck into the hall and gestured. "In there."

They went into Elsie's bedroom. The window to the fire escape was up. Chuck understood it now. Timmons had come up this way, making sure that there were no lodgers on this upper floor.

"Out with you! Remember, you don't get away from me alive! One break an' yow're finished. Straight goods, Leslie."

He thrust Chuck down the fire escape in advance of him. It was dark out here; the fire escape descended to within some eight feet of the back yard.

"Now drop off. I swear if you make a break--"

Chuck did not dare take the chance. He hopped to the deserted yard. Timmons leaped down beside him. They crossed the yard, scaled a low fence into a tradesmen's alley. It led to another street. A limousine stood by the curb."In with you!"

The West car. Chuck gazed around the street. There were no pedestrians near enough to notice what was going on. Timmons, with the revolver pressed against Chuck, shoved him forward. The door of the car opened. Hands gripped Chuck, pulled him in.

"So I've got you back, Leslie?" It was West's ironical voice, but with a rasp to it that Chuck had never heard before. "Sit quiet! You think you're Chuck Daniels? Is that it, young fellow? Well, all in good time we'll fix that."

His weapon dug into Chuck. Timmons had slammed the door, climbed into the driver's seat; the car rolled away.

They turned south on Tenth Avenue. They gathered speed. There was no pursuit. It had all been swiftly done. Elsie, up there in the bedroom, would not dare call out for a few minutes. McGuire and Dr. Adams were still in the taxi, no doubt beginning to be impatient at Chuck's delay. But their suspicions were concentrated on the lurking flivver.

Chuck heard his captors discussing that car. West had believed that Chuck and Elsie would go home. The rest of West's party had gone to a New York hotel for the night.The house out at Tarlington was deserted.

The car with Chuck and his two assailants threaded its way north through the city, out into the country, speeding for the somber house in the outskirts of Tarlington.

<b>CHAPTER XII.</b>

THE THING IN THE HIDDEN ROOM.

In the ground-floor library of the West home Chuck faced his two captors. The big house was silent and menacing. It stood darkly brooding under the huge somber trees. Over it a full moon struggled with swiftly moving black clouds.

In the library the shadows lurked in the room corners. The shrouded French windows that gave onto the terrace were dimly visible. The safe against the interior wall, with book shelves beside it, was in shadow. The single light stood upon the table. Its circular yellow sheen fell upon Chuck's white face as he sat slouched in a chair where they had flung him. His coat was off; his shirt sleeves torn back to bare his arms. He sat close under the light.

In front of him Timmons stood with leveled weapon. The thick-set, hunched figure of Dr. West moved about the room, opened the safe.

There was no escape. Chuck knew it. These men, whatever their purpose, were desperate. Dr. West had gone too far now to retreat.Chuck sat silent; tense and alert. Dr. West was going to give him the injection. That much he knew. A few moments now, and he would go blank--unconscious, to awaken as Leslie West. Dr. Adams and McGuire would seek him out, of course. They would come out here.

Chuck's horrified mind pictured it. McGuire demanding Chuck Daniels. How easily West could confront him! There would be no Chuck Daniels! A docile, trembling young man who, Dr. West would say, looked like Chuck Daniels possibly, but was Leslie West.

There was no influence of Leslie West in Chuck now. He sat cold and tense, eying Timmons. What were these men after? His memory clutched for it. He could almost reach it, but not quite.

Dr. West's footsteps echoed in the silence of the library. The safe door grated as he opened it. He came back and stood before Chuck. His hypodermic and a phial of drug were in his hand.

"In a moment more, Leslie--"

Chuck burst out suddenly, "To hell with that Leslie stuff! I'm not your son; you know it! What do you want of me? What are you doing this to me for?"

"Timmons--""I won't move!" Chuck interrupted. "He needn't worry, I don't want to get a bullet in me."

Stalling for time. It flashed to Chuck--if he could hold them off long enough--or if, perhaps, he could manipulate so that West stood for a moment between him and Timmons; a sudden leap at West, Timmons would not dare fire, then a rush for the French window. With his shoulder he might plunge through it, chancing Timmons's following bullet--

But West kept to one side. He stood hunched; his hand holding the phial and the hypodermic. His heavy face was grim; his eyes gleaming. He said sardonically, "You don't understand? Why, I thought you did! Leslie West understands."

"Well, I don't. What have you got there?" It might bring West forward. But he did not move.

"A hypodermic. And your medicine." He added suddenly, "I don't mind telling you, Chuck Daniels. It's the last time Chuck Daniels will ever know anything!

"I made you my son, because I needed you in my plan. My son has to be married; and Gloria is a nice girl. I can't imagine why you're so stubborn that you won't marry."

Chuck managed a grim smile. "Am I supposed to understand that?"

If he could keep West talking! Anything but to have this drug forced upon him.West said abruptly, "Perhaps some of this talk will stay with you and go into Leslie West. I hope so. I'm not going to fool with you to-morrow. You're going to marry Gloria, you understand? And give her and me the management of your affairs."

"Oh, am I?" It stirred a vague memory. "Why?"

"Because I tell you to. Your grandfather's money--that accursed will."

* * * * *

As though the words had tapped a hidden well of memories, the knowledge of his purpose came to Chuck. It rushed out of the darkness of the past. The will! Leslie West's grandfather had loved the boy. He had left all his estate with a reputable trust company, to be held until Leslie was of age. Dr. West had been allowed the interest, to be expended for Leslie's upbringing. There was insanity in the West family. The grandfather had feared it for Leslie. If, at twenty-one, Leslie was mentally competent--and if he married--he and his wife were to have the principal. If he died, or was not mentally competent, the whole vast estate would go to endow a well-known institution for the insane.

Comprehension was on Chuck's face. Dr. West saw it. "So you do understand? I thought you would. A stake worth playing for, isn't it? You think we're going to let nearly eight million dollars get away from us?"He turned toward the door. "Watch him, Timmons, a minute or two. I'll be right back."

Chuck gasped, "Where are you going?"

"Sit quiet." West went through the library doorway. His heavy footsteps echoed from the dark hall outside.

Timmons murmured, "If you move now--"

A sudden rush of stupefying horror was surging over Chuck.

The last memory barrier was broken. Dr. West had gone to that little door hidden under the angle of the staircase. A secret panel, opening into a room hidden there.

The lurking thing would come out--Dr. West would bring it back, take those few drops of its blood and mix them with his strange drug, and inject it into the veins of Chuck Daniels.

A thin voice sounded through the silent house, an eerie, treble cry. Chuck stirred in his chair, and Timmons rasped:

"Easy! Damn you, sit still."Dr. West's heavy footsteps were returning, mingled with the stumbling sound of other footsteps.

Chuck gazed numbly. Leslie West! The real Leslie West! He came stumbling into the library ahead of his father. A young man in trousers and shirt open at the throat. Chuck's thoughts were whirling with memories of other times like this. The real Leslie West! This defective, this imbecile! Dr. West's voice broke the silence. He gestured to his son."Stand over there, Leslie. You silly fool, I won't hurt you! When we get through with this, I've got candy for you, sonny."
