A brooch of blood red Mexican opal set with emeralds was at her neck, catching the lapels of a frilled white collar which rose upon her neck, while around her waist a quaint armorial belt, fastened with an old and lustrous Chinese buckle, holding a purple fluorite and a green jadeite, added a note of exotic culture. She was radiant, and as the young man rose to his feet his face brightened into an appreciation which spoke almost too plainly his own admiration. Julieta was with her daughter, and her matronly outline and the dark color of her garb was a foil to the latter’s interesting loveliness.

“Your Honor, my Mother,” was the salutation of Miss Lorimer, who seemed eager to begin the serious work before them. The gentleman bowed and expressed his pleasure, and then as Mrs. Lorimer spoke of her daughter’s mission in connection with the new trial of the prisoners seized at the time of the destruction of the Settlement House, Helen Lorimer herself pushed a table forward, drew three chairs up to it and rang a bell.

Answering the summons a servant appeared with a number of portfolios, and almost at the same moment the parlor doors swung apart, and Mr. Theodore Lorimer accompanied by a broad featured gentleman entered. Introductions followed, and the Mayor learned that Mr. Lorimer’s companion was the clever and industrious lawyer who had discovered a mass of new evidence, and was willing to stake his reputation upon his complete ability to prove that five of the men under sentence, had nothing to do with the disastrous explosion and fire, leaving the guilt weighing heavily on the sixth, Polinck, Longtinsky’s confidant, and with a preponderant disposition of the facts pointing to the central and animating spirit of mischief as residing in Longtinsky himself.Mr. Moser was a short man with a relentless intimation of power in his expression. Somehow it rested in his broad shoulders, clean shaven and pugnacious jaw, his small deep set and musing eyes, the crisply moving thin fingers and particularly in his thin lips that closed against each other in a vise-like way that suggested an interior mental grasp as tight as this physical analogue.

When the portfolios had been opened and the entire party was seated at the table, Mr. Moser rose up, and developed his position. It was admirably done. It was clear; it seemed unmistakably certain that Mr. Moser could prove the points which came out with bristling alacrity. He made them in a matter-of-fact way, quite devoid of parade, and in a conversational undertone, with a relieving gesture, and now and then, especially aiming them at the Mayor, a convincing puckering of his tense eyebrows:

“Longtinsky has a serpent’s tongue and the brain of a demon. The men about him are his dupes. He plays them as you might play cards. He holds them in his hand and no scruples interfere with his using them for his own profit and their ruin. And yet he is a clumsy contriver. The simple confound the wise. A child steps in, and his fabric of deceit tumbles into pieces. It is a play-loving child, with a dash of curiosity, just a trace of the sleuth strangely developing—a fine prophetic instance of tendency Sir. This child drawn by some peculiarity in Longtinsky’s looks follows him; shows an old age interest in the man. And why? Because Longtinsky gave him a picture book. This inflamed the child’s imagination. It exerted some hypnotic influence. By a queer coincidence the book contained an illustrated story of a Russian nihilist, and the boy identified him with Longtinsky.He follows him, shadows him, if you will, a very mania of pictorial identification seized him. In Longtinsky he saw the hero of his story. In every incident he caught a reflexion of the episodes of his tale. Longtinsky either mistook his devotion or it was unobserved, or the tender years of this boy deceived him. The boy hung round his printing office; he was amused by Longtinsky who gave him blocks and toy type to play with. Longtinsky may have dreamed that the child would grow up into a keen edged accomplice in his games. At any rate he took little notice of the boy’s incessant observation, and he never suspected his sensitive memory.

“Every impression remained there, and they remained marvellously distinct. There are no shadows among them. The child’s mind is a photographic plate. The child was also attracted by Miss Lorimer. He was one of the visitors at the Settlement House, and made good use of its advantages. But it was a different sort of attraction. He had absorbed Longtinsky’s hatred for this excellent benefactor and mixing up the impressions of his hero in the Russian story he made out for himself that Longtinsky was the victim of persecution directed by Miss Lorimer.

“This boy was at the very centre of the operations that turned the Settlement House into ashes. He saw the bombs taken from Longtinsky’s office of The Beehive at night to the Settlement House. It was a night when the House was watched, the Janitor was away: there was collusion somewhere. Four men brought those implements of destruction there. TWO OF THEM WERE LONGTINSKY AND POLINCK.This boy although obsessed by an incredible devotion to Longtinsky never revealed his feeling but like some parasitic thread of life clung to him, slipped on behind him unknown, stood in the shadow of the doorways, slunk from retreat to retreat along the streets. He too was vitally active as if driven by a flame of madness. He saw everything and, what was more astonishing, he understood. He lived through it all, the life of his hero in the Russian tale. He slunk among the conspirators as their unseen or unnoticed Nemesis. His parched and contracted nature became monomaniacal with a detective precocity. His mind like wax took impressions that were retained as if in steel.

“This strange object I met—after Miss Lorimer had engaged me to look into this case—I saw him at the ruins of the Settlement House, and something—as he stood on the brink of the smoking excavation—piqued my curiosity—not averse Sir, to finding evidence in the smallest scraps—and I spoke to him. I found out that he had been in the House a few minutes before the explosion, that he knew Longtinsky. His warped precocity of observation had not changed a natural childlike simplicity for he was easily led on into the startling admission that showed that Longtinsky and Polinck had placed the bombs in the House themselves, had afterwards taken into the House the men who have been accused and convicted of the crime at the moment when the bombs were lit. This dodging boy had followed Longtinsky and Polinck in, when they placed the fuses. Afterwards Longtinsky escaped to his home, and left it to Polinck to inveigle and desert the victims of their mutual treachery. Polinck would have gotten away too except for the time lost in making the oil and rag fires that consumed the place.The boy has no parents but lived with some family who pitied his lonely condition and he wandered about the streets almost unobserved and uncared for. His peculiar temperament developed into a queer intensity of moroseness wherein the seeds of his unnatural attachment to Longtinsky grew and flourished.

“After the catastrophe this boy seemed stunned, and I met him at a psychological moment, when he needed a confessor. He craved the opportunity to tell all he knew and by some reversion as strange as his former hallucination, he now believes it necessary to do something for the men whom he saw taken by the police. Some of these men have protested their innocence of the exact crime, but the circumstances of their apparent participation were too strong against them. This boy your Honor will exculpate them, and I believe that he will keep the rigid immobile steadiness of his story under the fiercest cross-examination. He is of course uncorroborated by others, and it remains to be seen how his narrative will effect a jury. On him Sir, we pin with certainty our conviction that the men now under judgment of death will be freed.”

“Where is this boy?” said the Mayor.

Miss Lorimer rose and in a few moments returned with the singular being, whose abnormal and subtle watchfulness had put so complete a reversal upon the course of events with the suspects.

As the boy came up to the table the Mayor rose, and his example was followed by all present. The boy stood in the centre of a wondering group.He was a creature likely to attract attention among those accustomed to interest themselves in a face as the symbol and expression of the nature beneath it. Extremely thin, with a stunted effect produced by the drooping of one shoulder, and his cross-eyed vision, his arms would excite wonder at once. They were so long. His hands were flat and bony, and by some sort of interior nervousness they seemed to be constantly in motion, twitching or seizing his clothes as if worried by their fit or arrangement. His face would have attracted attention any where, but it would also have been the attention of sympathy and commiseration. It was at every point almost disfigured and misshapen. The eyes were not on a level and the ears were curiously small and shrivelled, his forehead, as in hydrocephalics, was swollen and protuberant, his nose was a sharp ridge and his cheeks were sunken. Only his mouth bore the traces of an infantile charm, and it was perfectly formed and opened over perfect teeth. This latter feature conjoined with the deep clustering and raven darkness—lustrous too as a raven’s wing—of his abundant hair, redeemed his physiognomy and imparted a singular elfin weirdness to it, at least it raised the baffling surmise that his face was made in two parts, a worser and a better half, and their discord reflected some temperamental confusion, some alternating balance of moral or mental tendency. His eyes seemed to dread the light, and he kept them cast down, which further imparted a dead and expressionless vacancy to the rather blighted and uninteresting face.This blankness remained only when his eyes were averted. Raised and opened, they became partially corrected in their alignment, by the frequent flashes of excitement they betrayed, and then intelligence entering his expression, his vexed and ill treated features gained something in orderliness, or the more repugnant hid themselves, and were forgotten, under this exaltation. He was short for his years, and pale, with flushes of color coming at the moments of excitement.

He was a small boy with excellent proportions of body other than in the simian arms and the defect of the drooping shoulder. In him again Miss Lorimer had turned the tables upon her antagonist. Angelo Shimura was an idolator. He craved objects of worship; they remained with him in his waking moments as supernatural incentives to his devotion. The subtlety and strength of his attachment had been tested with Longtinsky, as a mere objective personality, upon which he must lavish his imaginative attributes. But in reality his heart had not been warmed, and in that heart lay the unappeasable fires of the most slavish self-sacrifice.

The shock of the terrific incident which he had watched in its growth into a tragic issue had perhaps shattered that first extreme and provisional adherence; and now, brought under the power of a lofty nature, and fed with the nutriment of sympathy, his little passionate soul became steeped in love for his benefactress. In that company, whenever he raised his eyes, they sought the face of Miss Lorimer, as a dumb wistful yearning animal follows with unquestioning faithfulness and service its master’s. Its pathos touched the young Mayor, and his own heart followed the boy’s to the same goal.When Mr. Moser had finished his short story after adding a few other more emphatic affirmations of his complete confidence to bring about a reversal of the conviction of five of the six men—Polinck always should be excepted—the company again took their places around the table, with the boy sitting alongside of Miss Lorimer and catching in a queer studious way the drift of the dialogue though his attention was more shown by the movement of his head than by the glances of his eyes.

The Mayor had become thoroughly aroused and felt deeply his indebtedness to Miss Lorimer for her interference. It had probably saved him and the community from a serious weight of remorse and opprobrium. Already the stay of the execution had been ordered by the Mayor, and the further steps to secure a new order for a trial were agreed upon. The case could not be now considered before the fall, and in the meanwhile it was hoped that the Mayor’s action would not meet censure. The diagrams and drawings in the portfolios were inspected. Many of them showed the stages in the construction of the old Settlement House, one was a photograph of the fire, another was of the prisoners and a third, of the most significant interest in their present hunt, was a chance photograph of Pitt Street, made by some wandering tourist engaged in a dilettante search for reminders of “the slums,” in which appeared two figures recognizable as Longtinsky and Polinck standing upon the opposite side of the street, and looking upward to the walls of the Settlement House, while far from them crouching in a doorway was the odd figure of the haunting Angelo Shimura.The Mayor was surprised to see among these sketches, a series of perfected drawings for a new Settlement House, larger and more ornate than its predecessor and designed to comprise a Museum. On this point Miss Lorimer became eloquent. Her animation was intense and the charmed young man listened to her with delight. The nobility of her design heightened the fires burning brightly in his own heart, of sympathy with the “submerged,” and those to whom the chances of recreation and distraction are denied, the strangely forgotten units of life, who somehow must be masoned into the structure we call “The Nation,” and from whom, in the lottery of Fortune’s choice, great destinies may arise, or more practically, immense acquisitions of sane sentiment be won.

“The large museums” said Miss Lorimer, “are a long way off from the people I intend to help in this way. A museum, like a library, should be constantly used. Repetition tells, and memory makes a permanent record when it is deeply graven by iteration. Then it seems to me no museum, as I find them, does the work that can be done for these people in the most helpful way. Everything is remote and difficult in its presentation, or else simply pictorial. And my museum will be composite, and it will be national. I mean that there will be history and art and science in it, but all these will be concerned with America and its interests and objects.”

“I don’t imagine,” said the Mayor laughing, “that you intend to nationalize astronomy, for instance. We can hardly pretend to any monopoly of the stars.”

“You must not ridicule my project, Sir. Let me show you what I mean, in a way at least. Come and see my own museum. I have one in this house.”“Indeed may I see it?” said the Mayor eagerly, “I may not know what you mean to do, but I can understand what you have done.”

“Yes, that would please me,” Miss Lorimer answered, as she rose and explained to the company her intention of giving the Mayor an object lesson in her “improvised museum at the top of the house.” She invited the others to accompany them, but Mr. Moser and Mr. Theodore Lorimer had a discussion under way, which they could not afford to interrupt, and Julieta excused herself. Thus the Mayor and Miss Lorimer went together through rooms and corridors, up staircases where niches rose imposingly on either side, adorned with sculptures, and at last entered the high attic, into a broad and long room set around with cases, occupied with tables, and somewhat divided into alcoves, while the windows were breaches in the walls, opening out upon the topmost balcony, which overlooked the trees, and now at night commanded a far-spread network of twinkling lights.

The place was singularly pleasant, and the feeling of physical elevation, as now and then the eye caught a moving barge of lights upon the Hudson or the halloo of shouts rose from the velvety darkness beneath, seemed grateful. The Mayor noticed that the objects promiscuously heaped around and in almost unrestrained confusion were for the most part pictures, prints, etchings, woodcuts, framed and loose, with pottery, porcelains, statuettes, and furniture. There were few of the usual accessories of the Natural History museum; a few shells in a case, a specimen, of large size, of quartz or feldspar or calcite, some gorgeous bird, a stuffed small animal, and a rather amusing collection of fossils. The Mayor had some scientific proclivities and a well developed appreciation of the objects of nature.Miss Lorimer observed his surprise. “No,” she said, a slight blush mounting in her cheeks “I have not been very anxious for the regulation display of a scientific museum, and that is not quite what I intend to make mine in the Settlement House. I must confess art and history appeal so much more to me. It would be very foolish for anyone to complain of Science but—Well, I will be frank and disparage myself in your eyes—I <i>do</i> not love Science. IT TRIES TO EXPLAIN TOO MUCH. I can’t help but think of it,” Miss Lorimer was laughing now, “as a near sighted and precise middle aged gentleman, who sees everything through his glasses which must be brought at short range with the things he is looking at. They are very clear to his eye, but they have no atmosphere, and the poetic—might I say—the religious lights, and the broad vistas escape him altogether. Labels and names, nomenclature and theories make up his life. He stumbles a good deal because he does not see very far beyond his nose, and yet I will say his intentions are excellent, and he does much good, but because he is near sighted he breaks beautiful vases filled with flowers, and puts his defacing hand on many a fresh picture.”

“Well,” retorted the Mayor, “you must admit that science has made our everyday life more healthful and convenient; that it has taught us a great deal, and influenced us to think more of just those unfortunates whom you desire to help. Would you really care to see science less powerful in this wonderful modern world?”“Why, no,” answered the lady as the two sat down upon a bench placed in the recess of a window, “I think of science as a handmaid to the arts of life, as a purveyor of the necessities, making us comfortable and keeping us healthful. It is quite irreproachable but, can you love it?”

The Mayor’s pale face flushed, and the beautiful look returned to it which had for an instant enthralled Helen Lorimer when she met him in the City Hall. She looked at him intently. He rose as if confused or pained, and walked a few steps away. A fine Barbazon bronze stood upon a table at his elbow, and he answered, looking at this, as if finding his text somewhere in its graven lines.

“Science <i>does</i> keep us sane and real. Romanticism, the sort the Germans encouraged two centuries ago, can hardly live in the world of science. As to loving it, those who follow science seem to surrender themselves to it with a feeling as deep, or deeper, than even Love would ask of men. It possesses them like an inspiring demon. Perhaps that abnegation of all interests to the one great call of Wisdom is only realized in the more profound and zealous, but it is there, <i>with them</i>. Why! to add one little scrap to the pages of knowledge, just to put there a new unobserved fact, imparts a curious elation. But as to Love—Ah! well love is something different.”

“Yes,” Miss Lorimer’s voice was low, as if she too had surrendered to a train of interior reflections, “love is more human than Science. It cherishes old faiths, old things, its rays are not the concentrated radiance from patent reflectors, but the old sweet sort of light that you see in a woman’s eyes.”“Why not a man’s, also?” softly whispered the Mayor, who left his absorbed contemplation of the bronze, and stood near Miss Lorimer.

“Well—” she answered, “I suppose so too, but—it is a concession which must not be made too much of. There is good authority for believing women monopolize that kind of illumination. Do you recall Shakespeare’s adage?”

“Yes, but” retorted the young man “the same poet has put in the mouths of men the deepest and the truest things about love.”

“Of course,” laughed his companion, “men are great soothsayers. I think a very wise jester in Shakespeare averred that.

“Let me show you my meaning, the hope I have of putting in the new House, over there on the east side, something more useful to the people than labelled insects and shells and fossils. I want to have a series of rooms each one of which will be a page of history as it were, that is, a section of historic time, and, as the visitor passes from one to the next, he will feel the changing atmosphere of the rolling ages. It will be the paleontology of culture. Instead of fossils arranged to show the evolution of animals you shall here see the evolution of the human idea of living.”

“It’s a grand thought, but perhaps it is larger than the space you have to put it in,” said the Mayor.“I think, Sir,” and the speaker moved closer to him and looked almost inquiringly into his face, “you intend to jest with me. But I will have your submission. Let me show you how well even in a small way this all can be done. I intend to do it with pictures, a few small replicas of buildings, and some figures, with just enough archeology to make the pictures and the figures real. Be patient with my good intentions and remember I have chosen you for my ally.”

“There are some reserved rights of criticism for an ally,” said the Mayor, taking up the border of a tapestry which hung near them. “But I should be, I fear, a poor critic. My allegiance is too unquestioning.”

“Well you shall have privileges. In my scheme there are aspects of civics to be considered. That will be your affair.”

“Perhaps,” the Mayor whispered in serio-comic tone, “We ought really to consult Briggs?”

Helen laughed outright; “OH! that Cerberus. No, indeed. He would discover some means to ruin all my plans. He is implacable. He will not forgive me his discomfiture at the Hall. He sees in me the embodiment of a rabid useless philanthropy which is about as bad in his eyes as the red Jacobinism he fears in the <i>sans culottes</i>. But you approve of me don’t you?”

The speaker turned upon the young man the appealing beauty of her face and the gesture was too significant not to escape the jealous attention of her hearer, who never felt so happy.“Miss Lorimer, my admiration of what you are attempting is a good deal deeper than approval. I think I am not careless in using words, when I say that I love you for what you are doing and have done.”

A red tide of color flushed Helen Lorimer’s face, her eyes sought the floor, and the Mayor looked at her with a wondering smile, which was an excellent index of his feeling that his words were not resented. There was a short silence and then Miss Lorimer rose, and moved towards the opposite end of the long room, while she half turned and invited the Mayor to follow.

“I have indeed made a step forward in my plan. Come and see the Egyptian phase. I call these phases of civilization—chapters. It is the great book of life I wish to show these people, as if the human mind advanced in cycles of development, and each of these cycles was a chapter in a continuous history. There will be rooms, each room a chapter, each chapter successional to the others that precede it. But that is not all—not all—I have so much to show you.”

They stopped a moment at the door of a room, which when opened, revealed a half arranged assortment of maps, pictures, models, apparel, with a number of stereoscopic cameras, and relievos, all illustrating scenes, customs, and the ruins of Egypt. There were here great portfolios filled with drawings, photographs, and colored sketches. A number of well made relief maps showed the configuration of the ancient country, while small but exquisitely executed restorations of temples, propylons, sphinxes, obelisks, occupied a table in the middle of the apartment. There was the usual miscellaneous assortment of scarabaei, glass vessels, vases, mortuary images, ises, and one sarcophagus.Miss Lorimer viewed them all with pride, though she was silent for an instant as if estimating the forlorn confusion among these crowded reminders of the past; but her embarrassment quickly vanished.

“I shall try,” she said, contemplating with a little perplexity the disarray of objects, “with these types, and with books and labels—yes with poetry and epigrams—to set before the people a picture, a restoration of Old Egypt. If they can be made to realize its awful distance from our modern variegation, its monolithic cryptic strangeness, if they can feel the breath of its exaggerated egotism, and the weird pantheistic aspirations of its religions, or catch in an imaginative picture its stolid immensity of architectural violence, will that not be an education? Suppose I can draw them along from room or hall to other rooms and halls, each one the concentration of the idea of a peculiar culture, as the Greeks, the Romans, the Mediaeval, the Mahommetan, the Indian and, expanding as they move upward to our modern day, into treatment of nations or races, as cultural units what a breadth of well defined impressions I can make!

“I can invite them into beautiful avenues of thought, recreating by the spell of portraiture and language these obliterated complexes of ideation; the Egyptian aridity and monumental titanism, the art and limpidity of Greece, the strength and legal emphasis of Rome, and then may I not attempt somehow to show the warming power of Christianity by which our hearts become susceptible to all beauty, for Christianity Sir, unlocked the treasury of Sympathies we possess with all life.”There was something very lovely in Helen Lorimer when her enthusiasm—always easily evoked—kindled, and the young Mayor thought nothing in the form of lovely woman could be more captivating. He was less attentive to her plans as he watched her, than he was thoughtfully wondering with himself how impossible it was for him to control his own feelings, and then again how far away from him seemed the radiant gifted and noble woman whose voice rose to him with a penetrating sweetness, and whose eyes met his with challenging sincerity. Curiously he found himself retracing the steps of his own life, so that while he listened, while he watched—and watching loved—some hidden operative power of thought reviewed the toiling days he had gone through, and his divided attention, like a rider on two horses, noticed Miss Lorimer’s ideals—probably a little vaguely—and his own struggles amid deprivations and against obstacles—perhaps a little acutely.

He saw himself the child of humble parents—his father being a bookkeeper and his mother a lady of simple birth—who had grown rapidly and was devoured by a passion for place. At school he fought with his schoolmates for the best position in the school base-ball team, in the class room he engaged every power he possessed of self-control to seize and hold the first place. He admitted this superiority came easily, his lessons were quickly and well learned, and his physical skill, the celerity of his movement, were ungrudgingly acknowledged by less successful rivals. His disposition—he was not embarrassed in making the confession—was genial and loving, with traces of irritability, and though his worsted opponents might regret their failures, somehow—if the individual was not too cross grained and unforgiving—he could coax them back into good humor and even make his own victory serviceable to their needs.But he must be on top; the rage for conquest burned fiercely in his boyish heart, and this ambition kept him restlessly aggressive. He bounded through the grammar school, he leaped over the grades in the High School, as if, figuratively, he was an acrobat bursting paper rings. Then he flashed through the City College, and his coruscating transit remained a vivid bewilderment to the faculty and his class mates. But he was always poor. He came home after the day’s exciting experiences to a very simple home life, wherein the delicate and sweet figure of his patiently toiling Mother, like a consecration, deepened his love, and brought feverish resolves in his mind to lift her, his father, and himself into affluence.

He had not indeed gained riches, but he had won <i>place</i>, the highest in the city-state, and before him opened untrodden vistas of ascending power, social and financial. After graduating from college he studied law. His wit and energy, the tireless practice of self imposed tasks, his utter absorption in the thing at hand, bore the customary fruit. He won out easily, and before a bench of jurists delivered an oration—argumentative, speculative, and didactic—on the claims of socialism that almost brought that deliberative body to its feet, and for an instant endangered its dignity by an irruption of hand-clapping. His subsequent course had been persistently upward. Prestige, reputation, employment were naturally gained. And finally as he wished it, the offer of the nomination for governor was tendered to him, and art and opportunity combined to seat him in the governor’s chair.He had done well, the rewards were not undeservingly allotted; no man exerted more relentlessly his ability to work, or aimed higher in the standards of excellence his ambition proposed to his skill. Yet—the opposition is unusual—with this incessant application for success, under the good of his own unappeasable thirst for place, he displayed a most irrelevant modesty. It was an almost unsupportable personal enigma. A man ruthlessly overcoming opposition, battling like a maniac for position, and yet at the surcease of effort declining into self-effacement. It seemed to his friends a neurological accident; it was nervous exhaustion and they looked upon it as a dangerous symptom of a physical decay.

As the Mayor thus mused, still retaining in his ears and mind the lavish picture that Miss Lorimer was drawing, he felt again the challenge to a new sort of conquest. Could he capture the heart, the hand, of this famous woman, the soul of an aristocratic coterie—a Hypatia indeed, upon whose shoulders dwelt the prestige of wealth and talent, and yet one mingling her daily life with the currents of the people’s lives in the ministrations of mercy, of education.

How amazingly acceptable to him—a man eager to disenthrall the commonality from the sordid commonplaces of their lives, and bring to them the assets of an intellectual endowment—was this condescension. And yet he secretly but acutely augured that he and Miss Lorimer were separated by a deep moral hostility. Helen Lorimer dwelt wholly within the borders of a severe and ecstatic religious faith, and he—Ah! how should he reconcile to her his repudiation of all creed, the complete erasure from his heart of all faith and the bleak hardihood of his reiterated negations before the fabric and the vouchers of the Church?The Mayor was enrolled among those who accept the scientific conduct of life, who look along the years ahead of them as periods of duration to be used discreetly, but who spurned the illusion of any forbearance from above, any posture of agreement with a God, any dream of perpetuation beyond the pit and doom of death. Perhaps he felt the authority and corrective uses of the Church, but like Napoleon who saw in the Concordat a political compact and nothing more, he appreciated its invincible excellence as a police guarantee for ORDER.

He could not have said with Napoleon, “I do not believe in religions, <i>but in the idea of a God</i>;” he believed in neither. To the world about him, to the men of daily intercourse he was reticent. He respected the rights of conviction, and he knew their value. But with this new aspect of life delightfully developing before him, with the ingratiating companionship of a woman’s heart and soul, leading him into untraversed joys—and such a woman—could there safely be long concealment? He saw how immensely earnest she was, that perhaps she in a measure lived for invisible realities; should he hesitate to be frank?

Well at any rate there was now no need for brutal, disconcerting, confessions; let Love have her perfect way at first, and let reconcilement follow later. His present happiness was too great to be disturbed by shocks of disagreement. He was quite ready to fight hard for the splendid prize, and strategy—though he despised mean motives—was a legitimate resource.There was a short silence which might have left an unpleasant impression upon the lady had it been continued, when the Mayor spoke; “It is a splendid thought, but can you realize it, as you wish; can you make these people understand? I suppose too, there is an ulterior aim, something outside of the mere literary or poetic image of these superseded cultures. What is that?”

“Oh, there will be much more than my museum. There will be instructions, lectures and these must all conform to a definite progressive training in the idea of the improved modern state over all these decadent and vanished social states. I wish to show the rise of the conception and the realization under our modern forms of government of property; I want to accentuate the individualistic idea, the definite personal progression, by which the whole community rises to higher things, and I want to give that all a christian imprint, because I believe that the christian formula is the only one that fits the requirements of our day, as I believe that they have always fitted the requirements of every day.

“The vagaries and the perplexities of men would vanish if they could all feel—rich and poor alike—the dissolving efficacy of Love, mutual toleration, mutual forbearance, and if too in accordance with the hierarchical type of the Church they could feel the necessary relations of higher and lower in the social scale. The socialists are levellers: they would crop all men short to one height and that the shortest; the wonderful picture of our world with its atmosphere and relief would all become like a Chinese drawing, all foreground, out of proportion and distorted.”The Mayor was hearing very carefully now, and he was looking at the speaker with a flushed face—the index of some sudden resolution—his hands folded before him and his lips just slightly tightened—a further assurance of a forming determination.

“It will surprise you, Miss Lorimer, but <i>I</i> am a socialist in a way, and perhaps you will remonstrate with me if I say that I would invoke the traditions and the teachings of Christianity to convince you, that you ought to be one too—<i>in a way</i>.”

The speaker was now smiling with a deferential suggestion of not wishing to be misunderstood or rebuked. When he looked that way he was at his best, and Helen Lorimer forsook her dogmatism and answered back with a strange girlish sweetness and yearning; “Ah! please: You wish to confuse me. Tell me just what you mean, and tell me too how you can be a socialist and say the things you said to me at the City Hall when I came to you to crave mercy for socialists. I think I can undeceive you as to Christianity being socialistic—in your sense.”

The Mayor sat down and leaning on the arm of his chair spoke slowly, softly, without ostentation, and with an earnestness that was the outpouring of his deepest convictions.

“Christianity must, I think, be socialistic in all senses. What does it pretend to be, to do? Surely it is not solely a Gospel of Preparation, it is also a Gospel of Life;this life, this weary work-a-day world, with its inequalities, its disrupting accidents, its amazing wildernesses of need, of sickness, of despair, of the manifold disorganizing vagaries of temper, and of the vast Upas Tree of evil.

“Is it not meant to meet all that and help it? How can it be done? I think in one way only. By preaching the essential community of all the productive powers of the state, by helping to make society a vast machine, if you will, running smoothly like a perfect machine, with all its parts coordinated, adjusted, registered, inter-active, reciprocating. Christianity should denounce the tyranny of the individual and the individualistic conceit. Society organized upon individualization is organized upon Greed. The principle of individualism is the feral type, the manner of wild nature, of preying and carnivorous appetite; it is the struggle, internecine, sanguinary, continuing, wherein the strongest survives and it alone.

“Christianity I take you mean to assert, is a superinduced order at war with nature, intended to overcome nature even to the extent of exterminating its normal instincts,” the voice speaking became as it were stealthily murmurous for an instant, and then it rose again sonorous and persuasive.

“Can I care, can you care, can anyone care, whether an industrial metamorphosis like this strikes at the roots of literature and art, so long as it means redemption, elevation, and happiness for Humanity?<i>The main fact about life is living.</i> Should we not be more interested in making the world absolutely habitable for the millions that are in it, than in furnishing subjects to art and letters, because it is tragic and disappointing; full now of dark shadows and high lights too sharply contrasted conditions, too dangerous abysses and too risky peaks?

“Art of the kind we have known, and literature of the kind we have read, might be discouraged in an earth absolutely at peace, absolutely happy, absolutely comfortable, absolutely <i>bourgeoise</i>, if you will. But perhaps some other sorts of art and writing might appear. It is not easy to stagnate the human mind, but if its activity depends on the misery of the human heart then let it stagnate.

“And your Christianity, why is it not socialistic? It began so. Your Christ, observe Miss Lorimer I will be abjectly honest, not mine—<i>your</i> Christ told the rich man to give all he had to the poor and to follow him;” at these words Helen Lorimer seemed to quiver as if with a sort of terror, still her eyes never left the speaker’s face, and because they dwelt so courageously upon his own the speaker surrendered himself to the sway of his impulse, without reservation, driven on by the novel inspiration of imposing upon the splendid being in front of him by his own indomitable frankness.

“Why can not the civilized minds of the world get together and establish some sort of a vast homogeneity, some standards of co-operative living, an international fusion, a set of rules governing every aspect of life, at least so far as rules will.Let it be a machine made society, machine run, but like a perfect machine running smoothly with oiled parts, with certainty, and exquisitely effective, from top to bottom clean and happy.”

The Mayor ended but Miss Lorimer continued her fixed gaze at least for a moment longer; that ended too as she rose, withdrew a little, passing her hands over a cleverly reconstructed mimic sphinx. The young man’s face had resumed its paleness, but his eyes shone; their eagerness was not unnoticed, and when Helen Lorimer after a minute’s indecision returned, and stood near him, his expression changed from its challenging confidence, and grew quietly grave, and then he looked at her with a queer questioning humility:

“Do I offend you? You will not think less of me, will you, if I have been sincere? As to your visit to the City Hall in the interest of these unfortunate men, and my stubbornness there, that has nothing to do with my convictions. Perhaps I have not much sympathy with the Longtinsky type, and at any rate his ideals, if he has any, are not mine, and Order, Security, Law must be continued at all hazards. I do not think that chaos will form a desirable introduction to a perfect state.”

“No indeed, Mr. Mayor,” returned Miss Lorimer, a smile struggling upward in features that had become a little daunted and astonished with his unexpected ardor and confession; “No. I could feel no offense—but somehow I cannot think you are right. Society is not mechanical; you cannot make it so.In my mind christian society is an organization, whose life—I mean its spiritual vitality—is religion, and let me too be frank—the religion of the Catholic Church; an organism with parts, functions, and these of high and low degree though all serviceable to one end—and bearing the blossom and fruit of character.

“Perhaps you will say an organism is socialistic—that our own bodies with their various parts are socialistic. Well—perhaps—and yet—” the speaker reflectively looked at the Mayor with a puzzled honest glance, which brought the pleasantest of smiles into his face, and with a little gentle laugh he hastened to increase her confusion:

“Of course—if any living thing were not a socialistic compound, it would fall to pieces. All of its organs must become fraternally united, fraternally active, fraternally sound: if they compete with each other, if they engage in self assertions of independence, and begin a war upon each other, they are no longer fraternal but fratricidal. Has not most society, as we read of it in history, been just that?”

Miss Lorimer did not seem to be convinced. The problem “gave her pause,” and the half pained, half amused indecision in her face served naturally to make the Mayor and herself better acquainted. Indeed it pushed them forward to a very close mental companionship. Miss Lorimer’s brow cleared, her eyes lost their humorous dismay, she felt the grip of argument and flung back her answer with almost a vindictive taunt.“But society can be social without being socialistic; individual initiative does not forbid mutual toleration, help, recognition. Will you kill the principle of property that it has taken so long to form, formed too by bloodshed, heroism of all sorts, invention and patience? The savage is a socialist, the despot is one too, and tyrannies are socialistic. The goad to effort is the prospect of possible possession. Your socialistic society will be a sponge, made of innumerable identical monads, not a Ship of State moving on to new lands, new hopes, new treasures.”

“No simile will help you,” retorted the Mayor, who was enjoying himself, who felt the thrills of a deepening friendship with the woman before him, deepened too by just such an amiable and thoughtful contest, “the Ship is itself a socialistic State. Each man who is on it has his work, his pay, and lives on the winnings of the voyage. Socialism does not preclude promotion, nor to my mind, property, but it greatly equalizes the chances of having anything at all for those who to-day have nothing.

“As a child without liberty, a man, supported by his family, lives, the imprudences or accidents of his life deprive him of support when he becomes of age and is given liberty. Liberty to five or six men out of ten means destruction. Let the State once become a family, and all of its children live, for it will be a family rich enough to support them. The best, the smartest, if you will, the most able will control, but not altogether for themselves. They will no longer use their wits to get the best of those around them—as I have, as others do—for selfish reasons. They will be saved from themselves.Greatness of character, that I know Miss Lorimer you adore, will not be diminished because struggle—that is the hard, sordid, internecine—conflict sort—ceases; greatness of character will be apportioned more liberally to many. And there will be struggle enough. No ideal state can escape it; struggle against sin, sickness, disease, ignorance, waste, revolt, prejudice, against storm, earthquake the viciousness of nature. Only by a process of unification, consolidation, can the race burst its bonds, and reach Utopia. You say, it is a dream—perhaps—but dreams have lifted men to Heaven.”

Miss Lorimer hesitated for one instant, but a new light of appreciation entered her face; the Mayor had won—something—and perhaps much.

“Well, Mr. Mayor, you press it strongly, and because it now comes to me under a different guise, let me suspend judgment. You may teach me more—but surely individual responsibility cannot carelessly be forgotten, and with me, back of everything is the Church. Bonald has written that society does not consist of the walls of our houses or the ramparts of our towns but that wherever a human being is found there is a father, a mother, a child, a language, heaven, earth, God and a religion.”

A shadow of pain crossed the face of the young man. He rose from his chair, stooped towards Miss Lorimer, who had sat down and in a voice that vibrated with a distinct suggestion of sorrow, said: “You may teach me what you can about the Church, but to me it seems unlikely that the Church stands so stubbornly for things as they are.”“The Church revolutionized the world. It brought into action all the unselfish sentiments you appeal to. The culture in sympathy it has given to the world makes your Socialism possible. Besides” Miss Lorimer grew more beautiful before his face, as some transfiguring grace of Hope and Faith rested on her, and gave her an indefinable sweetness, “the Church gazes beyond the rim of this earth. Its feet stand on the earth but its head is above the clouds. You remember Virgil’s image:

<i>Ingreditur que solo, et caput inter nubila condit?</i>”

The Mayor laughed, “Ah, yes, Miss Lorimer, but for most practical purposes it is best to keep our eyes fastened on the ground. Really star gazing seldom helps us to walk straight. But do not rebuke me, I understand. I do indeed, and I would not do myself such harm in your eyes as to appear to be cynical.”

A swinging window frame pushed by a softly rising breeze borne to them from the river, swung back, and the delicious summer air, fragrant and warm, blew in upon them, breathing its delicate febrile sensuousness, and almost—as if a voice, by some disembodiment only present to their interior sense, had spoken—summoning them to come out into the night The window opened upon one of the broad balconies which encircled the mansion at this attic story, and as they both looked to the portal caught by a common sense of interest in the pleasant draught of air, the moonlight also smote the floor, though barely seen in the lighted room.It came from the full moon that now had floated upward over Long Island, and inundated the outspread city with a golden glow, whose burnish only rested here and there where it was not repelled by the electric aureole below it, withdrawing timidly from the spaces bathed in the nether glow of its terrestrial rival.

As it happened the room in which they were, was at the north end of the villa, and at that point occurred one of the gaps in the artificial illumination of the city upon which, as if by its own preemption, the disheartened moonlight still presumingly remained unchallenged. The invitation met their wishes. This personal collision of opinion was out of place that glorious night. The Mayor dearly wished to have done with it, earnest as he was, and just then his heart rebelled against this intellectual coercion. And why was it that Helen Lorimer wistfully gazed at the shrinking intrusion of the wisps of moonlight, and rose obediently to the same unuttered welcome to the outer night? It was a quaint incident that they both rose and walked toward the open window, and they both laughed a little. It seemed to help. Louder laughter came to their ears from the river, where flaming barges sailed with bursts of music, and waifs of song, bewitched—however crude they were—into delightsome sound by the melting amiable night. They could see the opposite banks of the noble stream where the Palisades, formed then into a reservation, showed their columnar masses weirdly glistening, for on them fell the moonlight with the peccant light of the wavering electrical auroras.

“May we sit here?” said the Mayor.“The night is too lovely to be missed, and this view of the valley and those far distant Van Cortlandt towers is wonderful; it always is. Ah, and the air boats are abroad to-night,” and the speaker pointed upward to the arrowy trails of light, and the Mayor listened to the hum of the aeroplanes.

The chairs were brought out by the Mayor, and the two sat down silent for an instant, startled by the strangeness of their abandonment to the scarcely felt intuition of a mutual recognition.

Below them shone the lamps in an oval courtyard, where sanded paths wound about slender marble pillars, amid stunted and clipped fig trees, where clambering eglantine masked the stone pedestals on which the electric lights glowed, all caught in the embrace of a peristyle whose seats also looked off to the glinting waters of the river. They could see the outstretched roofs of the many mansions of Carrara and flaring on the horizon many steeply rising flames of light, issuing from the monuments in Van Cortlandt Park, like sentinels, along the ridgy back of the Mosholu.

“The world is a task master that it is hard to please.” It was the Mayor’s voice, and the accusing tone seemed sad with some ulterior meaning; he continued: “I mean in all our vocations we fail to meet expectations.”

“Because the world expects too much; it is always looking for an idol and an ideal. We all do. It is better to be disappointed in what we do than to be destitute of ambitions. But the end of the matter is that this world is insufficient. It is provisional, probational, and the prelude of completer states.“_The flower that smiles to-day
To-morrow dies
All that we wish to stay
  Tempts and then flies.
What is this world’s delight?
Lightning that mocks the night,
  Brief even as bright._”

“Those are Shelley’s words,” answered the Mayor, “I know them; but they are vitiated by an obliquity of thought. The flower that fades, the fleeting pleasure, the thought—quick lightning, are beautiful, <i>because</i> they vanish, because they are the transports of a moment, because they are figures in the shimmering flying weave of things. It is the marvel of change that helps us to live, and it is a progressive change, too, an eternally upward march. The vanished delights are only replaced by better ones.”

“_So on our heels a fresh perfection treads,
A power more strong in beauty, born of us
And fated to excel us, as we pass
In glory that old darkness._

“And <i>with</i> us, as eternal souls, the fresh perfection is Paradise or Heaven.” Again there was a silence, and the Mayor’s face was turned to the speaker gazing so intently with perhaps, could it have been narrowly determined, a haunting wonder and amused delight, as if he prized in her the power of faith, so dead in his own heart. But he intended to concede nothing:“The thought is pleasant, but is it true? Is it not wiser to submit to our limitations, to bow the neck to the necessitous hardship of our place and not too fondly dwell in dreams?”

“Our limitations are made by ourselves. There can be no necessary limit to belief, if we will but listen.”

“Listen—listen to what?” the voice trembled a little.

“Well I will not say the Church—the Revelation—but listen to the desires within us. Surely it is as legitimate to believe our hopes as to set up our Despair, and worship that.”

“No,” answered the Mayor, “not Despair but Negation—and not set up as an idol, but like an inner controlling voice—like our conscience, if you will—whispering warnings against intoxicating fancies, against too warm and vaporous delusions, that will make us incapable and foolish. Let the Promethean age come, but it can only come by effort, work, thought, not by submission to veiled and luxurious promises.”

The voice was not hard or strained, nor was there any suspicion of defiance in it; if anything, there was in it, to the ears of the listener—and the listener was alert, eager, yearning even—an admonition of gentle remonstrance only.

“If Christianity does not mean effort, work, thought, I do not know what it means; and the Promethean age, will it come on this earth, and as we are?Can it? Does it help to murmur with the Cenci:

        “_If there should be
No God, no heaven, no earth, in the void world,
The wide grey lampless deep unpeopled world?_

And are dreams so useless? In what harbor of refuge do we crave to enter when we sleep, if it is not pleasant dreams. And is not even sleep itself necessary? If doubt and angry questionings are to fill our lives with turmoil, can we work at all? There must be a foothold somewhere, somehow, a ground level where our new ambitions rise.”

“Hardly,” the Mayor answered quickly, and his voice rose with that peculiar sweetness of tone that fell on Helen Lorimer’s ears like music, “Work for work’s sake is ground enough; bettering conditions, giving beauty to the world, hastening the conquest of nature, mastering power, dispelling disease, winning knowledge, conquering the silence and oppression of things, rising eternally to far off dispensations of greater and greater power. Man’s ultimatum is the world and what he can make of <i>it</i>, and <i>himself</i>.

        “_The lightning is his slave; heavens utmost deep
        Gives up her stars, and like a flock of sheep
They pass before his eye, are numbered, and roll on!
        The tempest is his steed, he strides the air;
        And the abyss shouts from her depth laid bare,
Heaven, hast thou secrets? Man unveils me; I have none._”“And yet—there remains Death. Will you conquer Him? And Age and Sorrow? Does Knowledge and Power mean everything, do they mean anything without companionship and Love?” Miss Lorimer stopped, checked by a sudden embarrassment, her words carried meanings which stirred her blood, and her instinct told her too acutely it was for both of them dangerous going—just there; the word she had uttered seemed even then to her like some torch which, inconsiderately dropped, lights the eager dry prairies when the wind is rising. Perhaps it was such; her own heart leaped strangely, an unwonted warmth flowed stealthily yet urgently through her body, and the whispering summer night bore to her ears its undulous distant sounds, as if they were faintly calling to her to follow the new summons of a new joy, opening before her, rich, splendid, and fathomless. She leaned on the balustrade of the balcony, and watched the sky, shamed by some untoward concussion of her being, unknown before, discovered in this last instant, as when a man walking in the night enters a disk of illumination which reveals himself and his position, and startled, he finds another step reaches a brink of peril.

And what were these serious words that came now to her ears, moving faster and faster, breathed, she seemed to feel, almost upon her cheeks, eloquent with conviction; prayers they were—and such prayers!—not as she knew them in her Church—tapers of devotion—but prayers from an adoring man’s heart, that smote her own as caresses; how willingly she let them enter there, where she stored all her aspirations, secret treasures of affection, and her own best resolves!And now when they were in, how proud they grew, holding that inner citadel of her own as theirs and staunchly possessing the whole place! But this was surrender—she must eject them, push them out from those uninvaded precincts, and close the doors again, or else—Oh how desolating it was to have those wicked prayers within, believing themselves welcome, and acting so importunately the parts of welcomed guests. Oh for some stalwart hand to rid her of them, to send them backward, stumbling backward, unrewarded, repelled, killed—No! No! not killed; that were inhuman; just ordered off and out Forever?

Ah, well, let them stand off awhile. This haste is insolence, worse, ingratitude; let these clustering rampant prayers become more formal, more respectful, less rapacious with their stormy signals and provocations. There must be decorum, a slow approach, the signs of parley and convention. These prayers are blatant, blustering, overbearing; they have entered an open door and—now—once in, they will not out. Oh, God!—they are indeed too cruel!

Her Pride, that glorious mistress of her heart tells them to leave, and they do not stir. They taunt her, they seize her, tie her chaste hands—Merciful Heavens must it be so!—and throw <i>her</i> out, and then—these perfidious demons!—smile at their conquest, dry their beseeching eyes, and inhabit unmoved that dear shrine of her soul wherein she thought no steps should ever tread but those of Memory and Faith. Has it been made so rare and lovely for this profanation—Oh, Mother of Sorrows hear;—and Helen Lorimer’s head sank upon her arms, on the stone railing, and she sobbed, and in her sobs the prayers still repeated themselves, and now stood like pitiful supplicants—no longer bold or careless—and she put her hands forward to them, and pressed them to her breast.Suddenly they all were dear to her; she would not have lost one! No, they should not go; the place was theirs; her heart received them, and kept them. And these were the prayers.

“Yes, Love and Companionship, those accompany the best effort. They are the linked comrades who hold up the arms while the battle rages, who press to our lips the chalice which feeds our strength, who fold us in their arms when the victory is won and who bring sleep to our wearied brain. Oh, have I not yearned for that shelter and encouragement! But neither Church nor Religion could bring to me that aid. Pardon me Miss Lorimer, but the empty voices of a Church, or the old reiterations of a creed are not the Love nor the Companionship a man craves; Oh, something much nearer the inmost inscrutable needs of his nature. Surely your own heart tells you and if—no restraint can withhold this confession Miss Lorimer—I tell you now that I love you, that I have with a fixed unswerving, tireless determination, meant to say this all to you. It is an effrontery for me to speak now, but you touched the overflowing stream of feeling, and it has burst its bounds. Do not disdain me for it; do not let this error, this rashness, foil or hurt me in your approval.

“I beg you to listen, to let the open chance be mine to win you, to forgive this advantage of opportunity I have taken. Hear my promises. I will not molest you; I will prove my love; I will be patient if you can be. You turn away. Are you astonished? Do you feel resentment? Oh say that you do not. Say that I am forgiven, that you will let me see you often. It is a priceless boon for me to know you. The thought of you has grown daily in my mind, until it has become an overmastering desire and adoration.I have lived a barren and struggling life among men, cut off by the relentless pressure of work from the priceless joys of womanly culture. And yet I do not know that matters now. I rejoice that it has been so, for as you have entered my life you come with all the more abounding blessedness of promises, I have until this moment, never dreamed of.

“Speak to me one word—just this—that this sudden loss of restraint is forgiven. I do not know, I did not dream—that I would be so hopelessly swept off my feet. I meant no harm, by every throb of my poor bewildered heart I meant none. Miss Lorimer, I love you, and though that love were born but yesterday, a century would not make it greater. Do you despise me because I cannot believe as you believe? I know that you honor candor, just common honesty—no soul so upright as yours could do otherwise—and I have been candid. Does that seem wrong? I am willing to learn all things. Let me learn from you.”

The head of the sobbing woman before him was raised, its tears were wiped away and the figure that bore it rose, and then the eyes—those wondrous, burning, searching, welcoming eyes—were turned to his, and on that face was born—as if from the enchantment of his own words—a smile, and somehow the young man knew that it was the gesture of permission, and he bowed before the splendor of that beauty, while his heart stood still, engulfed in its own unutterable hopes.<b>CHAPTER VIII</b>

THE CHARIOT OF FLIGHT

Tom Ratler had prospered. He was in favor with God and man. The Mayor liked him. Mr. Briggs, the Mayor’s secretary liked him, especially well, he was so attentive to that gentleman’s social monologues, and his graphic pictures of coming crises. His uninterrupted assent to all Mr. Briggs predicted gave him an enviable preferment, and Mr. Briggs’ own pugnacious instincts, and his muscular prowess made him a natural admirer of Tom’s fine physique, and his manly courage and aplomb. Tom understood most of Mr. Briggs’ discourses, but his apparent acquiescence sprang less from endorsement, than indifference, and his sense of obligation. He was indeed impressed favorably by much of Mr. Briggs’ talk, lately, since his ravished eyes had fallen on the pleasant face and alluring form of Jessie Longtinsky, whose enforced—as he fondly was pleased to think—imprisonment, amid the murky, cloudy and black walls of a murder-breathing, dark, conniving, and impious nihilism, made him more and more anxious for the opportunity of freeing her, and placing her more conveniently near himself.

And among Tom’s friends and patrons none exceeded in admiration the Mayor’s mother. Her benignant eyes shone upon him through her bright spectacles, almost with the fondness of his own dear mother’s, and he became so assiduous, painstaking, and thoughtful, over her comfort and safety in the big family auto, that the old lady experienced a wonderful happiness in seeing him, and being with him; his cheerful ringing voice brought gladness to her heart, and amid the growing evils of the day—for Mrs. Benziger was one of Mr.Briggs’ sympathetic listeners—she felt that her son had a good strong bulwark of defense in Tom, when the dark hour came, when, as Mr. Briggs had often told her, “the streets of the city would be filled with the cries and shot of an intestinal conflict.”

Tom Ratler had frankly admitted to his friend, Ned Links, that he was mortally wounded, that he was in love with a “fairy” on the East Side, and that happiness for him could only be attained by the undivided possession of the beautiful darling, whom he must and would capture. He did not tell his companion that he had met her—this disturbing vision—quite frequently, that the passion had been mutual, and that the accidental glance up to her at the window of her home and her own appreciative view of himself, had conspired into a complete reciprocity, and that the heart struggles of the two partners to the contract, had been of the most excruciating and distinctively exciting sort. To be sure, Tom was hilariously certain that Jessie Longtinsky would be his; his mind leaped over obstacles and his heart thoroughly approved its confidence, but to the tender and filial soul of Jessie, the way ahead of them was dark with parental objections, and the more deadly and hideous shadow of the ravening jealousy of her lover Poniatowsky.

Tom had been made, by the exigencies of his own love, the most alert and subtle sort of a detective, and when he and Mr. Briggs left off their surveillance of the editorial chambers of The Beehive, Tom nightly watched it, and thus discovered the new retreat of the paper, and his idol.Love’s ingenuity had not lost any of its skill in eluding detection or detention in the year 2000, and Jessica was not more willing to meet Lorenzo, than Jessie, in spite of all her maidenly terrors and shrinkings, was happy over the thought of seeing Tom. For she truly loved him. He suddenly seemed to her the impersonation of all the bright and happy things a man may be to a woman, and she was, without any doubt, to Tom, the “best ever.”

So their meetings had been as numerous as they could make them, and Tom was rapidly forming plans for doing away with them altogether by marrying Jessie. These hopes Jessie deprecated because of Poniatowsky. She hoped that Poniatowsky would be called away on some errand of her father’s, or in some way disappear. And yet she admitted to herself, that the man was fiercely enamored of her. She trembled to think of his rage should he find out the true state of things between her and Tom. He had apparently forgotten his first suspicions in that regard, and although he fretted and grew moody over Jessie’s coldness, he was successfully deceived by the lovers as to their meetings. Jessie in her household duties found it necessary to market for the house on the west side of the city, and here, away from observation of her own people, her neighbors, and the prowling steps of her father’s confederates, she saw Tom, and as they agreed to meet very early in the mornings, nothing happened as yet either in Tom’s work, or the meddlesome gossip of her friends, to interfere with these sweet engagements.Now it had come to pass that Ned Links had deserted his employment—lucrative at it was—in the Suicidarium, and the buxom and languorous lady whom Tom had first met there as one of the visualized angels of the future world, to which the suicides were directed, had deserted her employment at the same place. Ned had done this, through Tom’s own disgust with the business, and because Tom had discovered for him an extraordinary opportunity as the motorist of a dirigible balloon. It had become in New York, at that distant time, a summer sport to traverse the skies at night with illuminated balloons. On the most auspicious evenings the spectacle thus created in the air was extraordinary, and the elements of a new expensive entertainment added another wonderment to the extravagance and exhilaration of the city’s tempestuous life. Many of these balloons were used by the public, and what were termed “aeronauticals,” were popular excursions through the atmospheric regions over the city, and the surrounding country. The balloons could be hired, carrying parties of not more than six, and they were taken at stations called “levitories.” These were large—usually square—enclosures, in which the balloons were stalled. Here they were inflated, repaired, and prepared for use. They were known also as Zeppelins, from the count of that name who had in Germany, at the beginning of the twentieth century, made his excursion in the air in this manner. An attached car suspended behind the operator of the balloon (himself in the elevated “pilot”) accommodated the travellers, an apparatus of revolving fans propelled it, and a steering gear of simple efficacy turned and wheeled it through the invisible thoroughfares of the air.The happy chance came when Ned was able to invite his friend Tom to take one of these aerial trips and to both the young men the thought came instantly that the trip would be of incomparable charm if the ladies of their hearts accompanied them. Thus alone, in the circling heights of the star covered sky, their loves would take new delights. It certainly was a pretty thought, and Tom pronounced it “stunning,” which gave Ned much pleasure as he had an immeasurable estimate of Tom’s opinion, and he knew that Tom had repulsed him after that dreadful night at the Suicidarium. So it was done. “Neddy you’re just a kissing machine, and it’s not me that’s going to have my mouth blistered with your smacks;” and then, as the wrathful Neddy started to his feet: “Well, a dozen more Neddy for good measure, and I’ll not miss them, dearest.”The party made their <i>rendez-vous</i> at a Zeppelin in the Bronx, not far from the edge of the Zoological Park. The little group stared a few moments at the brick walls of the balloon-house. The light within the enclosure came from hissing flaring gas flames, and this light, almost all placed below, threw upward a pale penumbral illumination over the enormous shape above, swaying heavily with an imminent expression of suddenly losing patience, and freeing itself with a monstrous spasm to leap to its wide home in the skies. It was an enormous bulk; depending cables of rope and rods of steel held two platforms, one arranged like a cushioned box, the other holding the chair of the operator. Its monstrous size resembled some colossal cigar, and around it, clasping it like giant bracelets were five rings of electric lamps. Above and below the slopes which defined its forward and backwards ends, were also placed four strong lamps with reflectors.All of these lamps were fed from an electric battery on the operator’s platform, and could be turned off and on, with the lever of a switch. Thus designed the aerial boat swam in the atmosphere like a shooting column of light, some translated phosphorescent ascidian from the deep, and, when the lights on some fabrics were variegated in colors, the strange shimmering and scudding object, in the night, became a fantastic whisking prism.

None of these lamps were illuminated as the friends entered the doorway, and the whole mass over their heads, inertly slumberous and yet stirring with wheezing oscillations, looked dark and dangerous. Ned had made arrangements for the service of an operator so that he and Tom and their companions might enjoy uninterruptedly the pleasant frolic. Everything was in readiness, the balloon filled, the fans in working order, while the dead still night auspiciously augured a successful trip. The balloon was released, slightly slipping upward along steel guides with a rasping groan, and the passenger car was attached, the operator took his place, and the party of merry-makers, with little shrieks from Delia, and subdued exclamations of wonder from Jessie gathered quickly in the comfortable roofless car with its windowed sides, while almost at the same moment with a few gaspy creaks the whole monster rose softly, and like a cloud was lifted above the barn, through the wide open roof, and floated like a gigantic thistle down into the amazing beauty of the night.How wonderful it was; Suddenly the lamps upon the great drum over their heads flashed upon the eyes of the voyagers, a shrill piping whistle announced the flight, and upward with broad curves, swinging right and left, the machine accomplished its magic circuit through the sky. Higher and higher, the delicious warm air fanning their cheeks, the infinite delicate splendor of the city beneath them, and far and far away the veiled country-sides sprinkled and pricked with lights. It was like passing into a world of unearthly things, and the charmed Jessie and stunned Delia with Tom—the affair was not so uncommon to Ned—were silenced into admiration of the strange experience.

And, as they turned from side to side, watching the shifting features below them, darts of moving lights swept from the same earth, announcing the arrival into the fields of the air of new phantoms like themselves. These increased in numbers. The time was propitious, and ascending from the nether shades, like immense glowing insects the luminous bodies stole in from all quarters crossing and recrossing, dipping and bending, swooping here with awful speed, and there as if becalmed, swept by the mild currents of the atmosphere, alone, when, as it often happened, songs and music issued from the balancing cars until—it seemed so to Jessie and Tom—they had become a part of some heavenly realm.

For some time they kept in with the sporting crowd of vessels, sailing up and down over the spread chromosphere of the great city so far below. They slid upward upon invisible runners far aloft, then turned and launched backward, and down, with such impetuosity as to draw from the girls faint cries, and a whistling wonder from the lips of Tom.It had grown to be July since Tom had reached New York, and the nights were warm, though in the serene upper airs with salty emanations from the sea sweeping over them the temperature was soft and cooling, and—here was the wonder of it—it was the glorious Fourth. It was amazing to note the city like a curdled pond of light with disks, streams, undulations, spots, and dark clots in it. If they approached it nearer a faint hum came to their ears; when they rose high the antennae of lights stretched indefinitely off in skeins of lines and braids. Now and then they caught the roar of an earth train of cars, sometimes the chirp of a bird passed them in the sky, and sometimes they ran through a cloud of insects. The wonderful instrument obeyed every whim of its pilot, and followed the aerial heights and depths as a ship rises and falls on the liquid hills of the ocean.

The new moon blinked at them when they first started out, and it somehow seemed a companion in the clouds, its golden crescent leaving them at last over the vaporous edge of the swinging earth. They mounted higher, and they shivered with a strange cosmic cold, the tiny pavement of cirrus dipped cloudlets seemed just above them, and the weirdness kept them still with amazement.

They all laughed when Jessie whispered; “Don’t let us leave the earth yet,” and Tom swept her a little closer to himself and told her, “It wouldn’t matter where they went as long as SHE was with them, and he guessed it might be a little safer for both of them, if he and she started out for another star,” which allusion to Poniatowsky brought Jessie’s cheeks nearer to Tom, and then their lips touched, in which mutual consolation they both attained a philosophic indifference to Fate.“Lor! But this is dandy,” exclaimed the suppressed Delia at length, “Those scoops at Coney, and the Ferris Wheel aren’t in it. It sorter makes my head go round and it’s just shivering cold,” to which complaint Ned resorted to such readjustments of his arms as might correct this inconvenience.

Again they fled from these heights to the warmer zones, and again skimmed over the broad city, that bubbled below, and now, in the darkening night began its wondrous celebration of the nation’s birth day. Along the lines of increasing splendor, in all its functions, the great city in the year 2000, had developed a regard for the spectacular possibilities of the Fourth that made that day the climax of the year’s shows. A southern temperament had stolen into the city’s blood. It was hard to say exactly how it happened, unless the settlement of French, and the expanding influence of the Italian fusions, had effected it, but in all things artistic and ceremonial, this American city attained vaster designs of beauty. Even the <i>Mardi Gras</i> had started its grotesque and glittering processions within New York. Some affirmed that the milder climate had aided the aesthetic regeneration, and others ruefully regretted a change, which began to put on the city the semblance of an extravagant dissipation.

The exaltation of the Fourth began about the middle of the century, about 1950, when it was proposed to give it a new official dignity in the calendar of the city’s holidays. It was felt by some agitated patriots that the great anniversary grew clouded in its meaning, that the sense of it could be supremely accentuated by taking advantage of the growing instincts for display, and making of the day a memorable splash of splendor in the year.Subscriptions were opened, throughout the year, and the money thus given with a liberal largess from the city’s treasury, was devoted to the placement of marvellous firework exhibitions. The occasion was really remarkable. Night and day were given over to its celebration. A gorgeous procession filled up the forenoon of the day; at noon the bands of the city played at all available points for two hours; then succeeded a free performance at the theatres of a play, usually patriotic, or quite as frequently classic and elevating, some Shakespearean drama being performed at the enormous auditorium built by the city for free educational drama. And at night, after a furious cannonading, at nine o’clock the pyrotechnic ebullition began.

The growing habit of “ballooning” made the precautions necessary, to regulate, on the night of the Fourth of July, these aerial revellers, and so a certain time was instituted for the beginning of the show. All private pyrotechnics were discouraged or forbidden, and the city turned out at night, the crowds threading their ways to the point of display. The regulations for the balloons were for them to remain at the elevation of one thousand feet, or to congregate over the centre of the city, for the firework stands were placed around the city, further towards its confines, and here they were set off from tall towers. This permitted the air voyagers the advantage of centering their barks over the centre of the city from which view point the splendor of the spectacle grew enormously. The picture above, below, was one of festal power.The city was illuminated, the bells of the churches were rung in chimes at seven, the shining balloons congregated over the central parts of the city, or far above the range of rockets and starry exploding bombs, and at nine of the evening, began the stupendous assault upon the skies, of the ascending battalions of terrestrial meteors. The affair became noteworthy throughout the country, and although imitated in many of the larger cities, the New York outburst easily excelled the rest. Strangers poured into the city, and the bonifaces of the metropolis found it profitable for them to swell with liberal donations the public funds for this dazzling function.

Ned Link’s party, in the midst of congregated equipages, was floating, immobile, over the great middle districts of the city, waiting for the imminent splendors that would soon terminate the inordinate and ambitious day. A shout from the aerial sightseers announced the first glimmerings of blue stars, rising into the skies about the edges of the huge town. They sprouted up around the cliffy congeries of buildings. For once the electric fervor in the air died out, and the propitious suppression allowed the power of the fireworks full sway. The blue stars crept higher, higher; they fenced the horizon with spouting, azure, soaring, and eclipsing flashes. It seemed as if the voyagers were enclosed in some enchanted and deepening circle of light. Then suddenly above these first reconnaissances of fire, rose swiftly, and with firm gushes of spreading flakes of color, the sheaves of sky-rockets. They made a higher fence than the first blue stars, and set in the dark sky a tiara of thickly sprinkled jewels, borne like a crown by the imposing and noble city.These flaming messengers dashed upon the blackness of the night, the faint hiss of their ascent just heard, while, with wayward and ever graceful vagaries of motion the speeding sparks shot outward, mingling in chains of dropping, glowing, ashes.

The rocking flotilla in the summer air was encircled by the spouting fires. Ever and anon some audacious blade of light seemed to hover over them, and the descending sparks of the wooden bolt which had risen with the rocket fell among them. Then there was a little fright, and scared cries came from here and there to be succeeded the next moments by shouts and exclamations of wonder, as fresh climbing ladders of flame rose in the air. The third and most marvellous stage of the show was reached when the enchanted margins of the wide circle below them sent up the whizzing, whistling, bursting bombs. The zenith seemed to rain stars, the pale orbs of the heavens were extinguished, and a carpet of mixed lights was spread through the sky, into which with sudden coruscations, at regular intervals, sprang a sun-like meteor which scattered far and wide golden jets, shafts of fiery fury that sped hither and thither eclipsing all their colored companions, and looking like improvised suns, or perhaps monstrous unfolding chrysanthemums of light, their sinuous petals ejected from their centres, in writhing lines, each ended with a ball of intenser gold.

Cheers broke from the hitherto stilled audience in the air, and it was reechoed from the earth, where thousands stood in the blackness far underneath; it seemed as if some elemental stage of creation was going on around them, or as if the ruptured earth was vomiting its interior fires upward from its burning depths, and they stayed within the centre of its belching energy.It was indeed altogether wonderful, and Tom and Jessie felt its scenic majesty, while the less responsible Delia and the idle-hearted Ned, spent themselves in gurgles and exclamations. Suddenly again it was inky black about them; not a spark lingered in the tired air. It was the theatrical prelude to the last astonishment. The dead silence accorded with the emptiness of sky, and then without announcement, without sound, there stole upward from the towers, the shrubby Bengal lights, blue, green, and red, like funeral pyres on the horizon. They sprouted and fumed, and cast a strange reverberant patchwork of colors on the black background, and then behind them, so it seemed, a higher erection of pale purple lights strove outward like quickening ghosts, speeding high, quivering and falling down, towering at this point, and anon declining into fringes of light simply. Then came a wizard mixture of bombs and rockets, and weird shrieking sticks of flame, the whole medley in flowery confusion embroidering the sky with arabesques and patterns, and then the hastened climax ended all. The bridges of the city crossing the enclosing channels of the Hudson and the East river flashed into sight as pendulous girdles of flickering, dancing, mobile gold. It was most superb. The transporting beauty of it, the sudden elation of it, the compelling splendor and the fairy opulence! Beneath its radiance the city shone, its multiplied structure rose startlingly, its serrations along the edges looked like huge saw teeth, and the reflecting roofs glistened liquidly, while, as the illumination died away, with hiss and smoke and the wafted smells of powder filled the air, the electric lights slowly raised their heads, as it were, again, and the penumbral glows succeeded, and the great display was a memory.The aerial equipages moved away this way and that, their resuscitated lamps gleaming as they scattered off like gigantic fire-flies, most of them descending to the earth, and some bent on a night’s sport, soaring up, while numbers from surrounding towns shot hither and thither, back to their resting places in New Jersey, Connecticut, and the north. A few distant lightning flashes shown on the gathered clouds, and faint low rumbles told of thickening thunder storms.

“What do you say to a run to the end of the island—I mean Long Island—then a dip out to sea, and back again by the Narrows. It’s a good night and our lunch baskets are full?” It was Ned’s voice that made the proposal and there was an instant’s silence. It appeared too risky. The man at the motor yelled something back to them that he would take them home if they wanted to return, or would he turn the ship and give them a longer sail?

“Come,” reiterated Ned, “it’s a splendid night and so still and then—the girls;” but it was just the girls who were driven into sharp spasms over the novelty of doing it. Jessie was really anxious about going back, and Delia was frankly frightened. Ned had telephoned back to the man, and the balloon was headed for the Sound. It sailed low and the steamers could be made out as streaks of light on the sooty waters. The motion was delightful, the spread world, over which their winnowing way like a bird’s flight led, was specked with villages and towns and—well the expostulations were overcome; Tom promised it would be “all right,” and Ned soothed Delia into a charmed consent.Away they went, the churning fans shot them on, and the sea air soon blew in their faces. The outlying edges of Brooklyn were being left, and the spangled ground, diapered with lamps, caught up into clots and then spilled over the flat plane in twinkling lines, came into view indistinctly, for they were flying low. A moan and the machine with a half sickening dip swept upward, and the air grew chillier, the stars came nearer, and far away on the dark rim of the world sheet lightnings played again, and a rattle in the rigging of the boat, was premonitory of a coming squall, perhaps, though Ned said; “Humbug! no storm to-night. Anyhow we can beat round the island, and win out at the start, before the wind stirs a ripple on the ocean.”

He called again to the sky skipper, and the scudding fans took on a swifter motion, as a slight droning hum broke out from them, and with a start the ship scudded like some fleeing bird over the land, past Hempstead plains, a little web of light, past the wrinkled shores of Northport, on—, on—, over the spark dotted outlines of Lake Ronkonkoma, and swerving again to the hoarse shores over Patchogue, to domiciled Moriches, over Eastport and West Hampton, with midnight ringing out from its tall steeples; closer to the ground by the Shinnecock Hills, where the roosters sent up to them their morning cheer; again across the island to Orient, sleeping in the dark with a returning dash on the wings of the wind to Riverhead, where Chalmers lived and died;again with a turn that made their heads spin across Peconic Bay, over Shelter Island and Sag Harbor to East Hampton, and Amagansett, yet dreaming on its watery hills, the rest and haven of artists, and then, with a tearing gait that made the machinery rip and whistle, and shook them as they dashed against the inrushing winds, to Montauk Point, where wharves, lined in lights, told the sharp story of change, for now the steamers from Europe made this their “touchdown,” and the emigrant and tourist was whisked across Long Island to New York.

It was time to turn back, it was no longer certain that the night would remain clear; the rumblings had increased, and from the caverns of the east and south sprang forks of lightning. The girls were now a little appalled, and Tom felt that they must make a run for it. Ned told the driver to keep out to sea, and along the shore, and with the wind behind them they went forward furiously.

But, quicker than they, came the hurrying storm, and the piled clouds seemed to stand in their way like black buttresses, spitting fire. The gloom encircled them; they suddenly tossed and spun like a top in a whirlpool of currents. There was consternation, and, for a moment only, indecision, then Ned roared through the “phone” to lighten up and ascend, and the next instant with wheeze and crack, the gray hulk pushed upward through what seemed a sort of atmospheric funnel, while buffeting winds slapped the voyagers in the face, and the rocking balloon, pitched hither and thither, sometimes actually whirled around in circles, that threatened to rupture and destroy her. But with a sort of obstinate energy it pushed its way through seething clouds, amid a crashing report of thunder and the tracery gleamings of lightning.The rain splashed upon them in bucketfulls, and plugs on the floor of the basket were removed to let the deluge escape; but the ascent continued, while the little party crouched and clung together in a very real terror. At a moment when deliverance seemed impossible, and the darkness closed in upon them closer, while the billows of air spun their craft around and jerked it here and there, and the ominous chafing of the cables promised some sudden separation of the wheeling and shivering carriage in which they rode, and a stern chilling frost made itself felt in the rattling hail—at that very moment, when Tom’s stout heart quailed, and the girls had fainted, and Ned clung to the reeling seats, the nose of the ship issued into ether above all the disturbance, and the staunch vehicle floated again on even keel in a splendid radiance of stars. It was a miracle of change. They looked down upon a surface like the surges of an angry ocean, a vortex of colliding vapors, rent with seams of light, and belching thunders.

The stunned travellers aroused themselves, and Tom and Ned set about resuscitating their frightened friends, when suddenly a heavy dark object shot to their feet, and on the floor of the cage they saw with horror, the crimped and curled form of the stricken pilot of the balloon. They had almost forgotten him in their own excitement, although Ned had noticed that he was swaying at his post. He might indeed have missed falling on the lower compartment, and dropped instead plump to the earth, had not a tilted position of the balloon actually brought him above the under car. There he lay unconscious. It was a desperate position, the soaring machine unguided, forcing its way on undirected, and the man at their feet who had controlled its wheel, tossed from his seat, overcome and possibly dying. A cry broke from the terror stricken group.Ned cast a sharp glance overhead, and saw that the platform of the pilot was unharmed. <i>But it was empty.</i> Could he reach it? Tom bent over the unconscious motorist and found him injured, but alive. He moaned in pain and cried out as his limp form was moved. It was too evident that some bones were broken, and Tom seized some of the blankets and rugs, made an improvised bed, and then gave the sinking man some of the wine they had fortunately provided themselves with, as Ned knew the cold they might encounter.

All were now startled into attention by their singular predicament and all looked to Ned for the next move. They were not long left in doubt as to his intentions. He told them he must ascend to the deserted platform. It was a hard, it seemed an impossible attempt, but there was nothing else for it. As it was, they were drifting on a pathless ocean, and actually headed away from the mainland. Tom and Ned both knew the situation meant their eventual destruction, and both grew pale with dread, and the poor women strained every nerve to keep their consciousness.

Could he climb to the deserted seat by the cables and attachments of the car? The conditions were favorable. The air was quiet, and the unjostled framework permitted perhaps the dangerous enterprise. And yet—there appeared to be one chance. The travelling car was swung on chains from the central body of the balloon, and quite detached from the platform of the pilot, that occupied a forward place, and was itself fastened by iron rods to the gas-tank. There was but one connection from the car to the steering platform, and that hardly promised assistance. It was the chain that carried the telephone wire from one to the other. The chain was moderately stout. It looked serviceable; but was it?It could be tested. Then one other thought suggested itself to the anxious scrutiny of Ned, standing stiffly there in the strange exigency of a peril that was to him much more like death than the painted shows which he had watched, wherein indeed so many foolish hearts had quit the world.

This other way of possible rescue was to creep up along the heavier cables holding the car, to the body of the equipage, insert himself among the binding cordage around the cylinder of the balloon, twist somehow through them to the part of the body immediately over the platform, and drop there to the pilot. There was his own strength and skill to be considered, and the measure of both required by the two methods. This gave him pause, and Tom quickly realized the dilemma, and estimated the balancing possibilities.

Both men strained their eyes to follow the chain up to its fastenings on the platform, but into the darkness the links of steel slipped away, and they could not see their continuity more than a few feet.

“Let’s pull it, and find out how it holds,” whispered Tom, and the women stood up behind them listening too, and watching with blanched faces. For them the ordeal was breaking, but while Delia moaned, Jessie was silent. Tom and Ned pulled, at first easily, and then more strongly, until they pressed almost their full strength on the chain. It held. Ned was the first to speak;

“Yes. This is the way. I’ll try it Tom. It’s this or nothing. All the control is up on that platform, and we can do nothing here. Give me a drink and up I go and—Goodbye.”The whisky flask was produced, and Ned took a fillip, but he had scarcely handed the glass back to Tom, when the motionless figure of the man at their feet stirred, and a voice spoke;

“Play out the chain its whole length, and slip off the edge of the car; let one man hold the slack, and then you’ll get a straight pull and go up hand over hand to the pilot. The chain will stand it. I know,” and with a groan the voice ceased, and Tom and Ned without looking at the sufferer, prepared to obey his instructions. First Tom rubbed Ned’s hands until they burned; Ned in spite of the cold, stripped off his coat, but underneath was a cardigan, which might suffice to keep out the creeping chill. Tom told the girls to take their seats, and then he opened the door of one side of the enclosing framework of the car, and Ned seized the coiled chain at a sufficient distance to allow him to drop about vertically underneath the pilot while Tom caught the links beyond him, and they approached to the dreadful edge of the softly swaying car. No word was spoken, and Ned hung his legs off into space, slowly moving outward while Tom firmly held the chain, and so lowered him. It was well done, and Ned hanging in mid-air seemed like a long plumb-bob, just faintly visible. Then the voice spoke again, the voice of the maimed man on the floor;

“You can touch up those lights from the car. There’s a wire at this end of the car, that is tied to the lever of the switch. That will give you more light. I turned them off when the squall struck us.” But Tom was afraid to move to do this. The sudden afflux of light where all had been previously half dark might so startle the suspended man, that he would lose his steadfastness and fall.And yet Tom felt the value of the suggestion, as the light flowing down from the lamps above, would give Ned courage, and make the task less awful, besides the material benefit of showing him better what he was doing. Wavering for an instant as to what he should do, his ears heard Ned’s voice calling him to him; “Pull me back! back! quick!”

Tom caught the chain, and gently, without any shock, hauled Ned back to the car, on which the brave boy scrambled.

“What was it?” asked the wondering Tom.

“It’s too cold for my fingers. They’ll stiffen and drop me. The chain is like ice. You’ve got some gloves here, cut off the fingers, and put them on mine. All I want is the tips. My hand is good for the job.”

Tom had a pair of old kid gloves in his pocket, and these were quickly cut and fixed upon the ends of Ned’s fingers, and once again the two approached to the edge of the car, and Ned prepared to launch himself into the darkling air. But before this Tom had told him of the communication of the wire to the lever of the lamps, and Ned assented to have it pulled. The test was made, and indeed the circle of lights above, on the lower sides of the great body of the balloon, flashed into action, and the descending light softly bathed the anxious group, and glinted on the hanging chain, and lost itself in the dark abysses below, where yet the reverberations of the storm lingered, and the shattered clouds pulsated with occasional strokes of lightning.Ned slid off, slowly allowed by Tom to reach a straight position under the pilot and when this was done, the pull up began, and hand over hand he ascended. Tom watched him with his heart in his mouth, but with such a glow of delight in his splendid courage! Up—up—he went. He was nearing the pilot and Tom could scarcely keep his voice, it seemed so certain he would reach it, and succeed. But, no! he stopped, and Tom could see that Ned had raised his head, Oh! so very slowly—and peeked over to the platform, almost on the level of his face; Tom thought he heard a cry, and then—my God!—the chain fell, past him, its end dangling in the fathomless atmosphere, while its weight dragging on the unspent portion in the car rattled the links about Tom’s feet.

Tom’s eyes had followed the chain in its fall, but though he peered over the sickening side below him, in the cones of light thrown by the electric lamps, he caught no sight of the hurled body of his companion. Had Ned been able to have held on to the chain, the flight downward and the sharp arrest at its end would have wrenched him away and shot him pitilessly to the earth. Tom dreaded to raise his head, for one moment he pressed his hands over his face, and a queer tearing shiver passed through his body, and then—

In that moment the girls had gathered about Tom. They had understood the attempt Ned was to make, and instead now of crouching against each other in a sort of swooning, convulsed embrace, they had watched with eager strained eyes the whole effort. They had seen the loosened chain, the cautiously disappearing body of their friend over the boarded margin of the car, they had even held their gaze fixed upon Ned there in the cold boundless air, and had followed him inch by inch as he rose above them towards that haven of safely—the pilot.Nor were they the only watchers. The poor fellow who had felt the slip, as of a push to eternity, from his high perch, he too had lifted his eyes, and forgot his broken leg as he noted each instant of the perilous, blood curdling creep, up the glistening links of the chain. He had even drawn himself up to Tom, and so the little population of that speck of earth, winging its way through the infinite void, caught there with one shuddering hope of returning to the dear sunlight and the fair plains of the world, fixed upon Ned their hopes and prayers, and Ned—!

Tom pulled himself together, the horrible fancy of Ned falling, falling, falling, through that hideous depth, and plunging a dashed mass of bones on the hard earth, had almost turned him into a faint—slowly he too raised his eyes again upward, to where a second—it seemed to him an age—before, he had caught the scared stare of dear Ned. He looked and there—was Ned scrambling on the pilot; without a word Tom hauled in the hanging chain, and when its end appeared, he saw a BROKEN LINK. Ned had seen it too—seen it before its treacherous sundering had come, and it was in that awful glance that Tom caught his stare, and the sudden stoppage of his ascent. But quicker than Tom could watch, quicker than the fickle iron could part, Ned had snatched with one hand a stanchion on the pilot and, as the chain left him, twitching and rustling against his body, he hung to the support, and for one minute swayed in the vast gulf, unable to rise.The next, with all the terrified impulse of his good strength, he hauled himself up, and with the disengaged hand seized another rod on the pilot and thus anchored, managed to pull his body up on the floor. He was pretty well done up. The strain had been awful. He felt the cold too, though the exertion had done something to rouse the currents of his blood and, luckily enough, the party had been well provided with food. Once on the pilot Ned felt the danger of succumbing to the reaction. His senses were dizzy, and it was hard for him to think. A mental effort as powerful in its way as the muscular one which had brought him there was necessary, and Ned made it. And then things came easier. He knew the work, and seized the levers.

But where were they? Was New York to the north, south, east or west. In their wild ascent from the storm clouds and the drifting of the ship, direction had been lost, and at the height it was impossible to make out the configuration of the surfaces beneath them. Here was a new perplexity. The propellers kept up their interminable beating, and the aerial boat still fanned its way amid the stars. Ned stopped the machinery, and made some preparations for his own comfort. His friends who had glued their ravished eyes upon him were hailing him faintly, and he could hear Tom’s voice above them;

“Ned throw us a line, and we’ll pack a few things together and your coat, and—Simpson says, turn the ship around—and beat north.”Ned had already consulted the Barometer and found out their elevation—2500 feet. They must drop. He looked around him in the pilot and found an assortment of lines, tools, and fortunately the compass stand, which had been knocked down, with its dial unbroken. The telephone connexion with the car had been ruptured by the fall of the carrying chain, and Ned wondered if he could get a line to the car. It was less easy to call to his companions than it was for them to speak to him. He concluded to spare his breath. Rummaging about he found an air-line-thrower, a small affair modeled after the life saving machines. It did not take long for him to rig this in position and fire a line over the car; Tom caught it, tied it fast, and he and the girls set about making up a parcel of comforting things, among which wine and whisky were not forgotten. It was soon got ready, and waving to Ned—the parties on either side of the air gulf could see each other by reason of the electric lamps—the burden of delights was launched into the space, and Ned pulled it in. Ned got his coat, and after a replenishment that gave him strength and courage, again turned to the serious matter of getting back to New York.

The balloon had fallen some thousand feet, but beneath them nothing but darkness reigned, even the glasses showed nothing, and Ned peered through them a long time. It was warmer and altogether the marooned party felt greatly encouraged. Tom turned his attention to Simpson again, and succeeded in making the poor fellow more comfortable while he got from him his idea as to where they might then be, and how he came to fall out of the pilot.Simpson said that when the squall struck them, he had been blinded by the lightning, and in the flight up through the fitful howling tempest, he had been seized with a chill; after that he knew nothing, till he found himself stunned on the floor of the car; as to where they were he was certain that the balloon had been sheered to sea, and that every minute was taking them over the ocean and away from the city. Ned had turned the vehicle, and was running slowly, watching intently, for the manifestations of life below them, the compass pointed north, and in that direction they were now sailing.

“Oh, I see lights way down;” it was the soft voice of Jessie, and the eyes of her companions followed hers, and there indeed a long string of pin point lights emerged in the night, and they grew stronger, larger, steadier. It was one of the enormous steamers that brought Europe to New York in three days. Ned had already detected the leviathan, and he found out the balloon was sinking. Was it falling in the track of the steamer? Was it falling on it? A second later and the hoarse whistle of the huge barge below them sounded excitedly, then they all could hear voices, orders, cries, a confused tumult of sound, while the hoarse whistle swelled into a fierce revolting protest; its harsh, straining, shivering huskiness seemed to hit the very side of their car, and then more voices rose, volleys of calls; the voyagers could hear the sough of the engines, they caught the smells of smoke and habitation, and then the whole colossus sprang into sight, a wavy broken terrace and pyramid of lights.It was evident that the apparition of the descending aeronauts had started all the activities of the great steamer, while it also became clear that the cabins and berths were being emptied, and that the rumor of the falling balloon had penetrated throughout the vessel, for the storm of exclamations and outcries, not free from shouts of alarm, showed that the floating population below them was all aroused. Then through the commotion and buzz came the heavy calls of the megaphone;

“Hold hard there. Keep on the centre. Fall abaft the stacks. Mind all,” and then succeeded a sound of terrific scrambling, then crash upon crash as the great mass of the balloon bodily drifted and fell on the deck of the steamer. The occupants of the disabled “Chariot of Flight” had not been without danger. As the balloon came down to the deck of the steamer, which had ceased its motion, the car struck the upper works of the vessel first, and, with a slow settling oscillation, rubbed along the decks and finally hung fast. Before this Tom had extricated his charges easily, leaping out of the car first himself, and helping them. Then he succeeded in dragging Simpson over the edge, and with help from the crowding crew of the steamer got him away. Ned had soon jumped to the deck, and none too soon, as the overhanging bulk then descended, thudding the deck, splintering rods, pins, rails, seats, nettings, and cracking with a few subsiding thumps the deck itself. When Ned saw the inevitable collision he had opened the valves, and thus hastened the descent. It also robbed the balloon of all possibility of motion, and so, at last, the long cylinder lay on the upper decks of the steamer, its axis parallel with that of the big liner. Taken altogether the outcome of the affair was a matter of congratulation.The captain took Ned and Tom to his cabin, and heard their story. He was very grave at first, and muttered some remarks about “a piece of damned hoodlumism,” but he softened at length, after learning no extensive damage had been done, and seeing the forlorn state of mind of the poor fellows, who now looked forward to some rasping rebukes, and no little loss of prestige. Tom was a little moody over it, but Ned more philosophically resigned himself, and began calculating the damages. They turned out to be considerable, and this was depressing, and then to Tom the outcome was in a measure disgraceful, and the whole aspect of the diversion might seem reprehensible. They should have returned after the fireworks, and not attempted the long unnecessary trip. It was altogether disagreeable and discomforting. Jessie and Delia felt shamefaced about it, and their position on the steamer was unpleasant. It turned out that the balloonists had gotten far out to seaward, in the line of the incoming steamers, and that they were, when the balloon gave out, heading for Sandy Hook. That morning the steamer entered the harbor, and the strange outlines of the recumbent balloon on its decks was hailed with wonder by the ships, tugs and ferry boats of the bay.

But the early morning breakfasters in the restaurants, and later the sober faced citizens, at their morning meal, found the entertaining tale in the papers, all served up hot and racy, sent before the strangely loaded steamer, by “<i>wireless</i>” to the eager journals. None perused the strange story more wonderingly than the Mayor and Mr. Briggs, and none with the rage and threatening menaces of Poniatowsky and Longtinsky. Some settlements and readjustments were inevitable.“Damnation! I knew it,” howled Poniatowsky into the dismayed face of Longtinsky, “that poodled-faced Ratler has been playing his tricks here. That’s plain. And you’ve been fooled, led by the ear like a stuck pig. Damn it, I’ll kill both of them. Why—”

“Pony you’re crazy. I tell you that the girl has been led into this naturally enough. They went up on the Fourth to see the show, and then they went off for a lark. Not her fault, I know Jessie has been overpersuaded. Don’t lose your wits. Kill Tom Ratler;—yes to—with him—kill the Mayor, kill Helen Lorimer,” and the disfigured face darkened with hate, “they’re all your enemies—mine too—but Jessie is yours, all yours then. Can’t you put two and two together. We’re in this for keeps. The trial comes off in a little more than three months. Don’t spill the mill now. Things are working our way. The Beehive is at work. If we can make our strike before Helen Lorimer gets the turn on us, and stands for more to our people than we do—all a damned bluff, a game of hoodwink—why then—then—we’ll get out of this.”

“Mean it?” and the furious deceived lover seized Longtinsky at his burly neck and gave that discomforted gentleman another sharp fright, after his frequent fashion.

“Mean it; of course I do. You know that. We’re swimming together, Pony, for the same shore, we neither of us can go on alone”—and yet while he said it, the soothing speaker felt that the time must soon come when he could disentangle himself from this tiresome and dangerous alliance; an alliance good enough for his purposes now, but later, FORCIBLY to be dissolved.Longtinsky persuaded—with difficulty—Pony to let him talk alone with Jessie, promising to put a better guard on her goings and comings and this time he was hard and bitter with his daughter. Jessie was genuinely contrite, and her marketings on the west side were summarily stopped, but the love she felt for Tom was as bright as ever, as strong, as sure, and so, with Juliet’s, “as boundless as the sea.”

Tom was himself in some dispraise, and worried with misgivings. Of course there were explanations, and they were accepted, though Tom felt himself resting in a shadow, and it tormented him. He looked eagerly for the time to come when he might redeem himself.

Ned’s employers found a most unexpected source of profit in the misadventure, more than enough to compensate for the damages incurred. This came about through the merited publicity given to Ned’s heroism. The exploit was well told in a dozen different ways, and it so enhanced Ned’s reputation that he became a lion, and the crowds that now resorted to the Zeppelin, whence the unhappy experience began, were almost emulous to take the excursions with so famous and brave an aeronaut. The receipts climbed up, and the happy owners of the balloon and of Ned, forgave him all, made him a feature, and pocketed their swelling revenues, without disparagement, even with many secret self felicitations that it happened as it did.

Delia’s admiration for her beau—the comely Ned—became almost delirious, and as she basked in his reflected glory, she assumed new airs, and, as they too became her, Ned was himself more inextricably entangled in his love,his reputation, and the assured bliss of a hastened marriage.

From it all Tom and Jessie had the most to regret. Jessie’s reprimands had been harsh, and she was now effectively guarded, for the infuriated Poniatowsky took upon himself a careful watch, his moody and sullen spirit hovering with a murderous menace over the least effort of Jessie’s to see her lover. Not indeed that they did not hear from each other, nor that they did not see each other. Their loves became more indomitable and absorbing, it became that “madness most discreet,” which found ways and means that even a Poniatowsky and a Longtinsky could not circumvent or challenge. THE CARDINAL

It was a delightful room. One of those happy and artificial conjunctions of architecture and decoration sufficiently chaste to invite repose, sufficiently curious to awaken inquiry, which a scholar enjoys, and a man of taste envies. It was an elliptical apartment, illuminated from a shaded skylight, with low book-cases on two curving sides, their tops or mantels holding marble busts, and above them on the walls, faintly creamed with rose, oddly framed wood engravings of Albert Durer’s. At one extremity of the room an imposing full length portrait of Pope Sextus X, then reigning, with a <i>priedieu</i> before it, and an enormous candlestick holding a giant taper of red wax; at the other end a remarkable gothic tapestry of the 15th century, enclosed in a gothic frame. It represented “The Visit of the Magi,” its background was a deep peacock blue, sown with tiny crescents. The offering to the Holy Child was made by a venerable man with a long beard, wearing a sleeveless tunic of magnificent old-rose velvet, finished at the arms, neck, and around the edge with pearls and garnets, on white silk. The other attires in the woven picture were rich and the Virgin’s Mother’s of wonderful beauty.

There were six chairs in the room, all different in design, archaic and ponderous, cushioned with embossed leather and bearing the singular decoration of a spread eagle.A square heavily carved center table, on four stout mahogany legs formed of clambering boys on slips of trees whose branches supported the table top, and ended in twigs and leaves, which ran profusely around the sides of the table in a kind of frieze below its scarcely jutting edges, filled the centre of the room. It was in scrupulous order—the files of books upon it being carefully arranged in book racks, and so placed with reference to a leathern framed blotter, at one side of the table, as to suggest their convenient use by any one sitting there.

This place was indeed occupied by Cardinal Fenton—whom we have seen before—and, in his red cassock, with a pectoral cross, a beretta margining his black hair and that observant gaze, leaning back in a chair that surrounded him with griffins and perching imps, and with the brass emblem over him, half suggesting a bird’s flapping consternation, he gave to the room the desirable finish of personal dignity. And before him at the opposite side of the table stood a young man of remarkable beauty.

He was indeed rather beautiful than handsome. His oval face, not too weakly drawn, was crowned with curling jetty hair, his lips closed in a perfect bow, his eyes liquidly glowing, indicated tenderness, and the straight Grecian nose, with the white temples, the delicate modelling of the ears, and the suavity of posture in the lithe, and, a little undersized, figure imparted a half boyish, half feminine impression. Still there were reasons for thinking he was not under puberty and not possessed of power.A moustache covered his upper lip, and as the Cardinal spoke, or when he himself spoke, at moments, the color rose steadily in his cheeks, and his eyes flashed—and then the voice itself was also fully conditioned in age; it was a barytone, eloquent and sustained, with a tendency to deeper notes, not often used but obviously there, if a stronger emphasis was needed.

His scrupulous dress was excellent in its perfect fit, and it was noticeably quiet in color. Indeed in color there was in this interesting figure just one note, a scarf of red silk and a garnet pin.

“Yes,” it was the Cardinal’s voice, “we have reached a period of forcible religious contraction. The Church seems strong, is strong, but only because she is the surviving refuge. The world has decorously repudiated faith.”

“Decorously?” interrogated his listener.

“Yes, I think that suits,” answered the Cardinal gravely, “There is not hatred, though that exists; there is an amused toleration, more pernicious in its effects, more detestable, but absolutely indifferent. De Maistre, who believed in an <i>auto-da-fe</i> and defended the Inquisition, would be curiously confounded to-day. Rather than be burned at the stake, the present up-to-date philosopher would assent, and, under compulsion do all the things the Church asked him to do, and—without a semblance of immodesty—continue to do so with gayety in his heart and a lie on his lips.Not, believe me—” this in remonstrance to an attempted interruption—“that the same philosopher would feel as comfortable doing it as not doing it—not that he would not anathematize the authority, that commanded it—but, doing it to save his skin, feel it was about as important as wiping his feet or lifting his hat, equally a deference to custom. The modern philosopher is pusillanimous under durance; he has not the iron courage of the heretic of six centuries ago.”

“But why bring the Pope to the United States, Father?”

“Why? Look about you. These are the latter days of scripture. I believe the chosen number approaches completion, and religion will soon become abridged to a vanishing quantity and—left with its Science, its Philosophy, and with its Ribaldries of Sense—this old world will be let to go on its way, whither it will, unchecked and uncared for. Religion—I mean the Church—to-day has disappeared in Europe—let it entrench itself, for the short time remaining to it, in this virile country, yet responsive to the ordinances and formulas. Let the Head of the Church be with his church, and not continue the idle function of repeating a stale falsehood in a traditional position, no longer honored, but openly scorned. Reclamation is impossible—The Church can see but one finality, its own speedy departure, lifted up, as it were, as its Sacrament is daily lifted up, into the clouds, into the invisible realm of ideality, to be suddenly returned again with a tyrannous power—with burning fulminations—with sectionizing and filtering tests—with its Christ, to judge and to ordain.”The Cardinal sat erect, and the pale face flushed and the rebuking eyes—those singular and noticeably inquisitive features in the countenances of confessors—flamed, as if with inspiration. His auditor was not however averse to argument, and after a short silence which seemed a respectful acknowledgment of admiration—he spoke in an accent which showed his Italian extraction, but it was pleasing to hear, and detracted in no wise from his polished poise of language and utterance.

“The world is not yet burnt out. Neither as a physical fact nor as a spiritual being. It remains both ways. Is it not a better place to live in? Do we not live better each year? If wickedness thrives so does goodness thrive. I am a Catholic churchman. Yes. But I have common sense, appreciation, sympathy. Because men are not catholic churchmen—let us admit, because they are less and less catholic churchmen, must the world be considered a futility, must it end? If the Church is catholic indeed, I think, it must provide ways and means for those who are not catholic. Will you, Father and Prince of the Church, annihilate the blameless because they do not believe?”

The Cardinal leaned forward with both hands clenched on the blotter in front of him; he seemed to stretch out his neck and the words came, as if deliberately weighed, before they were allowed to pass his lips, they came so slowly.

“Yes; that is the condition; it is hard—but not merciless. Annihilation cannot be regretted, it might be feared. But this civilization does not fear it, therefore to it, annihilation is no penalty. Perhaps catholic theology has passed the point where every human soul must be accounted for.The Church is larval in this world, imaginal elsewhere—and as it grows together the constituents of another transfigured existence in the hurly-burly of a world disowning it, catching, as it can, the materials for its ultimate perfectibility, it must obey at all times its LAW of FORM, the index of its crystallographic symmetry. It knows no compromise, it does not even know what compromise means. IT REMAINS ITSELF.

“Let the world be wise; let Science and Philosophy keep their votaries busy, let it grow more comfortable and more self indulgent, let it banish pain and prolong life—what of it? It means nothing; for such progress is a superficial gloss, the impermanent fashioning of perishable things. Utility is one thing; Immortality is another. The world improves one, the Church absolutely safeguards the other.

“The Church has no quarrel with human improvement, ease of living, bettered political conditions. It knows to-day it has itself been stultified by its own human ignorance—but its fraility, or its meanness, or its cruelty, inhered in its human elements. Its stains cannot obscure its prerogative. Let these ameliorations grow, multiply, and glory and gratitude be to those who bring them, but after all, are they more than the shuffling materialities of the crust? The Church moulds into immaterial permanence the human essence. It has its PROCESS. If the great workman Feil intended to make a lens so gigantic that its light gathering power would bring the Planet Mars to our feet, that lens could be made in but one way. The patented right to make an everlasting soul is the Church’s—let that be the challenge.”There was again a pause—the young man sank into a chair, his eyes half strangely—almost with astonishment—perusing the Cardinal, who had lost the tense attitude, and looked sorrowfully, and yet smiled at him.
