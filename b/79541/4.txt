From our porch a wide bowl of farmland and fields sweeps to the foot of the western mountain—a singular rocky summit that seems a reclined head staring immovably into the sky,—and when the sunset colors flame and burn above it and the bowl of fields and farmhouses, begins to grow purple and russet, in the fading day, and behind in the east, almost as if above our house a reflected sunset repeats the west with tenderer and chaster tints—then it is glorious. Not that exactly either—then the place makes you strong and happy,” and the poor woman bowed her head upon the stone and wept.

The worried embarrassed youth knelt at her side; he could have seized her, held her face to his own with gentle and unyielding hands, and kissed away the drops of her misery, that made his heart ache, and yet brought to him—so his thought ran—assurance of her fondness for him. The handsome man had no foolish vanity, no spoiled self-esteem, and yet he was not unconscious of some merit in a woman’s eye, not altogether unwise as to his power to entertain and help. His visits to the mansion had been encouraged, he had talked with Miss Lorimer upon so many things, which mutually interested them, had become so familiar, even in the short three weeks, or slightly more, of their acquaintance, with some of her fondest hopes, that he felt himself somehow established as her admirer, and he had met no one else—as he recalled—that might dispute that title.

Even as he knelt there, not touching her, indeed with no thought of such a profanation, how odd it was that there suddenly ran in his head Malvolio’s stupid conceit, and the words of the confessed fool:“Why every thing adheres together, that no drachm of a scruple, no scruple of a scruple, no obstacle, no incredulous or unsafe circumstance—What can be said? Nothing that can be can come between me and the full prospect of my hopes.”

Amato sprang to his feet. He felt disgraced with this fulsome self flattery; could it be possible that he followed this new master Love with so ignoble a confidence?

“Pardon me Miss Lorimer,” he said, “if you will let me bring you something; perhaps—”

“No thank you—a little nerves, that’s all. You do not know American women well Mr. Amato. Our cruel climate makes us less stalwart and invariable than your European ladies.”

“It amuses me Miss Lorimer;” Amato cheerfully replied, “that you assume I know anything about European ladies, and the disadvantageous comparison you make, seems to me unfair. Are not ladies much the same everywhere?—I mean as regards sensibility?”

“How very unflattering!” Miss Lorimer was smiling again, and she rose too; “You should not make the mistake to regard us as all cast in the same mould. Would you care to be confounded with all your fellow men by an indistinguishable sameness?”“Don’t be so hard with me. The question was whether American ladies are more susceptible—more sensitive, I mean—than European ladies. I have rather been led to think that while American women are more sympathetic, they are quite as self-possessed.”

“Well we won’t quarrel now about ladies and that life-long difficulty of telling just how much we are superior to our European sisters. You know we intend to keep you at Stowe, and I shall put to the test your strength and patience. I must be out and walk; and your assistance will be much insisted on.

“I shall be surprised if you are not disappointed with our small mountain. Don’t look for a Matterhorn and glaciers. Our pretty country side is hardly dramatic, and the people are not costumed guides, nor are there any titled tourists.”

“Why Miss Lorimer,” answered Amato laughing heartily himself, “one would think that you take me for a foreigner. Have I not been all my life in this country? Is it not mine? Please remember that I was born here. Europe is more strange and unknown to me than it is to you.”

“Well there is the continental air about you somehow. After all it is the blood rather than the atmosphere that makes race. When we say blood what do you mean really?”

“Everything that goes to the making of this body and with it the mind. It cannot be the red corpuscles alone.Blood means temperament and temperament means after all just so much nerve and muscle and flesh and bone, and because all flesh and bone is the same the differences come in the putting of it all together, as you make different patterns with the same thread.”

“Perhaps,” said Miss Lorimer, “it’s something like a difference in air. The chemist tells us the air all over the globe, top and bottom airs, yields to his analysis the same proportion of oxygen and nitrogen, and yet how to our finer sense they vary, and how one air kills, and another revives us,

        _like the sweet south

“Heredity stamps differences in us, and occupation or custom strengthens them,” said Amato.

“Science, if this is science,” answered Helen, “explains very little. You know Amato,” the young man started with this gentle familiarity, “I scorn science. In a bad sense it is the great deodorizer; it expels the perfume of every thing. In its grand role of making things plain, it makes them common. It labels, and classifies, and sticking names on all creation converts this mysterious and lovely world into a huge encyclopædic catalogue. Oh, it is an old quarrel with me;” she bent her head once more, for the thought of her serious arguments with the Mayor returned and grieved her.“It is pretty certain,” said Amato, “that science utterly fails to explain anything fundamentally. Its Laws, so much boasted of, are superficial rules which never go the smallest fraction’s depth into the meaning and nature of things. We are less frightened than once, but we are not the least bit less amazed.”

“That suits me, that suits me,” cried Miss Lorimer, “Ah Mr. Amato for you and me, is it not permitted through our Faith to feel

        _that the spirit
Clothed in its changeless purity, may know
How soonest to accomplish the great end
For which it has its being, and may taste
That peace which in the end all life will share?_”

“Well! the vision grows in these later days, and I believe that Science must be thanked for much. Lucretius said that as boys fear many things in the dark so men in broad daylight have been afraid of the future, and their lives, but that reason will dispel these foolish fears as the sun or the lamp the darkness that scared the boys. It is so.

“We must be duly thankful for the emancipation of this day from mere stupidity.”

Miss Lorimer watched young Amato with an increased interest. She certainly felt that she was admitting his friendship to a more valued place in her esteem.“Oh yes—stupidity is always hateful, when it blots and disfigures thinking. But science in another way is stupid too. Its style of knowing it all, its preposterous agnosticism, the patronizing air of its professors, its denuded common senseness, the literal manner of its assertions, and the blank stare it offers to all poetic meanings—its absorption in names and guesses!

“And indeed what of it? If we think we know the way the world was made are we any better off? We rejoice in some interesting propositions and some men derive a few worldly honors for their sagacity. It employs time and research and indefatigable brains, but suppose it weakens our quality of aspiration, of religious hopes, of contented living, of wondering joy in the mysteries of things, are we the gainers?”

“I believe that science as a whole saturates our present life quite favorably” said Amato, leaning with arms crossed against the parapet and looking towards the southern city. “It advances along so many lines of sane living, and marvellous conveniences! It is not only that we have perhaps a new way of thinking—which you and I may deplore—but with these new ways comes in a flood of inventions which make living safer, longer, more delightful, and give us, for instance, that miraculous light that to-night seems almost a message from Heaven.

“The old latin and grecian philosophers guessed and wondered, and we are told learning was much a littering of thought with systems of whys and wherefores. But nothing certain.To-day if we are as far off as ever from knowing the <i>Altogether</i>, perfectly, we are infinitely nearer seeing the complexity of things, and the adjustments, the checks, the rearrangements, the vast economy, the deliberate purpose, the balance of creation, and so, without guessing, we have an absolute gain in knowledge, and, like visitors to some beautiful garden are IN it, and no longer OUTSIDE of it.”

“But all this increase of knowing does not change your Faith?” queried Helen with animation.

“No,” said Amato “it does not. The habit of belief has been fixed in me. But somehow I think that the area of faith grows less and less. Lake a vanishing surface in a flood it is inundated on all sides. The rock of resistance is our Church, and to that as to a very citadel of safety, those, to whom religion is life, must flee.”

“<i>They must</i>,” rejoined Miss Lorimer. “And Amato” again the young man resisted the impulse to seize the speaker’s hand, and confess his soul to her, there in the quiet night; it almost seemed to him as if she paused, waiting for him to take the offered chance—she continued; “if this vanishing is a fact, is it not the part of wisdom, is it not a supreme duty, to concentrate the army of believers here in this country, where Catholicism wears the cleanest face, is the most pure and radiant, lives up, I think, to the highest standards, and nourishes a brighter flame of devotion?”“I know what you refer to Miss—Lorimer,” said Amato “the bringing of the Pope to this country. Well are we not both agreed about this? The Cardinal told me but the other day that the plan broadens, and grows, that the fathers of the Church are turning their eyes to this country, and believe that in the slow subjection of the Church in Europe, its losing hold upon influential thought, that the Pope should come to America.

“The Cardinal says it would mean a new chapter in the Church’s history—he calls it the Church’s DEMOCRATIZATION, for the Pope in America would simply be a ruling Bishop, though yet the reigning semblance of Christ on earth. Instead of a throned monarch he would here become a citizen, and, while screened within the decent guards of privacy, he would put on the expression of our common equality before the Law. In this country, says the Cardinal, must come the final apotheosis of the Church, from these shores it must rise into the opening firmament of the WORLD TO COME.”

“As if” continued Helen, seizing his thought and finishing it, “the Beatific Vision should culminate on these shores, before

_The cloud-capped towers, the gorgeous palaces
The solemn temples, the great globe itself,
Yea, all which it inherits shall dissolve._”

Helen Lorimer’s face glowed with a strange beauty. Amato could see in the half light—he was so near to her—that her eyes half filled with tears, and the long dreaming gaze taught him the religious depth of her nature, and,as she rose, and offered him her hand, and she came close to him her loveliness shone in his eyes like some transfiguration. She said; “Yes we all shall work for this. What a triumvirate of plotters we shall make. Ah!—Well to-morrow even at about this same hour, you shall see the HEAD at Mansfield watching the skies, waiting like some bound and wearied Prometheus for the day-star of perfect Truth to set him,—and <i>all</i> free.”

“Miss Lorimer,” it was Amato speaking, and as he spoke he slowly dropped her hand, “There are fetters besides Ignorance, and they are bonds which I would not exchange for Liberty.”

Helen Lorimer had passed to the top of the stairs, leading from the tower, and she turned to him, and on her face there rested he thought a smile, as if she understood and wished him well. And now it came to his mind, with an alacrity he wondered at, that through all the time he had enjoyed her acquaintance she had been so grave, at times a great vividness of speech, a rapid interest in things, almost as if seeking a distraction—but always grave, perhaps sad—and now—this keen smile as if joy came to her! It made his own heart leap, and the blushes of delight flamed his cheeks. He felt his pulses quicken, and something choking his throat stopped his voice. He almost ran to the top of the stairway,—but she had vanished. He heard her calling from below:

“Come! Come! We must start early to-morrow. Good-night, good-night!”The next day Julieta, Helen, and Amato, took the White Mountain Express for Vermont. The party attracted some attention at the splendid Grand Central Depot, for Miss Lorimer was known, her mother was yet a most beautiful lady, and the handsome beaming face of Amato set tongues wagging, and did not fail to draw the eyes of ingenuous maidens.

The trip was a new one for Amato, and he noted with studious interest the changing rushing picture, which indeed Helen had commanded him to observe, saying: “See! if we are not almost as good-looking as you in Europe,” a reproach to which the young man vigorously protested. But he obeyed devoutly, and let nothing escape him.

From New Haven the country grew increasingly interesting; the wide fluviatile valley of the Connecticut River, with here and there separated and towering meridional ridges of Triassic sandstone, was very striking, while the testimony of enormous erosion and decay was seen in the clay pits, all the way to Hartford, and beyond to Springfield, and further. From Springfield they shot, on an arrow’s wing, past Holyoke, and Mt. Tom, with its lonely surmounting hotel (still maintained), past Northfield, with Moody’s ancient camp, and then to Bellows Falls, which makes such a bright picture!

From Brattleboro, Windsor, to White River Junction, the charm of the Connecticut was always with them; hundreds of the loveliest scenes were encountered, and the varying phases of the river kept his attention brimming over with pleasure.Charming homes appeared from time to time, opposite pleasing or noble stretches of the river, and Tobacco was a noticeable product, growing with great luxuriance in the river farms.

From White River Junction they travelled to Waterbury. This ride through a long turning and returning valley, quite closely hedged with hills, lanes, and old beaches, was also delightful. The day grew gray and rainy, and much of the beauty of this ride through the lonely cold vales, with the compact little villages was lost, but the wild and frugal life of the people, the keen set look of the country, and the ever present strolling or rapid river were well seen. It was the same as to-day, for that prevalent desertion of the country for the city still prevailed at the beginning of the twenty-first century, indeed the cities grew with startling ease, and the country put on a new forestation, which renewed its ancient woods, and brought back, with strange quickness, its wild animals. One thing alone counteracted this tendency, and that was the increasing pride of the rich in the country. They scoured it over for beautiful sites, and revelled in giving to the broad acres they bought, a ravishing pastoral charm. It was not unnaturally allied to that aristocratic effort of establishment which we have referred to, and which Helen Lorimer so much delighted in.She said herself; “if the rich, the cultured, those who know the arts of living, will root themselves in our country-sides, and spread beauty and elegance of rural grace about, benefitting little communities with the application of their wealth, ennobling natural views and places with expressive architecture, weaving everywhere, like scarfs up hill and down dale, good roads, that will throughout make living safer, easier, and more delightful—if they will do this, they would justify their existence, and they would surely become better Americans. They would truly love their country.”

The movement had grown and distended itself, actuated by a new sudden contagion of enthusiasm and the growth of emulation. It had made whole sections of New England, and parts of the rural New York counties marvels of loveliness. It ransomed from commonplaceness many spots which needed only the touch of an artificial embellishment to become pretty. It created the most fascinating surprises, where ingeniously designed villas on hill top and spur, in valley and by lake side, gave to the landscape a borrowed European air while at a thousand points the nestling colonial homes, like the most delicate visions, sprang to view with positive enchantment.

The most fortunate rivalry had arisen between states, and the rich entered upon the most profitable sort—for the public’s benefit—of competition. They strove to make their states attractive, to give them an unusual beauty, and in this friendly and delightful contest added new incentives to all the occupants of the state to support them.Schools of Artifice—as they were called—came into being, each having for its aim the development of a spirit of aesthetic ambition, and the peculiar use of the features of the state, so as to produce the best picturesque effects. So there had grown up a stronger demand for beauty, and it began an almost despotic sway in the living of men. It influenced the most humble and chastened the most vulgar. In the cities it assumed such grandiose forms as the monumental splendor of Carrara in New York.

It had touched the South, where it was indeed most needed, and the old time slovenliness and dreary hopelessness of board and brick, flatness and indifference, succumbed to a sprightlier fancy. The South had become rich also, and this allowed its owners to resume the old time princeliness of the Virginia colonial style, and so through the devious changing beauty of the Appalachians, from the Potomac to Tallulah, art swept its redeeming hand, and blent its best effects with nature’s unharmed loveliness.

Mr. Theodore Lorimer had everything in readiness, and being something of a manager, besides an almost acrimonious critic of conveniences, the house and grounds of the Lorimers at Stowe were in perfect shape, and the place wore its most fascinating guise.

Mr. Lorimer had suffered, though a selfish arrogant and narrow man, some qualms of self-reproach over his treatment of the Mayor, and his own obstinate concealment of their interview. He really feared his niece, and any explanation to her of his conduct would he knew precipitate a difficult situation. He avoided that.He realized Amato’s helpful influence, and while his dull and sluggish spirit lamented, in a torpid way, Miss Lorimer’s evident suffering, he believed too much in the remedial and—as he thought—easy application of the young man’s gifts.

But his gnawing sense of self-dissatisfaction—an unusual feeling with Mr. Lorimer—sought relief in his own stricter efforts to please and divert her. So he had taken great pains to have all things at the Stowe villa in apple-pie order, and long before the family had expected to leave New York, he had come up to the place, and made the elaborate preparation in flower beds, trimmed outlooks, and cleaned and painted rooms, with a few added pictures, a few new rugs, some jardinieres, a set of new curtains, a dainty new setting of a little veranda tea room, polished floors, some exquisite china for the bed rooms, and a pergola with seats, where the sitters could see the wide vale, dimpled with farm houses, and the peaked ridge of Mount Mansfield. All these things betokened the man’s tenderness for his niece, and the injurious sense of his own perfidy towards her.

He had exerted himself to be agreeable to Amato, and he showed his personal pleasure in the young man’s visits. His encouragement had been pleasantly assisted by Julieta’s address and winning welcome. Amato had been careful not to overstep the bounds of an admiring attention. He had been reticent and simply friendly, but in a hundred ways his love expressed itself less obviously and, because Miss Lorimer herself had shown the restraint of some pre-occupation, he allowed himself only the formal privileges of a friend.His conduct was adroit and delicate in its union of respect and the warmer qualities of devotion. All this had its sensible effect upon Miss Lorimer, yet she remembered the Mayor with an almost unshaken attachment. The first harsh wound had healed, and her pride—perhaps not so truculent and preservative as with less gentle and less distinguished characters—had come to the rescue, as it always does with women who possess it, as a gift from the armory of nature’s prudent kindnesses. In this expectant and refined relation of hope and preparation, of withdrawal and insistence, of sympathy and regret, the matter stood when the Lorimers came to Stowe.

At length an auspicious day late in August dawned, after a throng of thunder showers had swept over the country side, when the ascent of Mount Mansfield could be made, and the clear lucent atmosphere enjoyed, for a perfect view from its rugged summit.

The party which left the villa consisted of Julieta, Helen Lorimer, Theodore Lorimer and Amato. Mr. Lorimer and Amato had at first stipulated to go on horseback, but were overruled, and the party left Stowe in a large carry-all in which Mr. Lorimer stowed away some viands and liquids which he said “would do well enough to help out the resources of the Summit House.”

The carriage bowled along the hard road, over the barricaded bridge above the little stream that crosses the valley, and on across the smiling vale, while Mr. Lorimer detailed the past history of the place, and pointed out the expanding lineaments of the mountain.Stowe, he told them, a hundred and more years ago had been a great resort with a handsome hotel, burned down somewhere about 1895, how it had been deserted and sidetracked by more ambitious places, how a Mrs. Taber had come to the valley enchanted with its beauty, and how her talented son, whose life had been actually prolonged in these salubrious airs, had designed the house the Lorimers then occupied,—so that the house was something more than a hundred years old,—how that the returning sense of the delicious beauty of the spot had brought back to it its old time prestige, and that now to the left and right of them were farms owned by rich proprietors whose houses he pointed out with much assiduity, as they could be discerned rising among groves of trees, or were momentarily seen through openings in the woods.

At last they began, under the very shadow of the range, the long ascent and steadily rose, with many glimpses backwards over the country, the village of Stowe—like a toy hamlet on the horizon—and the far eastern range of Worcester. Finally after many turns they came directly under the lee of the high rocky cliff, which is the nose of the great face of rock, which fancy has formed from the regular upraised pinnacles along this steep and elevated ridge. A last long pull and they were at the doors of the Summit House.

Some of the day yet remained and after a slight refreshment Helen and Amato—the work involved seemed too strenuous for Mr. Lorimer, and Julieta absolutely refused—started up the Nose in a stiff gale that made mutual assistance imperative. Helen likened it to climbing the Pyramids, for the schistose rock was hewed out in terraced steps.From the whistling peak of the Nose they could see the mimic configuration that, in the distance, as seen from the valley looked so much like a countenance. First to the south, the Forehead, and then this beetling perpendicular mass of rock forming the Nose, then the long Upper Lip northward, the Lower Lip—both made by projections of rock somewhat superficially broken up into irregular heaps of fragments—and finally the high upthrown Chin, and the receding throat with an Adam’s Apple, made by a conical elevation.

The day was shutting down, a flying scud of mist speeding over the Chin, and the outlook was discouraging for the morrow. The excited visitors did indeed return to the level of the Summit House, and then, in a rising gale, struggle on to the towering eminence of the Chin, but the clouds closed down, and the dragging tails of mist flung their moist wreaths around them, and effectually drew the curtains of concealment all about them, and so they hurried back over the tilting and pebble strewn upland path.

The next day opened ominously, and Helen, who was awake early to catch the first glimpse of the sun rising over the Worcester hills, was disappointed. She met Amato wandering over the mountain top. They both stopped a little while to talk, commenting on the strangeness of the silence.

“Just silence itself imparts a feeling of grandeur to places,” said Amato, “The banishment of all sound affects one strangely, as if we were no longer in the world.”“I think it is not the silence alone that does so, but that lift from the world that the mountain gives,” answered Helen.

“How unusual the voice sounds too here, as if its lightest tone was megaphoned into a shout.”

“There is a great echo up here further north, where the sounds are redoubled. It is weird enough when a cornet is played softly and the slowly emitted notes chase each other, with others rising to the call, as if a troop of invisible spirits sprang tiptoe from their hidings, and flung themselves upon the man-made note to destroy it, or else, one might think that they were freed voices, liberated by the human call, to rush on forever and forever round the world.”

“You remember Wordsworth’s beautiful lines?” queried Amato.

“_And they would shout
Across the watery vale, and shout again,
Responsive to his call—with quivering peals,
And long halloos, and screams, and echoes loud
Redoubled and redoubled; concourse wild
Of mirth and jocund din._”

“Hardly so, though that is indeed most enchanting. Something more idyllic and classic came to my mind about them. As if these echoes were silenced voices waiting in the crannies and crevices of the rocks for their resurrection, an aerial life to be given them by the human call they were fitted to answer.How harshly science robs us of all such fantasies!”

“And yet science delivers to us too a delightful image,” said Amato “for it permits us to imagine the invisible waves of sound dashed upon these hard rocks to be returned in reduplicated billows just as we watch the refluent crest thrown back from the headland on the sea.”

Helen had risen from the stone boulder, from which she scanned the circling fog wreaths around, and as she turned towards the Summit House a splinter of light seemed to fall at her feet;

“The sun” she cried, “it will break away! We shall have our ramble!” They returned to the House for the breakfast, which the consideration of their host and the watchfulness of Mr. Lorimer had made inviting. The table was set with early asters picked up lower on the mountain side, and scrambling stems of the Bitter-Sweet with the orange cups and red coral arils twined about the centre of the table. The temper of the place was happy and exhilarating, and the company partook of the infectious gayety which seemed to be a part of the pure and sparkling air. For the morning, after the preliminary period of distrust and uncertainty, had cleared, the mist rolled up into clouds, the blue sky appeared, and the direct sunshine struck sharply down, and from the windows the whole ridge, from the Nose to the Chin, appeared in its unbroken extent.

Mr. Theodore Lorimer and Julieta were both in a happy vein, and the isolation of the summit, inwrought with the singular power of mountain tops to elicit a healthy hunger, made the morning meal delightful.“I almost feel as if we ought to hire this Summit House, indemnify the landlord for losses through our monopoly, and make a stay here,” said Mr. Lorimer, “it seems so interesting and unusual, and it is so far away from worry.”

“It feels almost as if we were off the earth in a balloon,” said Julieta.

“Perhaps loose on a whirling Meteor, would suit the case better,” said Amato laughing.

“Yes,” responded Helen with animation. “We are. Just a bit of a world all to ourselves, and near enough to the old one to still see it.”

“A satellite so close to the earth as that Helen will surely return, we shall not be able to keep off long,” said Mr. Lorimer, looking curiously at Amato and his niece, as they sat together on one side of the table.

“But if we do,” continued Amato with a smile on his bright and handsome face, “the thump we make when we come back will furnish the newspapers with some terrible headlines, not to speak of the astonishment Miss Lorimer’s admirers among the reporters will experience to find her on the whisking asteroid.”

“If we should strike New York the Mayor would have no more problems to consider,” said Julieta, and the speech was unfortunate. Even Theodore Lorimer sent a glance of mute protest and rebuke to his sister-in-law. Julieta meant it most innocently; she was one who had realized Helen’s love for the Mayor, and she had noticed with astonishment his absence and Helen’s pre-occupation.But she rather favored Mr. Lorimer’s view, and she had thought the affair—as the phrase goes—was a mistake. Then she was attracted by Amato who had won all by his frankness, and ardor, and gentle reserve, and then—well she had been forgetful.

Amato noticed the sudden shudder that ran over Miss Lorimer, her pained eyes as she raised them to her mother, and in an instant he too discovered the truth. It was an unwelcome discovery, and it may have caused him some anxiety, but he was full of pity, and, with a gay air, the melody of his voice penetrating her ears, and with his eyes turned upon her with admiration, Helen heard him say:

“No, our little ball of rocks and stones and trees with its little population will keep its own course,

<i>Far into silent regions blue and pale</i>

and we shall all become very happy in each other’s company, and finally by some pleasant accident land lightly on some lovely sphere, inhabited by wondrous creatures who will give us their services and show us the beauties of their planet, and make us forget the cruel world, by their fairness and goodness and powers. Would you like that Miss Lorimer?”

The image did please Helen, as if, in some such way, she might enter Heaven, and she smiled, but the next moment she looked grave again, and said:“No—there can be no escape—just now; no running away from our duties. There is so much to be done! Can you think Amato, of any greater recreancy than leaving the Museum?”

“Or our incomes,” muttered Mr. Lorimer.

Julieta felt that she had been inadvertent; it pained her deeply, and she turned their attention to the streaming sunlight: “Show Mr. Amato the Smugglers’ Notch Helen. The day is really beautiful. We might arrange for a luncheon near the Lips where those caves are, while you explore the Chin. The views to-day will be glorious! The Lake and the Adirondacks will be superb!”

“Yes, let us do that. All day in the air, and the splendid pictures—!” exclaimed Helen.

So it was fixed that Helen and Amato should go on ahead to the Chin, while Julieta and Mr. Lorimer would follow more leisurely and prepare with the Host’s assistance a luncheon at the elevations called the Lips, just above two natural caverns that pierced the mountain side lower down. In a few moments Helen and Amato had started on their way over the stony foot-path, broken by rough projections of rock, and showing everywhere small patches of white pebbles dislodged or broken from the thickly crowded veins of quartz, which seamed the micaceous schist. The companions could not restrain their delight, as on the right hand and on the left, their eyes traced the lowlands to the distant mountains, the purpling Adirondacks in the west, the hazy White Mountains in the far east, with the Hogback range just beyond the vale of Stowe.And Lake Champlain!—how incomparable it appeared, rifted with islands. The light flooding this vast domain was broken by shifting shadows of clouds, and the effects of the sheer deep escarpments of rock were bold and strong. The abruptness of the verticality on either side of their tortuous path brought the eyes of the climbers quickly to the lowlands, giving a delightful visual sensation as if suspended above the caparisoned farmlands, the woods, the sinuous waters; and ever and anon lifting their gaze to the Adirondacks they saw the serrated peaks disappearing in a golden rush of sun-haze.

Amato was busy in his mind with the suggestion of the breakfast table, and he wondered as he helped the beautiful woman at his side over the rougher passage of the path, whether it was wise of him to think of speaking his heart to her. He felt the intangible obstacle, and his confidence sank a little as he recalled the mental strength of his rival—a sort of quality that would appeal well to Miss Lorimer. But then many things had encouraged him, and the apparent absence of the Mayor hinted at some disagreement which had come about before he entered the scene, and which had perhaps severed all intercourse between the Mayor and Helen Lorimer. That some rankling hurt remained behind was natural, but his own devotion might justly now, as at an appointed time, rise to allay that pain.

Miss Lorimer was debonair and happy, her spirits rose with the broadening and divine day. She accepted Amato’s assistance with laughing reproaches at his humiliating doubts over her own strength.These had been especially offered, and thankfully used, when, for an instant, they diverted their way down the rocks at the Lips to look into the crevice that was called a cave, and Amato felt his own heart beat faster as he pressed her hands or his face, by the accidents of the scramble, came near her own. They hurried on over the lichen crusted stones, slipping gaily, and feeling the intoxication of the alpine air.

It was bliss to Amato, it certainly was pleasure to Helen Lorimer. They had reached the extreme end of the Chin, and gazed down a precipitous descent to a lower level, where rested a small lake, while beyond rose again another eminence—the Adam’s Apple of this titanic visage. Below them a forest covered amphitheatrical bowl denoted the Smugglers’ Notch, where trees covered the whole lower land, in unbroken expanses—spruce, birch, beech, and oak—and now were tossing their arms in leafy confusion as some nether current of air rushed through them. It almost seemed as if they raised their multitudinous arms in salutation to the high perched humans who wonderingly watched their violent agitation.

When a cloud swept across the sun, and the pinnacled cumuli were beginning to float upward from the horizons, then the sylvan solitude beneath them became dark, and with its foreground of the chased mountain side, and the rough uninhabited rocks, put on an ominous and romantic air. It suggested night prowling expeditions, and hidden dens of the old smugglers. Amato asked about them, and why the name was used for the place.And Helen told him about the early days of the Embargo under Jefferson, when commerce with foreign countries—France and England—was prohibited, in retaliation for the depredations made by both countries upon American ships; and then how business languished, and the desperate citizens crossed the borders along the Canada line, fetching there their products, and how they used the Great Lakes and the long waterway and the wooded shores of Lake Champlain, with its many islands for retreat and harborage.

It was a new and pleasant story for her listener, to whom the woods, they then looked down upon, became silvered in the moonlight, and threaded by dark figures of the border smugglers in their nocturnal trades.

Helen told him that the products of the Champlain region were lumber, pork, brandy, and potash. These found a ready market at Albany and the thriving towns of Fort Edward and Whitehall. But when the Embargo came, the merchants turned to Canada; out of the lumber they made rafts, on the rafts they crowded their liquor, potash, and pork, hoisted the sails, and with the drifting wind floated down over the whole lake, across the line to Canada. Windmill Bay was a favorite place of shipment; sometimes twenty-five rafts went out, taking advantage of the darkness and the south wind. Wagons traversed the old roads to Canada, and these were sometimes closed by the militia; the rafts were stopped or seized, but the trade kept on. A revenue cutter came up the Onion river, southward toward Burlington, and fired on the smugglers but in the encounters the militia were usually unsuccessful. If a raft was captured, the captors were almost sure to be surprised and the raft cut out a few nights later.One night forty men, armed and painted as Indians, surrounded some troops near the boundary, and frightened them into a profound sleep, while a raft with thirty sails and measuring ten acres in surface, floated slowly by into Canadian waters.

“In this notch below us,” said Helen, “the traders hid their stores, and gathered in the nights to begin their smuggling march to Canada, with wheels tied up in rags, and muskets on their shoulders, or else they slipped away from here to the Champlain shores to embark upon the tardy rafts.”

The thought of this early time, with its wildness and aboriginal tinct, pleased Amato. He mused a little over it, and then, almost unbidden by any preparatory thought, he uttered the deep motive that ruled him.

“Perhaps Miss Lorimer,” he began “I can choose no better place than this to tell you”—Helen Lorimer was looking at him with a startled half puzzled stare—“that I love you from the bottom of my heart. I loved you the first day I ever saw you. I need not conceal it, for I am not going to be a nuisance. Let me be your friend at least—but—I shall not long be content with that alone—it would be impossible if my case is hopeless—I will fight my battle somewhere else. Does this grieve you?”

Helen was looking strangely away from him and something drawn and sad filled her face; the eyes were downcast, and she had pressed back her hair from her cheeks, whither it had blown, and in this disarray with a steamer cap in her other hand she became to Amato more bewitching and irresistible.He could not stop.

“Oh please Miss Lorimer, let me stay—let me hope. Such speeches as this you have heard before, but perhaps they have not been conjoined with so much patience—for I will wait, and wait—wait to prove to you how uttermost is my love—wait till you know yourself that my love can be unselfish, strange as that sounds. Think well of me and keep me here.”

“Ah—I do think well of you Rudolph”—Amato leaned towards her, he felt like falling at her feet; most grotesquely he thought that if she had asked him to jump down the ragged splintered cliff at their side he would have, then and there, done it, <i>for her sake</i>. His name from her lips sounded like music; he would prize that name henceforth, because SHE had spoken it.

“—but” she was speaking, “you must not speak of this again—not now—not till I say you may. I will try you. I have had my—pains:” she caught her flushed cheeks in her two hands, as if she would crush back to its hiding the tell-tale blood, “—but you are indeed my friend—you can help me. Yes, Rudolph,” again that sweet familiarity that seemed to close round the young man like a sensible embrace, “I do think well of you—”

And then a queer thing happened. It was one of those sympathetic responses in Nature that appear almost as if an intelligent compositor—or better a composer—set upon the physical face of things the seal of a spiritual and poetic beauty.A twittering cloud of birds surrounded them; they were the chaste little black-poll warblers, beating up from the forest, and, with an impulse of friendliness fearlessly settling on the angular rocks, so that they spread a confused surface of movement about them, and then—even as the two friends forgot their surprise—they rose without sound into the sky like a puff of smoke and went southward.

“The winter comes,” said Helen; she rose and began the slight descent from the cairn-like pinnacle whose escarpment on the north ended in the little tarn. In an instant Amato was at her side, helping her, and when he released her hand upon the flat path homewards he raised it and kissed it, and it was not withdrawn.

The lunch at the Lips was a great success. Amato was in ecstasies. Mr. Lorimer and his sister observed that Helen had brightened, and Helen herself launched out into brave railleries over the foolish exuberance of the feast. It was a handsome and incongruous array. A table cloth pulled over a flat rock made the table, and here garnished with pickles and olives, curries and salted nuts, was spread delicate cuts of cold beef and chicken, with interspersed salads, and potted lobster, with fresh rolls and crisp biscuit, iced tea and lemonade, and then, as a climax of the culinary bravado, the waiters disappeared into the rocks and emerged with an ice-cream freezer and a basket of champagne.

“Oh!” exclaimed Helen, “this is outrageous, monstrous, Uncle! It is pure desecration. Convention against nature! The Oreads will become our besetting Furies.Dew, roots, herbs, and wild honey should form our repast—and all this omnivorous hunger at the very mouth of the sleeping Giant!”

“Let us hope,” said Mr. Lorimer, “that the dear fellow will not wake. He might prefer us to this gastronomic medley. As for honey, here Helen, is some of the best—straight from Condits—and I think the dew, roots, and herbs can be dispensed with; you will certainly forget all about them when you have tasted this dry Mouquin.”

“And I,” spoke up Amato with mock gravity, “will take care of the Oreads; if they venture to disturb us I will exorcise them with a fine latin anathema that the Fathers recommended for Devils.”

It was a festival moment, and the savagery of the isolated ridge blended with the comic contradiction of a Fifth Avenue banquet amused and entertained them all. To Amato it was quite clear that good food—the best—acquired a new charm when the farthest removed from its origins. But their hopes for a continuance of the bright day were doomed to disappointment; the sky became filmed with clouds, that darkened into rain, and beating a retreat the merry company followed by the little retinue of caterers, scampered back to the refuge at the hotel.

Amato did wonders in entertainment. There was an old piano hammered out of all semblance to a musical instrument by the ambitious banging of innumerable strollers, lady performers and sledge fingered bucolics, “who played by ear,” in the parlor of the hotel;and from this Amato extorted enough becoming sound for the accompaniment of his really excellent singing. So the day passed. To Amato it had been one of victory, and when the night shut them in, and after a few games, all had retired, Amato alone sprang out upon the porch and walked up and down in the intervals of the showers, smoking, and thinking, and planning. Life had never seemed so desirable, so promising, and after midnight a stray fox, engaged in a reconnaissance of the chicken fold, stopped a moment outside in the wet boulders, to notice the perambulating spark that ceaselessly moved to and fro against the dripping-clapboards of the Summit House. To Amato his mission of love had become deepened with a new meaning; he cherished it more than ever, since he knew it might mean a <i>Mission of Mercy</i> too.<b>CHAPTER XII</b>

THE TRIAL

November had come and the day for the great Trial had come with it. Events had been moving rapidly, and political and social forces concurred to gather the citizens of the City-State into two opposing parties. Within one of them resided the aristocratic principles taught by Helen Lorimer, and within the other—though far more obscurely—the disorganizing tendencies of The Beehive. Between these formidable elements of political disturbance and political prestige the Mayor directed his course with consummate skill. The man’s enormous power of concentration, his impetuous activity, became the marvel of the commonwealth. This ambitious scholar and orator, touched with a utopian idealism, which Briggs had thought betokened at the outset simply weakness, and mental inertia—a view which the Mayor’s conduct at first quite appropriately justified—had amazed the politicians, with his swift recoil from all their shifty opportunism.

He had introduced important reforms, he had established new standards of proficiency in the city’s business, he had deliberately approached his constituents of all shades of opinion, and he had not hesitated to declare himself the exponent of a vast utilitarianism, which aimed at the perfect government of the City, and the realization within its borders of universal happiness. He had been referred to by some wag as the City’s Augustus, who was engaged in the Sisyphean task of reconciling irreconcilable elements, and building a permanent commonwealth upon the treacherous sands of caprice, selfishness, pride, extravagance, and envy.They said the Mayor was trying to maintain the traditional State—a well ordered union of all forces, conditions, and occupations—whereas the State oscillated between two opposite inclinations, one making for an oligarchical rule of wealth, and family, the other for a proletariat of a levelling demagoguery. They pointed to the fact that the Mayor encouraged the ceremonials of the Holidays, that he officiated at the laying of corner stones, he affected a public ostentation; his frequent allocutions to the people were in the nature of lectures, and his endorsement of all plans of public improvement, his familiar frequentation of the poorer section of the City, his undisguised appeal to the rich for support, all these things pointed to his realization of a conflict which only his multifarious sympathies for the present concealed. And they were right.

The Mayor was the visible bond of a provisional peace. His method, as these ingenious and learned critics pointed out, was the Augustinian <i>pax Romana</i>, by which he attempted as far as possible to reconcile divergent interests, and, in a process of universal stimulation, ministering to the municipal pride, to hide the increasing mobilization of the forces of conservatism and radicalism in opposite camps. But these forces daily grew more compact, and busily engaged their respective resources—in papers, magazine writers, public speakers—to illustrate and defend their theories. It was felt by the more thoughtful that society stood upon the verge of conflict, which might mean several things. Two prominently emerged. A civil state on the one hand constituted upon a stricter enforcement of the individual rights of property, and on the other some yet indefinable new social organism which subjected individual rights to legislative revision, and in all probability to legislative annulment.The situation had become interesting, and below the mere academic surface of affairs burned the fires of a temperamental hostility, that would not be content with a peaceful solution.

The Mayor—smarting with an inner pain he confessed to no one, as indeed he had no one to confess it to—became involved in a propaganda of mutual toleration, and worked upon an Aristotelian basis of a state utilizing all tendencies in the preservation of a fabric of checks and balances, with mutual toleration. The sting of his unmerited repulse by Mr. Lorimer, might have easily led him into reprisals and retaliation. It might naturally have turned his thoughts towards the creation of conditions bearing more heavily upon the rich—the Carrara group of proud conservatives but it did nothing of the sort. The man preserved a perfect mental serenity, and weighed with even more scrupulous care the exact proportions of his sympathies with either faction, hoping to develop a third and balancing power, which would control the electorate.

His public acts were marked with a fine regard for justice, his innovations in the financial government of the city were well considered, and his appointments critically excellent. Not a trace of selfishness could be detected in his work, not the slightest yielding to corrupt influences, not the most tenuous showing of political craft. And this in no wise jeopardized his reputation among the very classes who suffered rebuff at his hands. For it was too well understood, as Mr. Briggs told Tom Ratler, that the Mayor was a compromise candidate at the last election, and had himself emphasized the independent attitude he was entitled to bear towards all parties.All this had won him praise from the moderates and sensible, but it had also brought him into a less enviable estimate with the rancorous and radical of both sides.

Had he been a weak man, intellectually, this even course might have assumed the expression of timidity, or simply a puritanical notionalism that, by some kind of exact partition of its smiles to either quarter, hoped to conciliate all. Not so with the Mayor. His strength was unmistakable, and his language tremendously forcible and correct. No one knew that his ingenious and convincing language, his incessant anxiety and explanatory emphasis, the vigorous novelty of his addresses and Bulletins, had their source of power in the smarting and bruised heart of the man, for, as will occur with such temperaments, where a pride of despotic power, and at the same time a pride of jealous apprehension, the pride of an unusual mind associated with a personal history of struggle and self-denial, has been deeply wounded, the energies of the mind storm some new citadel of difficulty, and minister distraction. Such consolation is indeed temporary, but it suffices to allay the worst aches, and if the effort brings praise and distinction, smothers the fires of fever and desire.

In an article of much length which had appeared in the numbers of one of the best magazines, the Mayor had anonymously discussed the situation. The article attracted attention, and was made the text of editorials and sermons, of conversation and drawing room talk, and every one knew that its author was the Mayor. In it the Mayor had aired his studies of Roman History—a favorite topic with him—relying as he did upon such pictures of the first days of the Empire as Ferrero has given us. In a pregnant paragraph he depicted the whole dilemma.“The predicament of the ancient Roman State at the time of Augustus, if we accept the delineation of Ferrero, was the supineness of the governing classes, their reluctance to exercise the functions of Rule, their intolerable surrender to indulgence, to the depravity of a sensual and oriental luxuriance, and the dilettantism of literary tastes. With Rome at that time engaged in a hopeless task to revive an ancient decorum, and the stolidity of an antique moral forcefulness, the problem of Augustus seems to have been how to distribute the burdens of government, and save himself from the arduous responsibility of “running the machine” alone.

“At the top was a nobility dissipated, selfish, and forgetful, which had lost the manhood of its former state, and now revelled in glorious depravities which an eastern civilization had introduced, and made irresistible; next to it was a knighthood class interested in attaining wealth, in successful business enterprises, in the exploitation of the resources of the vast empire, and also too willing to surrender the burden of government to any one—and Augustus had grown in their eyes semi-divine—who would conduct it, so as to least disturb their enterprises of thrift and emolument. Below them came the masses of retainers, freemen, slaves, veterans, small merchants, and artificers, professional loafers, and impecunious litigants, who looked to the government and the richer guilds for entertainment and support, for gladiatorial shows and bread.“Our present condition is rather one of Social War, because all wish to govern, and the political prowess of individuals was never more imposing. There is here no disintegration of Ambition. Our danger is not one from surrendered rights, but rather from the clamorous claim for more rights, from the importunity of too many doctors.

“Society sensibly oscillates between two possible extremes, and the old adage that government is Compromise, forms, with this City-State, the <i>ideal programme</i>, under the control of sense, toleration, and accommodation. Our vitality is enormous. Our talent unlimited, our wealth boundless, the freshness and vigor of enterprise unimpaired, our mental activity exaggeratedly quick and responsive. There is no general lethargy here, though we enlist every element of pleasure. The elective franchise was never more greedily enjoyed, never more desperately believed in and used. We have become <i>doctrinaire</i>, but the philosopher carries his shillalah and his gun. Theories have become creeds, and there are martyrs to die for them. THE STATE MIGHT BECOME DISRUPTED THROUGH WAR, IT WILL NEVER DISAPPEAR THROUGH INANITION.”

The crystallizing process continued, and the two parties confronted each other, the Conservatives—called The Plum-Puddings—and the Radicals—called The Levellers—and the event of some determined strikes had more sharply defined them. Force had been vigorously used to suppress the disorders, that had arisen in their wake and The Beehive, anxious to breed a deeper feeling of distrust, willing to start into action the most wicked and dangerous characters upon some crusade of retaliation and violence, had seized the chance to sow the seed of discontent and Revolution.The Mayor had been thoroughly straightforward and insistent He had made it clear that street disorder must cease, he had denounced The Beehive, and threatened it with suppression; he had summoned a mass meeting of the temperate people and in a great speech had reiterated his control over the best elements, and effected a complete suspension of criticism. The Mayor loomed larger. His hand worked with masterly precision, and he was found at every point able and alert. The bigots—and Mr. Lorimer was one of them—remained silent in company, when his praises were sounded, through behind closed doors, and in each others’ company, they denounced him. The radicals feared him, and Longtinsky and his gang cursed him.

Mr. Briggs and Tom Ratler longed for the exile and forcible expulsion of Longtinsky. They kept up a close watch over The Beehive, and Briggs, in private, gave Tom long winded bodings of disaster, if the sheet was not soon stifled. He indulged—so far as the Mayor would let him—in ornamented appeals, but the Mayor discerned the mistake of interference just at the moment, and The Beehive continued its polluting course, though hidden from public view. The Mayor was right. The Beehive frenzied a few, and made them murderers at heart—as it turned out murderers in fact—but it had not extended influence, and its vicious constituency only seemed formidable, because it was hypothetically involved with a much less violent Socialism.The trial of the incendiaries, or the suspects, in some way focused the attention of all parties and factions. What with the applause of the Socialists over the re-trial, and the promised liberation of innocent men, the cries of protest put up by such extremists as Mr. Livingston, the intervention of the Mayor, and the known interest of Miss Lorimer in the matter, the trial attained a magnified importance.

It acted as a call for truce, and all agreed to watch its developments before they organized for a further campaign of assault and abuse. It became a strategic point, and might have been construed as a pivotal opportunity for one or the other side to score a victory. In reality it had no important relation to the political embarrassment. And this the Mayor knew. But it was distinctly distracting. It was an exhibition of magnanimity on the part of a representative aristocratic family—the Lorimers—and it was at the same time denounced by many of the Plum-Pudding complexion as a ridiculous tampering with the proper course of the Law, and a sop to discontent.

As a matter of fact its influence was favorable to the Plum-Puddings, and Miss Lorimer became a popular illustration of goodness. It disconcerted Longtinsky and Poniatowsky, and made them rave with hate. It gave Longtinsky some anxiety for the preservation of his own neck, and as his designs upon Helen Lorimer had suffered discomfiture, he was beside himself with fear lest the unjustly condemned men—professedly his confederates—would, in the case of their liberation, turn upon him and rend him.He consulted much with Poniatowsky who had become involved more closely with him, and voluntarily—for Jessie’s sake—embarked in his perilous ventures. Longtinsky had foreseen Poni’s capitulation, and he used him unscrupulously, as a tool, which he fully intended to reject and destroy at the right moment.

Longtinsky was terribly deceived as to the strength of his sympathizers, but in his delusion he concocted the outrageous and impossible scheme of a Massacre of the Rich. He planned it for the day or week after the completion of the trial. Then two things might happen. If it succeeded he would rush forward and forcibly establish a government, or somehow, in the tumult, secure a place in the new order of things; if it did not succeed he would flee, and—if Poniatowsky followed him—he would KILL him. So too in the subterranean camp of The Beehive and in the contorted depths of Longtinsky’s brain the trial became a datum point and a cause of action.

Helen Lorimer had interested Amato in the trial, and in the small child, Shimura, who was to upset the state’s contention. During the summer Shimura had been kept at the secluded and beautiful home of Mr. Moser in Maine, on the shores of Penobscot Bay. Here the deformed and strange waif, with his precocious sympathies, and his eager mind grew stronger, and his meagre and weird inconsistent body assumed a better aspect. Amato loved to talk with this boy and seemed to enjoy the curious psychological problem he presented. It was a most genuine interest and it affected Helen deeply; she recognized it and felt grateful for it.The trial court was in the New Court and Government Building upon Centre and Chatham Street, in a room of princely proportions, and handsomely decorated, after the style then prevalent of mural embellishments. The selection of the jury had been a long and wearisome process, for it was difficult to find twelve men sufficiently free of prepossessions to serve the purposes of the trial.

At last that preliminary was completed, and the trial began. Helen Lorimer had at first hesitated to attend the sessions of the court, for she believed that the Mayor might be also an occasional or even frequent visitor to the trial, and, as no word had passed between them since his letter of rejection, she dreaded to again meet him. She had indeed not been unobservant of his actions and words, and she secretly admired them, but his continued silence had angered her, and slowly—very slowly—she effaced the dear impression he had made, turning more and more willingly to the handsome creature at her side, who attached himself to her daily life by innumerable attentions, with a modest unwearying devotion, where too she saw the visible notes of passion.

Her apprehensions were suddenly removed by a public announcement in the public prints that the Mayor during the weeks or days of the trial would take a vacation in the far West, where, it was also reported he would be engaged in preparing his annual message to the Legislature—a report currently believed to be a personal vindication of his views as to the value of a purely material and utilitarian interpretation of government—an anonymous writer in one of the dailies had said that the Mayor believed in <i>Euthanasia</i> for all incurables, and death for the hopelessly insane.This retirement of the Mayor permitted Helen to attend the trial, and with her went Amato. The scene presented by the court was picturesque in the extreme and the movement of the trial dramatic. A marble canopy covered the chair of the presiding judge, a marble platform supported the desk of dark wood before him, on either side along the walls extended paintings of the course of Justice, from the delivery of the Ten Commandments, to the ratification of the Constitution of the United States, meeting at length, as they encompassed the sides of the great room, in a symbolic <i>alto relievo</i> statue of Justice herself, holding her balanced scales, with bandaged eyes, and her symmetrical sword directly opposite to the seat of the Judge, who thus might be constantly reminded of his duty and his power.

A fence of bronze enclosed the customary area in front of the judge, for the actors in the judicial play, filled with tables and chairs, and a raised marble and cushioned seat to the right formed the unusual rostrum for the witnesses. Behind all these details of necessary formal construction the court room held an amphitheatrical arrangement of perfectly white chairs, upon which the spectators were permitted to arrange themselves in still further contributory scenic groups. The color effect as might be imagined, was at least extraordinary; the illuminated walls, the white throne of the judge, the black wood of the desks and tables before him and the clustered white seats of the auditorium, with the carelessly composed but effective groups of the lawyers and clerks, and the crowded audience, in which mingled upon terms, at least, of physical contact, the elements and representatives of the divided political cohorts, a heterogeneous assemblage of faces and costumes, instilled throughout with expectancy and excitement.Among them all were not a few of the very poor, friends of the prisoners, and not a few dark and scowling faces, emissaries of The Beehive. Longtinsky was never seen at the trial, though Poniatowsky was.

One of the opening dramatic moments was the entering of the prisoners, the six men previously condemned to death; Dalzell, Vinta, Bingo, Fabrizio, Polinck, Rinker. Dalzell was an Englishman, Vinta, Fabrizio, and Polinck Italians, Bingo and Rinker born in America of Swedish parents. The six men filed in under a heavy guard, and took their seats within a railing to the left of the court room. Every face in the crushed space looked at them, and this stare they met in very different ways. Dalzell, Rinker, and Bingo smiled feebly, but appeared unconcerned; the Italians were less assured, and shrank together almost as if they resented the effrontery of this exposure. The moment passed, the names of the prisoners were called, and responded to, and the gavel of the judge fell as the prosecution promptly rose to open the case.

Among those who had secured the privilege to a seat within the rail were Mr. Briggs and Tom Ratler, the former as a <i>quasi</i> representative of the Mayor, and the latter as his companion. The eyes of these two searched the prisoners eagerly for Polinck. Although to Mr. Briggs the re-trial was nonsense, yet at least he felt that Polinck was the arch conspirator, next to Longtinsky, and the ring leader of the men captured. Polinck was perhaps the largest and strongest of all the prisoners. For an Italian he was of unusual height and stoutly composed.His dark face punctured by two flashing eyes and further intensified in its unpromising expression of villainy by a heavy and curled black moustache would have readily attracted attention, and almost the most indifferent scrutiny would have recoiled from its hard favored and sinister glance. Singularly for an Italian the hair was not profuse or abundant, and actually carried a few white threads among its black excess of rather thin locks, which, seen from the back, confessed an incipient baldness. This scantiness of hair gave the dreaded Polinck a curiously incongruous mien, which might have at first provoked amusement, had not any such inclination been quickly suppressed when he raised his malignant eyes. Tom Ratler studied his face with interest, and besides Tom in the back of the court room another student of physiognomy was engaged in the same duty. It was Poniatowsky. He regarded Polinck as a rival for the favor of Jessie, and indeed Polinck—in better days for both of them—had told him he intended to marry Jessie. It was not altogether to Poni’s liking that the huge Italian should return to the pursuit of his courtship, though Poni grimly smiled at the unlikelihood of his meeting with much encouragement, smiled even more grimly at the likelihood of his leaving this world altogether.

Even more interesting to Mr. Briggs than the prisoners were the members of the Jury.

On account of the political antagonism among men in the great city, and the assumed importance of the trial in strengthening their affiliations with one or the other parties, as there should be an acquittal or another conviction, the counsel for the prosecution and for the defense had strained every effort to exclude from the jury those who sympathized with the conservatives,as well as those who confessed their radical prejudices. The result had been only to fill the box with twelve men, who professed complete impartiality, and a satisfactory confidence to judge the evidence uninfluenced by any political sympathies. Mr. Briggs studied their faces with anxious scrutiny, and secured for his pains a general impression of intelligence, unrelieved by any distinction, and absolutely free of personal idiosyncrasies, which was a point in their favor. Helen Lorimer on the front row of the white chairs, reserved for the auditors, was no less interested, and her examination produced a similar impression of safety, though her wishes in regard to the trial were the reverse of those of the irreconcilable secretary.

Miss Lorimer and her companion attracted much attention, and their good looks won for them approving comments. A good many women and not a few men rejoiced to see the famous patroness of the poor, and of some of the culprits now being tried, and the no less famous propagandist of the philosophy of caste. It was naturally assumed that the handsome man who accompanied her was to be her future husband, though on that score the suspicions were rather whispered than spoken aloud. The excitement of the occasion and her own deep confession of solicitude gave to Miss Lorimer great animation, and added not a little to her radiance and beauty.

Indeed the lawyers for the prosecution would have willingly excluded her, for they felt her influence prejudicial to their case. They knew that it was largely through her efforts that the new testimony had been procured, and they acknowledged the power she exerted throughout the community by her unquestioned talent, goodness, and activity.They dreaded the effect upon the admiring and forgetful jurors. They had struggled with some legal subterfuges to secure her expulsion, but it was a forlorn attempt, and doomed to failure. The trial began, and Miss Lorimer remained an observant listener. And Amato was no less interested. The trial was a novelty for both, and its details with their dramatic touches, the skilled conflict of the lawyers, the dignity and impassiveness of the judge, and the manifold coloring of humanity in it all, even its dull processes and wearisome tracts of routine, attracted them. From first to last they remained absorbed auditors and listeners.

Mr. Meldexter, the District attorney for New York County, made the opening address to the Jury. He depicted in words of courteous rebuke the fresh attempt to release the condemned after a fair trial by their equals, and then, warming up to the subject, with withering scorn foretold its failure, affirming, in an eloquent conclusion, that the course of Justice could not be swerved by any subterfuges of interest, or any inventions of pity. His address gave Mr. Briggs the keenest satisfaction, and his nudges of Tom Ratler punctuated the telling points of the oration. Miss Lorimer heard it with incredulity and admiration. She knew Mr. Meldexter was quite unprepared for the sort of evidence Mr. Moser would offer, and his soaring tirade gave her no alarm.

The evidence against the accused followed. It consisted for the most part, as far as it was vital, of the testimony of the police, with some additional proofs of the criminal character of the prisoners.The principal point made, was the arrest of the prisoners among a crowd of others, who escaped, rushing from the burning building at the very moment when the explosions occurred which destroyed life, and wrecked the Settlement House, that quickly caught fire and disappeared in smoke and flames. This was followed by various identifications of the prisoners by witnesses, who offered their opinions and experiences with them (the prisoners) as raising a presumption that they (the prisoners) were undesirable citizens. The examination of one of these, unexpectedly furnished amusement to the court room, and threw even a shade of diversion over the sombre faces of the accused men.

The witness was an old woman who carried on her physiognomy the most complicated record of time’s effects, in a wilderness of wrinkles, and who kept one—it was apparently all she possessed—elongated incisor always in view, overlapping her lower lip, like the fang of a dog constantly on guard. She spoke in a voice which if not seriously damaged by time in its loudness, had been entirely robbed of any of its former sweetness. Her little eyes retired into dull browned and stained sockets, and from those hiding places kept a weirdly incessant stare at the examiner, twitching now and then convulsively, or closing completely as if they sought a moment’s respite from so much publicity. Her skinny and pallid fingers were clasped together over the top of an umbrella, whose distended sides and numerous patches betokened a contemporaneous age with its owner.

When asked her name and age she confessed to the appellation of <i>Emily Pint</i>, and with reference to her age snapped out that; “it didn’t consarn ennyone, and enny how was something she knowed nuthin’ about.”The examiner then directed her to raise her eyes and inspect the prisoners, and tell such things as she veraciously might affirm of their antecedents, and reputation.

The old woman turned round slowly and fixed her unwinking eyes upon the men. She remained this way silent and observing for a few minutes much to the astonishment of the lawyer who exhibited some annoyance, not unaccompanied with perplexity and who finally allowed his impatience to frame the indiscreet question, “whether she was ready to answer?”

“Why—yees—I am, purviding you don’t ask me too much.” “Madame I intend to ask you all that is required for the proper instruction of the Jury,” retorted the lawyer with dignity.

“Well—well—don’t git peevish. I’ll tell ye whativer I know but I suppose you don’t want me to tell you what I don’t know.”

An audible titter met this sally, and was rebuked by the court, who warned the witness that frivolity would be sternly punished; an allocution which brought the lean visage of the old woman sharply round in the direction of the judge and put on it an expression of such whimsical perturbation, that the previous titter threatened to become outright laughter.

“Tell the Jury if you know the prisoners or any one of them.”“I know ’em all, and a bad bunch too Mister,” almost shouted the witness, whose excitement carried her along in a torrent of abuse; “They come one night inter my store and slammed round outrageous; they pit me inter an empty barrel, and tied me ’ands and ’elped ’emselves to tobbaccy and pipes and amost enny thing they could lay their ’ands on; they is a good-fer nuthin’ loafin’, thievin’, snickerin’ lot of bums—and thet’s the gospel truth.”

“And they left no recompense to you for their abstractions?” asked the cross examiner.

“I’m a leetle deef” squealed the old woman with a disengaged hand to her ear. “Bit ef you mean by <i>tractions</i> the stuff they took off why—No—they jist robbed pure and simple, and I niver got out ef thet barrel till the nex day—the dirty trash dey are—and me a respectable ooman as must earn her way in the warld—Ah, Ha!” in her excitement she had risen from the marble witness bench, and was shaking her umbrella above her head at the amazed prisoners, while the amusement in the audience developed dangerously, and the judge was rapping for quiet, and the assistant sheriffs were hurrying in the direction of the irate female, who still shouted; “Ah-Ha! I’s got yees now mee honey-birds, and old Emily Pint will mek yees holler as ye did me, ye black skinned unrighteous varmints. Wen ye pit me in the barrel ye askt me ef I could preach a sarmon. Weel—I just guess ye don’t care for this sart of a sarmon—do ye—me jolly chucks.Is it more ye want me to say?—” and the frenzied woman, quite beside herself with the malice of revenge turned to the astounded judge, flourishing her umbrella in the air, while the energy of her action tumbled the ancient head-gear which shielded her frowzy locks down her back, and left her gasping with her minute eyes shining with the baleful flicker of a crazy joy.

Order was restored, and the exhausted witness sank back in the seat; the judge sternly inquired if the witness was necessary, and, being assured that the prosecution regretted the episode, turned to Mr. Moser, and requested him to cross-examine if he thought it expedient. Mr. Moser rose and approached the still panting woman and quietly—as if from some casual curiosity—asked her if she could say how many men were in the party that perpetrated this outrage.

“How many?” queried Emily Pint, “dere was tree of dem, one as stopped the door, another held me in de barrel—de slab-faced scab—and the ither swiped mee trade—” her excitement returned, and Mr. Moser waved to the clerks who led her away, while he audibly remarked that there happened to be six prisoners—a fact already impressed on the audience, who in various states of fluttering comment, had again leaned back in their chairs, and waited, with renewed animation, for the further progress of the case.

The prosecution without calling Longtinsky, had at the instigation of Mr. Briggs, undertaken to show the acquaintance of the prisoners with <i>him</i>, and the influence upon them of his paper, The Beehive. This was done by a number of witnesses, whose testimony was not even questioned by the defence. At last the prosecution rested, and Mr. Moser addressing the court, opened the defence.He admitted the heinous nature of the outrage committed, its dangerous effects, the necessity for its summary punishment, but he insisted that there could be but one opinion as to the further necessity of punishing the real miscreants, and that Justice would only fail in her mission if the innocent suffered, and the guilty escaped; that Society could not afford to blindly look for victims for her revenge—much as Emily Pint had done—without discriminating between its real foes and superstitious culprits. He alluded to the fact that the previous trial had been held under the immediate influence of great excitement, when the startled city, blinded by its apprehension of some menacing convulsion sought too quickly the condemnation of any one that might satisfy its natural but unreasonable thirst for retaliation. The defence, he said, engaged itself to show that three of the prisoners were absolutely innocent, two doubtfully concerned in the outrage, while it freed itself absolutely from any contract to limit the amount of guilt properly to be affixed to Lorenzo Albertino Marchesa Polinck.

This announcement was heard with very different sensations in all parts of the court room, and it was received everywhere with interest, and for the most part, among those not personally concerned in the outcome of the trial, with approval. Popular sentiment had already changed, and the new aspect put upon the case received deliberate and rational attention throughout the city. It was not acceptable to Mr. Briggs, it left Tom Ratler dubious. The sudden concentration of guilt upon Polinck brought the strangest sort of grin to the face of Poniatowsky, while to the excepted man himself it summoned a tide of color that darkened his swarthy cheeks, and spread in encircling rings about his bull-sinewed neck.Helen Lorimer and Amato lost no word of the address, and to Miss Lorimer the hope of saving the unfortunate men—deluded, deceived, duped, and misused, grew stronger. Mr. Moser continued.

He said he would introduce a new name in the register of the accused, the name of a man whom it would be necessary to subpoena to the trial, one whom—after he had finished his work—he believed the industrious and learned District Attorney would find it incumbent upon him to place where he now saw the six prisoners at the bar, and that man was Peter Tolstoy Longtinsky. There was a perceptible stir in the audience. It began at a number of points, and it consisted of a variety of movements. Some people suddenly looked up, others shifted their positions, and whispered to their neighbors, some glared and seemed uneasy, many smiled and shook their heads, with a “I told you so,” expression, and two men got up and left the court house. One of these was Mr. Briggs, the other was Poniatowsky. Mr. Briggs went out to take precautions that Longtinsky should be placed under surveillance at once, and that his chance of escaping from the city should be prevented; Poniatowsky went out to tell Longtinsky of the new threat and to make new plans.

Longtinsky had kept his eye and ear—so to speak—on the trial. Poni had told him of every move, and many others too. He knew the danger that filled the air for him, and yet he did not stir. Riveted by a strange infatuation that his destiny was upon the eve of blossoming into the powers of control that he dreamed of, and which seethed through his dusky brain, and ran before him in beckoning panoramas of riches and eminence, he refused to flee the city, though each day was to make it more and more clear that his safety was elsewhere.And now this deliberate assault by name; the certainty of his arrest, and possible trial, if not as a principal as an instigator, a conspirator, would almost surely follow.

No one suspected the new evidence, and it might be too damaging. Longtinsky and Poni knew the real state of the case, and to Poni it was a fixed fact that Longtinsky would be tried for the crime. He knew his guilt, Longtinsky had confessed that. Poni held Longtinsky in no great esteem, but they had become intertwined in a plot for gain, for revenge. Poni would have liked to come into close quarters with Tom Ratler, and Longtinsky thirsted for the death of Helen Lorimer. Poni felt no too great confidence in Longtinsky’s loyalty, but at the minute he felt his life was essential for his own plans. So he hurried from the court room with the announcement of Mr. Moser in his ears, just as Mr. Briggs made his way to the police headquarters to direct the close watch of the editor of The Beehive preliminary to his subpoena and arrest.

Mr. Moser had finished his speech, and deferred the offering of his evidence, just as Poniatowsky entered the small room—editorial chambers and bed-room—where the ponderous editor was buried deep in the composition of a great leader. He burst impatiently into the room; “Come put up your ink and pens—the long road for you. Tinsky you’re a pinched man, Moser says <i>you did it</i>,” he cried. Longtinsky raised his head and a terrified look covered his face, but he overcame it, and twisting his chair around, he faced the excited Italian, and told him to take a seat and tell all he knew.Poni not only told all he knew, but he added much more he did not know. He drew in the most alarming outlines the probable course of events, stopping at nothing that might unsettle the—to him—inexplicable inertia of the man before him. And yet in spite of his horrible forebodings, in which with sundry realistic gestures he tried to make the anarchist understand the unpleasant aspects of hanging, Longtinsky remained unmoved. His face did show momentary terror, but it vanished the instant he let his imagination dwell on the magnificent insurrection his irresistible appeals would bring about.

Suddenly he jumped to his feet, his ungainly figure making the effort more surprising; his misshapen mouth shaped itself into a strange smile, his little eyes glittered and his portentous ears seemed almost to extend themselves, as he seized the astonished informer by the shoulders, looked straight down upon him, and exclaimed;

“Poni, I will not leave. <i>Never.</i> Let them take me. It will be the sign for the great revolt. Already three bands of the faithful are ready to rise. One word and the new Era starts. Be ready. They can prove nothing. I will add this to the appeal here,” and he slapped his broad hand down upon the loose leaves of paper which bore his weekly message of discontent and upbraidings and exhortation, “Let it come, the Torch, the Dagger—but finally <i>Victory</i>.”

Poniatowsky stormed to his feet, maddened beyond endurance by this folly; the obsession of the man stupified him at times, he had been growing worse, Poni wondered if he was entirely sane;“Damnation,” he cried, “Tinsky you’re a doomed fool. If you can’t see the way out of this I can. I’ll not pull a rope tighter. Jessie and I get out of this. Where’s the girl?”

Again the plotting mood returned to the editor, and his stalking craft seized the opportunity to fasten the brigand before him to his purpose and plots. A deep malevolence beguiled him; neither he nor Poni nor Jessie nor any one should leave the city. The hate he bore Helen Lorimer rose and stifled sense. At any rate, first her death, and—then—perhaps—<i>perhaps</i>—Flight!

He motioned to Poni to take the chair he had thrown against the wall in disgust. The man reluctantly obeyed. Longtinsky stood in front of him, and his words were spoken in a lower tone, with more hesitation and with brief pauses; “Poniatowsky,” it was seldom his listener had been addressed with so much ceremony, and he resented it, but remained silent, “I want you to take my place here—right here with Jessie—see everything is fixing for the outbreak; we have ammunition, dynamite, and the men are all drilled for the <i>uprising</i>. Let them take me, let them imprison me, convict me,” his voice was rising with a sort of wail that fairly disconcerted his hearer, “and then I give the word—our plans are laid, and you lead an assault on the courthouse—see—there will be Tom Ratler,” a curse passed from the lips of Poni, “and there will be Briggs, and—there will be—I know it—I know it—there will be Helen Lorimer. Remember your promise—she is dead and Jessie is <i>all yours</i>—Yes all yours.Let Bomber strike the houses at Carrara, and Putkof light the torches at the river sides—and then our men will gather—<i>the men that lost the strikes</i>—They have forgotten nothing—nothing—they will march on the armories—on the City Hall—there are two thousand ready. I have strong husky men and—I can arm them—I have two barges on the East River full of guns—full—You start!—Yes, they are there: they look like dirt scows, but their holds are full of guns. Madder is on one, Felix on the other; they hate the Mayor; he threw them over after the elections—Bomber has a cache of guns and powder at High Bridge and Putkof enough kerosene to burn a navy. We have a friend in the janitor of the armory at Thirty-fourth street. We can make that our headquarters; we can sweep the city—the start made, the rest is easy. You must stay—you must take my directions, keep things together and—remember <i>Jessie is yours</i>—and—wealth, money, pleasure—power,” he was speaking louder, faster, and Poni, dazed with the revelation of the underground, unsuspected combinations, the immensity of the plot, was dumb; “Here are the keys to this desk,” he handed Poni a bunch of keys, “in there are directions—the whole plan—work it out for me, Poni, for Jessie; I see their cursed scheme—arrest me and all is nipped—Ah! ha! they think so—but Poni—give them the lie—the slip. Come let me finish this,” pointing to the unfinished editorial “and to-night, we will go over everything and get all ready, and then let them take me—”There was something almost maniacal in the man’s fever of desperation, as he revealed things he had never whispered before. In a moment he laid bare his big intrigues, and subterranean conspiracies. Poni was dumfounded; for once his nerve deserted him, he felt Longtinsky’s mental strength and silently he left the room, after a promise to return in the night, when Longtinsky would assemble also the <i>others</i>, and the plotters for the destruction of the City would complete their plans. Everything had been leading to this; the danger had been lowering; now with this new trial of the convicted men, with whose execution his—Longtinsky’s—preparations might have been further prolonged—with this new trial, and his dreaded Summons—his probable arrest—the cloud had broken, and nothing but resistance and the <i>Power of Riot</i> remained to be tried.

While Longtinsky thus shattered Poni’s dreams of escape from the spinning and tightening coil of circumstances, with a new bond of ambition, six plain clothes men—detectives—gathered in the street, and six more in the street beyond, and six upon Delancey and Grand streets, waiting for orders, to produce Longtinsky in court.

And that night the plotters came. There were Madder, a strong thick and undersized man, Felix a dark handsome Greek, Bomber a perverted American, tall and slender with a curling moustache which he kept waxed to the ends of its line-like length, and Putkof a Russian, glowering, silent, and dangerous. Longtinsky pulled out his papers from the drawer to which he had given Poni the keys, and sat among them, and went over everything. Poni of course was there too. The tough brigand was stupified with it all. His contempt for Longtinsky began to change into a devotional admiration and wonder.The man’s careful campaign, its parts and detail and the—monstrous audacity of it all!

Longtinsky talked and talked, his apathy of manner had vanished, the bulky frame shook with rage, and then the harsh voice ran on accentuating point after point. At times he lost command of the practical side of his Revolution, and he mounted into flights of empty and floundering eloquence. This was the old Longtinsky as Poni had known him, but the next minute he was outlining means and ways with the skill of a born commander. Every thing was rehearsed, suggestion from all sides heard; the reports were favorable, his confederates were sure of their men. They had blinded their eyes with promises, and infuriated them with denunciations. They would act together, and the distracted city would surrender. Besides the moment was auspicious; the military were for the most part away at some annual military practice in Chicago. When Longtinsky heard this he jumped to his feet, but instead of some voluble exclamation of pleasure, he just turned to Poni, and spoke swiftly and softly.

“Here is the man I leave to represent myself; if I am to be imprisoned consult with him. But Act, Act; leave nothing undone, and strike together, Poni at the Court House—Remember Poniatowsky to be THOROUGH—Bomber at Carrara, Putkof at the shipping, Madder and Felix where they are wanted.”

He sat down, and there was silence—and yet—had their ears not been deadened by the awful project that held them for the instant speechless, and Poni amongst them, enthralled with the, to him, utterly unaccountable magnitude of Longtinsky’s daring—had they not been so enthralled,they might have heard a faint groan somewhere above them as if in the ceiling itself. It came from Jessie. She had heard everything; she had heard her father’s threat during the day against Helen Lorimer, and she had heard Poni’s curse of her lover, and she had heard that her father was about to be arrested, and she had again seen the figures of the assembling officers of the law, from her attic porch.

Her mind had been excited and restless for weeks. Things she felt were drawing to a dreadful crisis. There had been goings and comings; silently at night; long in the depths of the night. She knew that men had slept in the little room which her father used for his work, and had left in the mornings, she knew that in the cellar where The Beehive was printed, there had been secreted strangers for days.

There was a sombre restlessness about Longtinsky; he had forgotten often to speak to her in the mornings as customary, and she heard him at night moaning—sometimes cursing—in his sleep, then shouting orders. Jessie became terrified; about her was a thickening mystery of anticipated terrors. And now;—she knew all—; in the crazy tenement where the walls were not so thick that patient and acute ears could not hear through them, there were also rents, and openings, and it was through one of these directly over their supposed concealment that Jessie, by removing a carelessly inserted block of wood, could see and hear the conspirators.The next day Mr. Moser resumed his case. The court room was packed; the news that Longtinsky would be summoned, the probability of new sensational evidence, the increasing half-realized conviction that the case would go against the State; the comment—generally favorable, often enthusiastic, sometimes, and in some editorial quarters, sarcastic—upon the presence of Helen Lorimer; the sensation created by her handsome companion, the unknown quantity of the new evidence; all contributed to draw the people, and to add to the surprise that the Mayor was not in the city. For it was currently understood that he had interested himself in securing a new trial, and that this again had been accomplished through the intermediation of Miss Lorimer.

Everybody was on hand as the clock announced the hour for the court sitting, and as the judge took his seat, and the court attendants scanned the multitude for any infractions of the dignity of the place, and the crier shouted his unintelligible formula, time-honored and venerable, and always declaimed with the frantic impatience of indifference to its immemorial usage. The eyes of the constant visitors saw two new features in the court room; they were quickly noted by every one; the big shapeless figure of Longtinsky, and the singular elfin body of the boy Angelo Shimura. And curiously they were seen in juxtaposition. Longtinsky, in charge of the Sheriff sat to the left within the railing enclosure, not far from Mr. Moser, and the child sat at the left side of the lawyer.Thus brought into proximity the sombre, sullen, and disfigured face of the Anarchist, surmounting a short, thick, and sprawling form, appeared more conspicuously in the contrast furnished by the weird, long and somewhat cadaverous boy with his copious black hair, his strained eyes, and a pale face, on which glowed the suspicious carnation of ill health in its pallid cheeks.

None watched this strange waif, thrown upward in the waves of confusion and mischief, to rescue the innocent, with more love and solicitude than the noble hearted woman who felt the danger of the boy’s inexperience and his strangeness, and his alarm, in the midst of this multitude, and in the glaring light of the public action. Her heart truly grieved for him, and she trembled at the probable chance that his story would not be believed, and that the relentless process of cross-examination might confuse and intimidate him, and beneath its pitiless exactions his tale—vouched for in her moral certitude that he told the whole truth—would crumble away, and vanish, fruitless and unfulfilled.

“Angelo Shimura take the chair,” called out the clerk, and the hitherto immobile and inconspicuous body stirred quickly, and, admonished by Mr. Moser, who followed him to his ordeal, walked to the chair, or bench, was sworn and, concentrating his eyes upon Mr. Moser, awaited his examination. To Miss Lorimer’s and Amato’s surprise and great relief, the child seemed indifferent to his surroundings. He scarcely noticed them; a glance at the observant judge alone showed his realization of a singular position, while for one instant he raised his face and looked towards Helen Lorimer and Amato, who both encouraged him with a smile, and Amato pressed his hands together as if clasping the boy’s own.That passed in a flash, and the precocious child leaned forward as if really anxious for the beginning of his testimony, his big head with its pale brow seeming exaggerated in size, by the abundant hair covering it.

“Angelo,” said Mr. Moser in a low tone, “where were you born?”

“In New York,” the voice was astonishingly clear, and the words spoken almost without a foreign accent.

“Where are your parents?”

“Dead,” without a quiver of feeling.

“How then were you brought up? Where have you lived?”

“Andrea Bocaglia and Michiela Bocaglia took me home and fed me.”

“You lived with them?”

“Yes Sir.”

Mr. Moser turned to two figures at his side, and said; “Andrea and Michiela Bocaglia stand up,” and then to Angelo “Are these the persons you mean?”

Angelo laughed softly, “Yes; they are.” The disconcerted and perplexed couple looked at the boy with smiling faces, and took their seats.“Angelo” continued Mr. Moser “How did you live with Andrea and Michiela Bocaglia?”

“I lived in the streets. They gave me food and I slept in a corner of the room.”

“Did you go to school while you lived in this way?”

“Sometimes Sir.”

Then followed question after question, showing the vagrant life of the child, his occasional weeks of attending school, his hours of wandering through the streets, selling papers, or matches, or shoe strings, his honest return of all the money he received to his foster parents, his own frequent hunger, his obedience, his longing for a better life—this told with a humble pathos that brought tears to the eyes of Helen Lorimer, and many others, in the huge audience—his meeting with a big man who gave him a book with pictures, and invited him to his home, where he did a little work about the house, and was paid for it, the good food he received in this house, from the man, and his daughter, who was very kind to him, and then with an almost preternatural grace and simplicity he told how his heart had grown to this man and he loved him, how he followed him, watched him, listened to him, and read—for he was precocious in this—his paper, which he said the man printed, and how he learned there—in the paper—that this man was going to free the people from slavery, and how he had one great enemy—here the boy became confused, and the blood surged to his cheeks, his eyes fell, and the tears ran down, and he wiped them away shamefacedly,as he turned his head aside, and covered his eyes with his arm. He refused to answer.

“What was the name of the great enemy of this man?” asked Mr. Moser coaxingly.

The boy was sobbing, and there was a murmur of sympathy in the crowd before him. Mr. Moser approached nearer, and only the prosecutor interfered to intercept his actual touching of the child, with his hand; “Angelo answer,” suddenly thundered the hitherto mildly persuasive questioner.

The boy uncovered his shrinking face, and whispered, “Miss Helen Lorimer.”

There was a wide spread motion in the audience, and some who were attending to Longtinsky saw his frame shiver, and his brows contract, and his fingers clench. His head fell forward on his chest.

“Is this man here in this room to-day?” continued Mr. Moser, his voice still high and resonant.

The boy looked at Longtinsky, and his face assumed the strangest expression; there was contrition there, and self-reproach one might have said, and then there followed it anger and contempt, and with it, flaring up in the same glow of temper a kind of pity. It was the face of an actor, the face of a fully developed human, of a man; something foreign in its complexity to the face of any child. The next instant Angelo had leaped to his feet, and pointing to Longtinsky shouted; “There he is.”It was one of the crucial moments in the trial, and, as if with one impulse a tide of wondering and astonishment swept the people off their feet, and hundreds rose in their seats, craning forward in the direction of the pointed finger of the boy. And then something even more unexpected happened. The dramatic pitch was given its finishing intensity, for Longtinsky roused into some consciousness of the possibility of creating a sensation, and always inflated with the delusion of his importance, his mission, and his power, sprang to his feet, slapped himself on the breast, and swinging round from the judge, the lawyers, and the witnesses, to the audience also shouted; “Yes I <i>am</i> Longtinsky;” and in the voice, not ill adapted for oratorical effects, sounded his imposing, ungovernable, conceit.

It was all so unusual, and unlooked for, that a stupefaction seemed to seize the court officers, and even the judge, but with the reaction in the audience of a wave of applause, sense returned; the judge pounded his gavel, the attendants crushed Longtinsky into his chair, and Angelo falling back in his bench sat silent and excited.

From this point onward the interest and power of the trial deepened, developments followed fast, the picture of the operations of Longtinsky and his accomplices came back to the boy, and with a fierce restlessness and eagerness, and with unwonted skill, with picturesque words, he drew the successive scenes of the plot, and the plotters, and the impending dawn of the catastrophe, and the catastrophe itself, forgetting nothing, recalling names, dates, attitudes, incidents, until the seasoned judge gazed at him with admiration,and Helen Lorimer absorbed and almost breathless fastened her eyes upon him with a feeling of terror. It appeared to her too exact, too remarkable, too, so to speak, theatrical.

He rehearsed meetings of Longtinsky and Polinck and other men in the office of The Beehive. Inscrutable as it appeared this boy had been present at many of them. He had secreted himself in some instances behind boxes, trunks, and under tables. He confessed to a distracting dread that somehow he would lose the man whom he strangely loved—Longtinsky, and this arch plotter regarded him without suspicion. He had even invited him in to the room when the means of destroying the Settlement House were talked over.

He followed Longtinsky in the streets at night; followed him to many conferences and meetings elsewhere, though he made it clear that Longtinsky was unwilling to leave his office, and, like a spider in a web, feeling all the threads of his snare, living at its very centre, concealed, himself, and lived a subterranean life. He passed from point to point, drawing nearer and nearer to the last arrangements for the destruction of the House, and the placing of the bombs.

Here Mr. Moser proceeded with caution, getting all he could from the boy, and retracing his steps, as if to confuse the young witness, but never succeeding. The boy’s memory never flagged. His nervous force maintained his strength, and it was evident to Amato, how wisely Mr. Moser had acted in adding all the physical strength possible to the frail frame, by his summering him in the exhilarating north.Helen Lorimer thought she detected a kind of overweening pride, almost a boastfulness in Angelo, as he felt the scenic pressure so to speak of the crowd’s attention, the absorption of the lawyers, the intentness of the prisoners, leaning over to catch his every word, and the growing dissatisfaction of the prosecutors, as he cleared the ground for the denouement of the last incendiarism of Longtinsky and his friend Polinck. For his narrative concerned them alone, and the men tried for their lives seemed almost absent in the repeated scenes he described of Longtinsky’s preparations.

The story had reached the point where the bombs were brought to Longtinsky’s office, and where a strange man whom Angelo said was not among the prisoners explained their make, and the method of their explosion. On that critical night the boy had been thrown out of Longtinsky’s house by Polinck, and had returned, creeping on all fours to the door of the editorial room—the apartment in Grand Street. It was a warmer night than customary, and a beating stream of rain flooded the city. Crouching at the door which was left unfastened, the boy, crazed with a diseased curiosity, and hungering for the safety of his patron, had slowly pushed it in, and, half protected against the rain, had overheard the last council.

The session of the court was drawing to a close for the day. Electric lights were beginning to flash in the streets, and the judge called the attention of Mr. Moser to the fact of the near adjournment. It was not unnoticed by that gentleman who desired to rest his witness, and begin on the next day the tremendous disclosure he had prepared. He bowed to the court and said;“Your Honor I will take the witness from the stand, after asking him one more question,” and then advancing to Angelo, whose strained excited visage became with each added memory more and more enthusiastic and eager, he asked him;

“Angelo Shimura do you recognize the prisoners at the bar?” and addressing the judge “may I ask that the prisoners rise for the witness’ better inspection.” It was the first time that Mr. Moser had called attention to the prisoners at all.

The men were ordered to rise, and the six with alacrity stood up; Angelo also stood at the witness stand, and looked at them. Still exalted with his sense of eminence, the boy called off their names, in a clear musical voice, a voice that many had already noted as peculiarly distinct and pure;

“John Dalzell, Pedro Vinta, Alonzo Fabrizio, Charles Bingo, Fred Rinker, Lorenzo Polinck.”

“No Sir, only Polinck.” There was some half smothered exclamations and the audience understood that the effective stroke was near at hand. The order of the day was however over. The clerk announced the adjournment the people rose while the judge left the bench, and then the surge outward to the doors began, and then too Longtinsky turned to the white chairs of the empty benches, and saw there one figure watching him with a blanched face. THE TRIAL (continued)

It had indeed been a curious study to the many watchers, the demeanor of Longtinsky under the ordeal of the day, and the astounding revelations thus taken from the mouth of a child. Many smiled at his complete blindness, some doubted it, others simply watched the man’s changing expression. There were times when Longtinsky gazed at the boy—who for his part seldom looked at him—with an absolute stupor of amazement; then he became scornful, and attempted cheap tricks of inattention and weariness, and less frequently he leaned forward fixing his blue eyes on the child, his face red with a tempest of rage, as if to disconcert and intimidate the inflexible inexorable little witness. Comments were numerous, and the gossiping groups of men, the lowering glances of many in the streets as they heard of Longtinsky’s arrest, the prodigious headlines of the evening papers, the covert allusions to it in the plays at the theatres and the manifest disturbance on the East Side where the police reported many brawls and muttered threats, betokened the excited and ominous train of interest.

It had been a matter of surprise to Helen Lorimer that her uncle seemed to avoid the trial, and made few references to it at their meetings. But on the day after Angelo Shimura had begun his testimony his interest was aroused, and something, peculiarly, almost anxious in his manner, amused Miss Lorimer and Amato as well. He wished to know how the evidence went and while he interrupted his informants with impatient comments upon the entire uselessness of the trial, he took some pains to find out exactly the drift of Mr.Moser’s examination of Angelo and what other witnesses might be called on the side of the defense.

The day succeeding the first day of Angelo’s testimony promised important developments. The boy had made an extraordinary witness, the tenacity of his memory astonished the oldest court attendants, and his impassive way, lightened up now and then with some burst of excitement, kept his hearers in a pleasant state of expectation that conduced to his popularity. His picture appeared in the papers and he was the talk of the moment. Already it was half discerned that Longtinsky and Polinck had endeavored to cover their tracks by complete secrecy and that the men apparently involved with them were ignorant accomplices. The doors of the court room were early besieged, and as different actors in the trial passed in, a rush forward of the multitude occurred, animated by a wild curiosity to see the boy Angelo, for his name passed from mouth to mouth, and, as crowds will, every arrival was hailed as the object of their interest. At last he did come with Mr. Moser, and his ineffective childish appearance, his slight malformation, and his oblique eyes proved disappointing.

“Looks like a simpleton,” was the remark of a big man who almost seized the boy, as Mr. Moser pushed him aside and shoved Angelo ahead of him.

“Out of the mouths of babes and sucklings God has ordained wisdom” was the quick and loud retort of Mr. Moser, whose sally was cheered.Within the court room every one seemed in nervous suspense. Animation prevailed among the attendants, and the streaming and crushing crowds were nervously excited. This tension became contagious. It was felt as an atmospheric disorder, and Helen Lorimer and Amato no less than the most sensational news monger acknowledged its disconcerting influence. The judge was early on the bench, the jurors were unusually prompt, the lawyers in their place, and the prisoners as they marched in, stolidly looking neither to the right nor to the left, when seated, bore an expression of expectation, indeed among most of them it revealed itself in smiles of confidence. They had not failed to note the overwhelming importance of the boy, Angelo’s testimony, and with them too the increasing fear was the boy’s power to withstand the cross-examination, and to vindicate his own credibility.

Slowly the great doors were shoved together and the clamorous and pressing throng without were shut off. The clerk stood up and repeated his formula, the judge rapped impatiently and summoned Mr. Moser to renew his case. One figure was not there; Longtinsky was in prison, and Poniatowsky, again among the crowd felt that the moment of action was upon him, and his conspirators.

“Angelo Shimura take the stand,” called out the clerk, who again swore the strange waif, thus suddenly thrown upward to confound the state and the law.

The examination had reached the stage where the witness was expected to detail the incidents of the night when the fire and explosions occurred.Everything had been carefully leading up to that; the repeated conferences between Longtinsky and the bomb maker and Polinck, and the final assignment of a date for the destruction of the Settlement House, and the breaking out of the Revolution.

Pitilessly Mr. Moser gathered the evidence together from the lips of the extraordinary boy as to the treasonable plots of Longtinsky, and still in it all the persons of the prisoners, excepting Polinck, never appeared. There were other men at the conferences and many others unrevealed somewhere on the outskirts of the grand project, but those men who had actually received their <i>condemnation to death</i> for murder and arson were never named. It stupified the hearers; the amazement grew. And Mr. Moser skillfully accented and accentuated it dwelling again, in his examination, upon the possibility of the boy’s mistake, calling his attention to the prisoners, pointing them out, calling them by name and each time gaining from the alert child a fresh denial of their complicity. Mr. Meldexter and his assistant were visibly non-plussed. Their case seemed to vanish under the breath of this boy into a veriest skein of smoke; their fabric of proof tumbled to pieces before their eyes.

And Mr. Moser standing close to Angelo asked him to tell all he knew about the night of the disaster. There was a breathless silence. Almost a thousand eyes were fixed upon the immobile child who for a moment closed his own as if to recall the vivid scene. Perhaps he purposed too to make his story more telling. He began slowly opening his eyes and staring at Mr. Moser.“It was a eveening, veery—that Mr. Polinck and Mr. Longtinsky came with the man—who put up the bombs. They came in the long room behind the press. It was dark and they sat at a table with—one light. They spoke low—Oh so low! but I back of a board on the side—and I could hear Mr. Longtinsky say—‘To-night—To-night. Get Alonzo Fabrizio, and Charles Bingo—They will carry these—to the Settlement House. Wrap them up like a long roll—and tell them they are ROLLS of PAPER.’”

People stretched forward their heads to hear the words; one of the lawyers for the state rose from his chair, and watched the boy; the judge turned towards him more directly, and the jurymen leaned forward with pale grave faces.

“Go on,” urged Mr. Moser as Angelo looked at him as if for encouragement, “What next.”

“So Mr. Polinck—he go away and come back with Alonzo Fabrizio and Charles Bingo. Is that all?” and Mr. Moser addressing the child looked about him, and back of him at the sea of eager faces.

“Then come everything Sir. Mr. Polinck and Mr. Longtinsky—they come to the men—out of the House—”

“What men?” interrupted the lawyer.

“Why Sir, Alonzo Fabrizio and Charles Bingo.”

“The men who had carried the rolls there as you said?” questioned the lawyer, his face turned away from the boy and fixed upon the District Attorney, who sprang to his feet and objected, but was overruled.

“Yes Sir, those men,” answered Angelo.

A titter swept over the crowd, as a breeze might ripple the dark face of an engulfing wave—it was the wave of a darkening rage, the fierce threat of revenge and contempt. “Among those men were there—”Mr. Meldexter was on his feet in a second. Mr. Moser recognized the danger.
