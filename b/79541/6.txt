AND THE HEAVENS HEARD. The head of the unconscious Rudolph Amato lay in the lap of Helen Lorimer on the floor of the soaring balloon, pitching and rolling in the billows of the ascending heat; her hands soothed his temples, and pressed to his lips the restoratives that slowly brought back color to his handsome face, and awoke the smiles of recognition in his gracious and loving eyes.Ned had not separated his inflammable gas-bag a moment too soon. As Amato had been drawn “aboard” belching clouds of winged sparks, like innumerable swarms of illuminated bugs, rose about the balloon; parts of the riggings caught afire, and were extinguished by the crew, and a stinging smoke followed. For the tremendous ranged pile of floors that had made up the great Municipal Building—a landmark in the wonderful city—had begun to double up, tumbling to the ground, ensheathed in volumes of flames and blinding fire-motes; a roar like the faraway beating of the shores by waves proceeded from it. Its pyramidal top, with the gallery, fenced with stone, where the rescued group had only just been standing, had bent curiously to the south—it was this rising of the north edge that had kept the balloon’s basket a little longer under the control of Amato—and the bowed head of the giant, dropped stones and disrupted ornaments from its crest upon the smashing pavements of the square south of it, some shooting outward to the platforms of the elevated railroad and the causeway approach to the East River Bridge. That great conduit of travel had been deserted; Brooklyn at the beginning of the fight had retreated, or the home swarming multitudes turned elsewhere for a safer transit.

Still the balloon drew off, mounting higher and higher. The blazing furnace shone like a colossal wand of light, its thunderous sounds of combustion died away; the last noises of the combat, yet unfinished about the City Hall, were scarcely distinguishable as Link pushed the aerial monster on, and, as it countered the upper air-currents above the mammoth metropolis, beaming in its nocturnal dress of electric aureoles, no note of the warfare there reached them.But lights along the East River side of the city showed where Putkof was busy, and, following Miss Lorimer’s request that they should all repair to Carrara, Link directed his aerial car towards that famous section.

Congratulations were fervent enough among the adventurers in this strange twenty-first century rescue, and the disorders that had threatened the safety of all were forgotten. Amato had become the hero, and the two men who had felt the force of his fists, seemed willing to acknowledge his merit, soothing their discomfiture with the thought that only his presence of mind and quick action prevented a dangerous panic. The poor stenographer hung over Amato in the cabin of the balloon, deck, where he lay nursed by Helen Lorimer. He had very soon recovered consciousness but movement was painful, because of the torn ligaments of his shoulders.

The cabin of the air-ship, of that remote day, to whose triumphs over the powers of the air, the indications of to-day so certainly point, was a small room on the deck of the balloon’s compartment, which was suspended from the balloon, and formed the accommodation for the passengers. In this space fitted with divans, its windows prettily curtained, and holding a few chairs, a table, and some convenient mirrors, serviceable, for their own vain inspection, to the merry companies that made such frequent use of the Zeppelins of the city, in the pleasant days. In this room Helen Lorimer sat by the side of Amato, and watched him with a strange pride and pleasure.Helen Lorimer’s nature was utterly lacking in coquetry, and she could no more have concealed her feelings, her quick interior acknowledgement of submission to this man, who had so modestly insisted upon recognition, and at this minute had stirred all her feminine reverence for courage, than she could have stultified herself at any instant in her life, by self-deception. Her frank and almost masculine candor prevented that, and her elevation of mind and position made the customary deceits of courtship unnecessary. She knew that this man loved her, and she frankly conceded her acceptance of his love at the exact moment when her own met and embraced it.

“It was an experience—something of a night-mare—” whispered Amato from his little couch as the balloon swam into the purer air; “and how awful it seemed to see you there, and all the possibilities—Ah—!” the pain made him groan a little, and he relapsed into an idolatrous gaze at the lovely woman who bent over him, adjusting with such gentleness the cushions and the wrappings about him.

“Night-mares are temporary expedients, fortunately, for the great purpose of letting us awake into the joy of living, and—there!—is that more comfortable?”

Amato almost laughed; “You did not finish that almost sublime bit of wisdom—of course you meant to say—at least I DO—living and LOVING—now did you not?” and he caught her hand and brought it to his lips.

“Under the circumstances,” and Helen Lorimer laughed almost as delightedly, though the color shot into her cheeks, and the faintest surprise stole from her eyes;“some indulgence is to be expected by you, bold Man, but you must not be as indiscreet in your thoughts as you are in your actions”—yet she did not withdraw her hand, just apparently leaned a little closer to him, so that its retention was more easily secured by the irresponsible party, who even tried to lift the hand of his lame arm to affirm its complete possession—and which brought on a sharp twinge of agony, and a few muttered ejaculations of disgust.

“Be careful. You may be even more useful when we reach the earth, than you have been in the skies. A little patience and your injury will be quite well, and then, perhaps some great risks—I feel grave over this revolt. What madness!—that shameless Longtinsky!”

“Risks,” he smiled delightedly. “The greatest risk I could run, <i>you</i>, have turned to success—Isn’t it so?—Helen?”

“You mean a—risk—with me? Well I cannot say—but I will be kind to you in your helplessness just now—I may retract later—you know women have privileges—you probably ran no risk. It was your own modesty that made it seem one——”

She disengaged her hand, and stood up beside him—his face shadowed a little, but the cloud passed and he almost cried out:

“If you invite me to boldness Miss Lorimer, I fear I may excel in that—it is an easier role to play!”“Yes?—but you remember the old adage—the adventurer who passed from room to room and met the last door, with the inscription ‘Be not too Bold.’”

She retreated a little, and bent towards him with her hands pressed upon her knees and a mock dissembling frown was allowed to add its charm to her beauty—a fact so overwhelmingly appreciated by the young man that he groaned at his impotence and then—without any attempted exculpation for his ingratitude—complained that his nurse neglected his comfort, that he needed more coverings—that his shoulder should be lifted higher, that he must sit up, that he was hungry—thirsty—in short that nursing, considered as an art of “absent treatment” was insufferable torture, and that Miss Lorimer MUST come nearer to him, and minister to his immediate necessities.

But that amiable lady remained at a safe distance, assuring him that her care for him was directed by the most approved wisdom—that excitement was absolutely fatal to convalescence—that he as a patient was not cold, nor hungry, nor thirsty, but—if he indeed did wish to sit up, and would ask for assistance to that end, less offensively she, as his constituted nurse, might be willing to offer him the requisite help. The patient did not remonstrate at this view of his condition, nor did he palliate the resentment of his nurse by assenting to her pacific proposal. He simply rolled and emitted a qualified form of howling—it might have been so called in the parlance of Scripture—and at any rate was sufficiently and intentionally subdued to such a point that it could not have been audible to the company on the outside, whose sympathy was, at the moment, curiously ignored.The effect of this uncontrolled suffering was immediate. It brought the apparently subservient nurse to the side of the wailing victim of circumstances—and, that her presence there was instantly consoling might have been inferred from the cessation of that unfortunate being’s distress—and, through some less conceivable reason than trained service as a nurse, it brought about a train of results not assumed necessary in ordinary hospital practice. As the young woman leaned over her subject his healthy arm became engaged somewhere in the vicinity of her neck—there was room for some confusion of mind in this operation as she appeared to assist him in the process—and then succeeded a slight cervical intermixture—<i>so to speak</i>—it almost seemed as if the nurse lifted him into an upright position, but there was a slight relaxation, and the patient was finally left conveniently propped up on cushions, with a singularly animated expression of complete relief, on his face, while the nurse—recognizing the urgency of the situation—sat near him with an appropriately pleased manner and some unmistakably—and improving—flushes of color upon her cheeks.

The company leaning on the taffrail of the compartment deck of the balloon, wondering at the marvellous prospect of the outspread city, thrilled with a delight that kept them silent over their wonderful escape, scarcely noticed the absence of Amato and Helen Lorimer, having perhaps some correct intuitions as to their mutual happiness. Onward in the illimitable lake of air the huge aerial leviathan made its way; the city below it was indistinguishable now in its details and became a punctured surface wherein dots of light ran into streams of sinuous gleamings while ever and anon a spoke of light emitted by some tremendous electric arc, shot up like a veritable pillar.The city was delimited by the vaporous splendor of the overspreading aurora of light, that strange wonder that had awakened Tom Ratler’s first amazement, and far away over Long Island the reverberations of light shimmered on the skies.

“Rather a narrow escape, your Honor,” said Meldexter, “I wonder what became of the Jury—and the Case?”

The Judge laughed. “Yes, it is a trying predicament as a legal precedent—a Jury liberated perhaps from mortal confinement by the victims of its own deliberations could hardly be trusted if their convictions were against the prisoners; they would be less or more than human if they did not give them the benefit of a change of mind, after they got out. There was probably no verdict reached and the Case is—as if—it had not been.”

“Of course the Jury must have escaped,” said Mr. Moser “Perhaps—the prisoners too.”

“The Case has undergone some enlargement since the morning,” it was one of the reporters who was speaking, “any guilt on the part of Longtinsky, Polinck & Co., has evolved into a tragic immensity that means treason to the State.”

“LOOK, LOOK!” the cry came from the opposite side of the deck. The men hurried round to the place whence the call had been heard, and there were the two women, the stenographer, the wireless operator, with the men of the balloon’s crew, pointing below them to a conflagration which flickered and smoked,rose and fell. The balloon had been rapidly falling. Ned Link intended to land somewhere in the flat space near the intersection of the present Speed way, Dyckman Street, and the Kingsbridge road. The anchors were no longer on board, and his descent was made more precarious, and necessitated a drop upon some even ground. This space was convenient, having been made into a grass land, holding lawn tennis courts, and croquet fields. But as he drove downward at 190th street, what was his astonishment when he saw that the region was swarming with men, and that the torch of the incendiary had fired the buildings of the public park upon the summit of St. George.

It was this spectacle that startled the sightseers on the balloon. But the balloon itself had not escaped detection from the earth, and as it approached, a sizzling bomb shot up towards it to explode harmlessly some hundred feet below it. The warning was however heeded and again Ned raised his apparatus, and soared in circles like a watching dragon, confusing and alarming his assailants beneath; they evidently feared his maneuvers, for now a sudden volley of missiles ascended, being for the most part pyrotechnical demonstrations, in the shape of sky-rockets. The little party of aeronauts were visibly impressed. It certainly resembled—this hostile gathering, there in the northern section of the city—some prearranged movement, and might as well as not have a direct connection with the outbreak at the City Hall.The balloon whirled upward again and Ned examined the conditions around the opposite hill of Carrara. Here all saw lights moving fitfully; confused murmurs reached them, and a sense of some sort of trepidation in the inmates of the tiered palaces came to them. Helen Lorimer had left for an instant her charge, after a confirmed assurance from him that he was altogether comfortable—at least for a few minutes, though his condition might, after the expiration of a short interval, assume desperate extremes—and was gazing too, with the rest, at the alarming symptoms of disorder in the city, with the Carrara homes as the immediate object of its attack.

The suddenness of the assault upon the Municipal Building had been astonishing, and its success unexpected; it was evident that the City was unguarded. Moser and Meldexter both recalled the fact of the absence of the Militia; the fighting around the City Hall showed the perilous state of affairs, and now they realized that, more significantly yet, a dangerous attempt to ruin the famous Carrara Ridge was in progress.

The more it was thought over the clearer it became; the city was in the throes of a convulsion. Helen Lorimer was agonized at the thought of the danger to her Mother, and of her home. What was to be done? She turned a questioning glance up to Ned Link in the pilot above her, and he saw her wistful face, pale and anxious in the light of the lamps, which had been again reillumined, after a short eclipse, when in the neighborhood of the skyrocketing rioters at St. George’s.“Well!” he answered exactly her mute interrogation, “we’ll sail down the Kingsbridge road a way, and I’ll let you out near Depot Lane—<i>perhaps</i>, if you care to chance it—you can get to the Carrara by the back way along the Lafayette Boulevard. I don’t think it’s very safe. I might get you somewhere else further away, but the ship has got to light soon; there’s something wrong—she’s scorched a hole or something—Hello—they’re off—” and he pointed to the east.

On the hillside where the broad street sweeps now from the brewery at Fort Mendel, to the Inwood valley, a moving train of lights was observed. There were not many and they came and went as if extinguished and again relighted, but they allowed the watchers dimly to discern descending a mass which could only be interpreted as the invading army of the mob. It was indeed Bomber’s force, timing his attack with the assault upon the City Hall. He was a little late; the fight in the City Hall was then waning with the rioters discomfited. Almost as the same instant red globes of fire ascended from the ridge in Carrara, like a silent yet luminous challenge—but that was not their meaning—they were the signals of distress, they were meant to call assistance. The party on the balloon understood them also; all there was again uncertainty and irresolution. Ned followed his own head. He determined to alight near Depot Lane and let them out; the balloon was becoming unmanageable. Suddenly it lurched and slipped through the air sideways, pitching the groups and the crew almost off their feet and righting again with a shock that knocked Amato from his cushions. Helen was at his side—how it delighted him to notice her alacrity—and as she helped him tenderly to regain his position, she told him of the discovery that an attack was about to take place at Carrara. It furnished him some food for thought.Link kept driving the balloon, shouting to the crew to shift ballast from time to time, and telling all to make ready to “GET OUT.” When the critical state of the region was first noticed, the Judge and the lawyers thought it would be best to go elsewhere, fearing the danger of alighting in the midst of the social hornet’s-nest, which was buzzing so obstreperously. But this council was intolerable to Helen Lorimer, and to Amato as well—injured though he was. Argument had progressed somewhat on these points when Ned Link finished all indecision by assuring them he must descend; he thought he could “fetch ’em a little east of the Arrowhead inn”. Thither their course was now directed. But they never reached it. The balloon either exhausted by its strenuous experiences, and perhaps, too, somewhat overcome and distrait with the reckless and unusual proceedings in the great city, even it may be surmised, suffering from a distracted sense of responsibility with its official and distinguished burden—or, it may be from some more prosaic cause, as a <i>hole</i> in its sides, began visibly to fall, still however sliding forward at lower and lower levels. Ned saw its inevitable collapse, and fearing to come down obliquely on the roof tops attempted to steer it into the highway of the Kingsbridge Boulevard. With his fans alternately set forward or reversed he managed to keep it, though rolling, (which motion played havoc with the gravity of its occupants and forced Helen to remain close to her charge assisted by Mr. Moser, the latter to Amato’s personal weariness) in the roadway, when seized by some last whim of desperation it rose, swerved, and—so to speak—dashed its brains out against a chance stone structure, whose convenient eminence caught its fancy, and went to pieces.A violent surge upward, a tilt that sent men and women sprawling, mingled with the irresolute and shifting furniture of the deck, a terrific bump, magnified, past all conception, beyond ordinary bumps, and then tearings, crashings, splinterings, and cries. That was the end of it. The contributions of cries came more from the streets than from the balloon. The streets were full of people: commotion and excitement reigned everywhere, and this new portent dropping from the air gave an additional and pleasurable impetus to the emotions of the miscellaneous crowds; another consternation for their jumping nerves.

Ned had leaped from the pilot, getting down to the compartment by a rope ladder, and ordered everyone to retire to the cabin. It was more stoutly made than the deck, and the balloon pitching heavily would probably split, and the extensions of the deck would act as guards. He had turned off all the power, put out the lights, and shot a hole in the gas-bag. Then he let things take their course, prepared for the last bump, and hoped to escape injury in the enclosed cabin.

The bump came, and with it a long hoarse wail, beginning with a queer murmurous hiss, and rising past the wail to a stertorous scream; it was the escaping gas, and the ripping sides of the gas-vault. The balloon shrank into a depleted cavernous bunch of waste, and this abrupt disappearance of the last remnant of buoyancy saved them all. They were all about flat on their backs, and the thud came just and disarranged them a little more, and really no one the worse for it. And after the thud came stagnation, and silence. It was again a humorous situation, and both Helen and Amato thought so.Not a sound had been uttered, even the two despairing women and the two belligerent reporters had been shocked into voiceless consternation. The distinguished Judge and the celebrated solicitors, the famous Helen Lorimer, reduced by danger—as she herself felt—to the ignominious level of clutching a more or less perpendicular floor, and waiting for eventualities, had certainly a comic interest. And when they at last settled down with the last jounce and no one seemed hurt, though, by reason of the mechanics of an inclined plane, they suffered from a little heterogeneous mixture, and Amato was in greater pain, both these happy souls laughed. In the darkness that laugh restored confidence and it was followed by Helen Lorimer’s voice:

“Well we can be quite sure that we have arrived; that was an unmistakable thump, but doesn’t it feel good to be here? Mother Earth has an unforgettable reality about her. Mr. Link, we owe our lives to you, and now if we can get out into the streets, and see about us, all will be right with us I know. As a sky-pilot, Mr. Link, you are a better success than many who boast of their prerogatives in that respect.”

This alertness and cheerfulness put all in good humor, and relieved the anxiety. There was really not much reason for alarm; the underpinning of the deck had been crushed out of all recognition, but its crumpling up had made the impact of the compartment less severe, and with the exception of the few bruises caught in their miscellaneous contact with the walls of the cabin, and with each other, the party was altogether unhurt.They found themselves at last on the earth again and proceeded with all possible haste to extricate themselves from the wreck. Willing and numerous hands helped them; they had fallen at about the junction of the old Kingsbridge road and Amsterdam Avenue. They were not so far away from Carrara. After they had “found themselves,” so to speak, Ned Link telephoned for the wrecking forces of the Zeppelin to which he belonged, to clear the wreckage; Mr. Moser offered his assistance to Amato and Miss Lorimer; the Judge, the two reporters, the two women, the stenographer, the wireless operator, and the elevator boy scattered, with more or less haste, to their several homes, after hurried congratulations. Moser, Helen, Amato repaired to a druggist’s where Amato was made more comfortable, and then Mr. Moser looked about for an auto to convey them to Miss Lorimer’s home. THE MAYOR’S DEATH

When the Mayor ordered out the 69th and 22d regiments, the sole remnants of the large militia forces of the city, he enjoined their commands to protect the properties at Carrara. At the same time, even as his messages flew to their destinations, Bomber was awaiting the influx of the men intended to effect its destruction. He had made his headquarters in the Park on St. George’s Hill, and he was not disappointed in the rapid concentration of an armed multitude. They came from the east, approaching their improvised <i>rendez-vous</i> over the Washington Bridge, and some moved southward from the north, reaching their position, over the hills of Fordham by the Mosholu Driveway, tramping in files or companies, all the way from the low lands of West Farms. The riot at the Municipal Building and the City Hall began at night-fall—about six o’clock,—and had been thwarted and defeated somewhere between nine and ten. The refugees whose perils we have recounted, had escaped to the roof of the Municipal Building at six, had been taken off about eight-thirty, and had returned to the north-central part of the city where this story has just left them, seeking safety and refuge at—it may be stated—nine, for the dial of the clock has some reference to the remaining incidents of this narrative.

The Mayor, after the repulse of the mob, had sent in a call for the ambulances, and essayed himself to help the wounded on both sides. It was a sorrowful and bitter sight. This, he thought, had at last been the outcome of the years of increasing class hatred.His heart bled for it; the fame of the noble city vanished, grown so beautiful and crowded too with manifold means of mercy! Was it just; was it right; was he to blame! The accusation smote him with an insufferable agony. He had worked hard to heal these social wounds, and the pride, the joy, alleviating his forlorn sense of desertion, had been an exulting realization that he had done so in these last months of tireless effort. And now—this pitiful cheerless tragic sequel! Tears started to his eyes, and sobs almost broke from his lips. He had loved New York so well and all its people. Was everything he loved to disappoint him?—and then the thought of the endangered Carrara flashed through his mind. He stood up, rising from the task of helping a poor wretch into an ambulance, with the young doctor that accompanied it, and saw his secretary Mr. Briggs following him.

He turned towards him almost with a motion of anger; this man had perhaps steeled his heart, blinded his sympathies, made him ruthless in the use of power! He knew his worth but it was a worth caked into a rigidity of cruel formalism by the prejudices of industrial needs, the set phrases of an apprehensive Capitalism. Personally he—the Mayor—had no faith, as faiths are counted, but he worshipped the ideal of a social Brotherhood—whatever that might be called. Perhaps he had allowed this man to obliterate this picture too easily with his incessant lectures, his frigidism, his secularity. He let his resentment flame out:

“Perhaps this Sir is your work—this slaughter—this fratricide. This is the outcome of the discord you love to foment. Do you like it?”He advanced towards him almost with a menace in his voice and action.

Mr. Briggs stopped suddenly and looked at the Mayor, just long enough to let the shadow of something like contemptuous pity steal into his eyes and then quietly answered:

“No Sir, it is not my work—nor YOURS; I have just put Tom Ratler in an ambulance and sent him to the hospital. I found him unconscious lying over there against the railing of the park.”

The Mayor was sincerely fond of Tom, and this information startled him out of all his brooding misgivings; over the field the doctors and nurses were working, lamps moved from place to place, groans too issued from many quarters; the police from the Hall covered the ground, and the outskirts of the park swarmed with crowds as the police pushed backward the sullen or the indifferent or the curious. The rioters had apparently vanished. Briggs told the Mayor he had sent to the same hospital and in the same ambulance Tom’s bitter enemy, Poniatowsky and with them had gone Jessie Longtinsky, though he added:

“Her predicament is painful, they are both her lovers; she fears one and loves the other; I need not say which is which,” and the speaker looked oddly at the Mayor and smiled.

“How did it happen; was Tom in the fight?” asked the Mayor.“Well, I think he <i>was</i>, and he was fighting his dearest foe—that Poniatowsky—they are both near the end of things.”

Pain came into the Mayor’s face, and some reminiscent revery held him for an instant. He looked over the workers gradually finishing the gruesome duty of getting the dead together, and despatching the wounded away to the hospitals. He turned again to Mr. Briggs:

“See that every one here—striker—” the word brought a sigh from his lips—were these the men he had repulsed?—“Ah—rioter and policeman alike are attended to. I shall take the machine and go north—to Carrara—”

Mr. Briggs was inclined to resist this determination at first, but he understood the Mayor’s feelings too well, and he really feared his outbursts if he provoked them; they could be unpleasantly vindictive and ugly; he would let nothing separate him from the Mayor. The man had been his best friend and he almost adored certain qualities in the Mayor, though he cherished a contempt for his dreams of social rehabilitation.

“Everything shall be attended to as you wish. But Sir if I might be permitted to offer one word of advice; it seems to me——”

“Not another word Briggs; the worst outbreak will be at Carrara—I know it. The very sight of those white palaces will make the blood boil in the veins of these unhappy, these misguided men. They will feel there as if they were about to throttle their enemy, kill him in his lair.If I can I will prevent it—whatever happens——”

He waved his hand and walked rapidly away; Briggs watched his disappearing form sadly. He was not a man of psychic sensibility but somehow he felt that a gulf of final separation was opening between him and the Mayor, and in that gulf lay the prostrate body of his master. A few moments later the Mayor shot from the City Hall park moving through a lane made for him by the police. His vehicle passed up Broadway which was streaming with crowds, restlessly surging towards and from the City Hall. Except for the trolley cars that occupied it, as they do to-day, there were no other carriages than the Mayor’s and it swiftly sped on its way.

A few recognized the Mayor’s automobile, and a shout or some hurled defiance, a cheer, a query, or simply the amazed gaze of the spectators greeted him. The Mayor was buried in his own excited thoughts. He felt the critical terror of this day, and he revolted with his whole soul against the needlessness, the awful consequences of it. It was not simply the pain of sympathy and bereavement, but the more disturbing dread of a disordered state, with civil unrest and embittered irreconcilable social elements, whose enmities would corrode the foundations of government and infiltrate all the veins and circulations of political life.

Sunk in the pillows of the Limousine he pondered over the action he should now take. He was not unacquainted with previous threats made against Carrara and he understood the hideous rage of the Longtinsky people. That man’s hatred of Helen Lorimer possessed him like a consuming fever.And now her home was to be destroyed, if it were possible, and she—his heart almost stopped at the thought—herself was in danger. And while the Mayor glided northward on the spinning wheels of his noiseless carriage, at intervals bruising the silence with its clamorous honk, and always sweeping the ground before it with two broad brushes of light; while Helen Lorimer and Amato and Mr. Moser were searching for a conveyance to the same destination as the Mayor’s; while the militiamen were hurrying to their armories expecting a sanguinary struggle at the same point, Bomber had anticipated these contrasted forces of opposition, and—though much later than he hoped—had moved down the Dyckman road and already was filing in three columns through Nagle, Sherman and Post Avenues to the marble terraces of Carrara, aiming especially to capture the home and mistress of the Lorimer villa.

Bomber was a man after Longtinsky’s own heart, and he had moulded him to his purposes, inflaming a bitter and criminal nature with the promise of gain, and filling his ears and mind with the murmurs of envy. An inordinate conceit, a feeling of injury, that he was the victim of a false social system, and a heart grown ruthless from brooding over imaginary wrongs, made him a fit instrument to accomplish Longtinsky’s designs. Bomber was the child of an Irish mother and an American father, and he inherited the verbal fluency of the first, with the undaunted self-confidence of the latter, and he had wandered far from the moral fortitude of both. His vanity, and the smoothness of his tongue had hurried him into foolish enterprises, and credulity and lack of discretion had invariably brought about his ruin. These disappointments had vexed his self-esteem and soured him.He had not been saved the consequences of an admired physical charm, and indulgences had perverted still more the easy rules of his life. Yet the strangest circumstance of this man’s life remains untold. HE TOO LOVED HELEN LORIMER.

At the death of his parents, Bomber inherited enough money to be able to live much as he wished. He had received a fairly good education, an education which might have been improved, if he had himself helped more the opportunities accompanying it. The city’s pleasures he understood, and his skill in talking, his dalliance with the brighter novelties of living, had given him a sort of vogue in a fast set of young men, among whom, occasionally, he met some belonging to the acquaintances of the beautiful Helen Lorimer. This was of course some years before. Bomber had often seen the famous lady at public places of entertainment, the theatres, the opera, at concerts—Bomber had some skill himself in playing, and much musical taste—and he had actually spoken with her at her Settlement House, when he endeavored to put himself in as good a light as possible, and thought that he had succeeded. Contact with women had bred in him an infatuation, a very great sense of his conquering personal charm. He felt pretty sure that Helen Lorimer must have noted it too. At the time he stood well, and the sharp reverses that later left him penniless had not yet developed. He had just then no use for socialism and rather sported his aristocratic prejudices.He undertook to give the “young Portia of the city,” as he called Miss Lorimer, advice in some well written letters, upon her various projects, and assiduously set himself about getting into her graces by an apparent interest in the poor—an hypocritical affectation then. The letters had been answered by Miss Lorimer’s secretary and a little curtly. Then he made a mistake, a most ill-considered blunder. He told her in a letter, full of ridiculous tropes and foolish imagery—a letter such as self-deluded men often write when fate drives them to their ruin—that he loved her. The letter reached Helen Lorimer. She blushed, laughed and carefully burned it up. Bomber waited for an answer. So intolerable was his conceit that he really deceived himself into believing that Miss Lorimer’s silence was to be interpreted as a favorable sign of her favor, and—making “his will lord of his reason”—wrote her another fatuous epistle. This letter Miss Lorimer did not see; it fell into the hands of her uncle, who, opening it by a mistake and catching its contents fell into a tremendous rage, and in an answer told the incorrigible Bomber that he was a jackass, that his effrontery deserved public exposure, that Miss Lorimer was insulted by any association with his name.

Bomber writhed under this repulse, and then his fortune failed, his plans went all wrong, his money vanished, the better sort of his companions deserted him, and he fell from round to round in the scale of decency, and he turned with an almost impious hatred upon all he had once admired. Bitten by jealousy, his heart poisoned by failure, his stupid pride affronted, almost to others a spectacle of indigence, the ulcer of discontent wore into his soul and he handed himself over to the forces of annihilation.He made a living by conceiving vitriolic attacks upon property, upon reputations, and he avenged himself for his repulse by insinuations, false, cruel and repulsive, on the fair name of Helen Lorimer.

The man became furiously bestial. His strength, his youth, carried him through trials of dissipation, that less stalwart or inured natures would have crumbled or decayed under. The fever of denunciation grew on him; Longtinsky’s plan, mad, impossible, delirious, suited him precisely. He rushed into it and helped its author to more completely arrange and perfect its details. He shared with Longtinsky the overmastering control of the whole impudent and hopeless scheme. But it was the chance to him for revenge! His impatience overcame every scruple, and he revelled in the dream of destroying the homes of Carrara, the villa of the Lorimers—even, in some more atrocious moments of desire of capturing, enslaving the queen of that proud society!

Thus, when Bomber led his little army to destruction, he found himself where at last he might work out his long cherished hopes. And it was a strange fact that in the notable uprising of this distant day, the leaders were entirely selfish—most of them—while the devoted believers in some sort of social upheaval, the men who formed the rank and file of the movement, were more moved by ideas of justice, and of readjustment, in which their rights would be restored and their ills righted.The shuffling lines of men came down the long hill of Fort George, they turned from Dyckman Street into the three avenues which lead southwesterly to the Kingsbridge Road. There was no uniform among them; here and there a red ribbon, and an occasional show of brass buttons in some frayed coat, hardly helped the physical homogeneity of about twelve hundred men of all sizes and service. Bomber had been disappointed at the numbers of his army, and he had a discouraged feeling about their enthusiasm. They were the enrolled members of an anarchistic society, and probably would fight well if it came to fighting. Longtinsky had once insisted that with any show of force, the Mayor would compromise—and then, on some favorable terms perhaps, they would gain power and there would be an UPRISING.

It all seemed simple enough, planned in a guarded room. Out in the open, beneath the stars the conquest of the city was more a matter of daring than words. So Bomber thought. Yet his hopes blinded him to fear. He did not expect any difficulties at Carrara. The place should be destroyed. He almost laughed to think of it. The police would be kept busy at the City Hall. Perhaps Helen Lorimer would have a little more respect for him after that!—suppose he took her a prisoner—an hostage of that sort, might make the stubborn necks and the dull heads of the Plum-Puddings bend, and think just a little. Indeed he was not averse to dwell upon the favorable chance of a conversation with Miss Lorimer and the diabolical fancy entered his mind of making some matrimonial agreement with her. So banal and ludicrous is a depraved vanity!Bomber’s tall frame had been moving among the men, as they slowly assembled, painting in glowing colors the ease of their work, and just what it was. They would be almost unopposed. Scouts had brought in the news that Carrara was being deserted; whether the Lorimer villa was empty was uncertain; lights were burning there, and figures had been seen moving in the chambers. But that was of no consequence—only some frightened women! Bomber naturally thought that Miss Lorimer had returned home from the trial, which, it was universally known, she had most punctually attended each day. He did not know of the conflagration and disaster at the Municipal Building.

He told his gloating hearers that the Revolution had been started at the City Hall—Putkof had set the shipping afire throughout the East Side—all the prisoners would be liberated, the city was panic-stricken—nerveless; the militia away—no regular troop anywhere around—the garrisons at the forts, at the Narrows sent away, and those at Throg’s Neck as well. They would win on their own terms. This was their chance to wipe out Carrara—the luxurious homes of their masters, the tyrants of wealth, the sleek vainglorious idlers who fattened on the sweat and earnings and skill and daily risks of the workers.

So he walked and so he talked—yet he had misgivings. One thing was annoying; his guns, his ammunition, had not arrived. They were concealed in an empty house at High Bridge, and the wagons drawing them with muffled wheels had not appeared. Only a few of those that pushed their way into the entrance of the park carried arms. However, he had no doubt that the guns would come—it made things late—though, and time counted for something.But a hollowness in the whole effort suddenly struck him; for one instant the great reserves of defence in the mammoth city were indeed abroad, but how long would it take for the four quarters of the country to pour in their support—how long before these reserves themselves would be storming his little host with all the implements of death? Unless—Ah, he pinned some hope on that—unless as Longtinsky told him, the Time EVERYWHERE was ripe for Revolution. He half believed it. The thought gave him nerve and confidence.

The attacks of the rioters at the City Hall, and on the East Side, had summoned all the police to the southern districts of the city, and Bomber, acquainted with the fact of the presence of the members of the 22d and 69th regiments in the city, was convinced that they would not be able to interfere with him. The Mayor’s warning and his own fears as well were destined to frustrate these hopes.

Still no supplies. Bomber chafed and raged—already more than an hour had been lost. He was general enough to realize the importance of promptness. Messenger after messenger had been despatched to High Bridge, and none had returned. Bomber was in a group of his lieutenants at the brow of the hill overlooking the Inwood depression, and the viaduct, to Kingsbridge, of the present subway. His first move had been to interrupt the trains of the Subway, and a detachment of his men had pushed their way up to the Dyckman Street station, and into the opening of the long tunnel leading south to 155th street—but no trains were running, the company deeming it too dangerous for one thing, and because orders from the armories requisitioned them for the service of the troops.Still Bomber’s men tore up the free rails of the tracks, for a short distance.

“What in thunder holds those men—Here—” to a dozen staring auditors who happened to carry guns on their shoulders, “Get down to High Bridge, a pack of you—Perhaps help is needed—Wait—” the men turned—“Let more of you go. There is a kit of tools up on the balcony—” pointing to the pleasure house raised in soaring terraces at the highest point of the park—“a few crowbars may help you—and for that matter a bomb or so—” he added savagely, for it was Bomber who gave these orders.

“Perhaps they can’t get at the guns and ammunition. Hammer everything to Hell, and bring them up;” then turning aside to a man who stood apart, watching the fitful signs of agitation across the valley, on the Carrara Hills—“We’re running our heads into a noose perhaps—Eh—but if we stand here and wait for sunrise it will be cold bullets!—”

The man did not stir; the whispered fear did not seem to interest him, it certainly failed to withdraw his scrutiny from the pale white palaces, now growing more and more indistinct, as the lights went out, or again illumined in a spectral glare as some white flash flared in the courtyards, or the gleam of a descending rocket shone on them. At length the man dropped his glass, and showed he was not unconscious of the speaker—but he only muttered—almost as to himself—pointing with his disengaged hand to the opposite rim of land.

“Win that and if the worst comes—there are ways out—Money provides.”Bomber seized his arm, drew him around to face himself, and then dropped his hands on the man’s shoulders and brought his face close to his:

“Yes, I know it does—but will a few empty houses furnish it? Do you know anything?”

“Know,” said the other returning the gaze fearlessly, and just perceptibly shrinking backward, as if finding this menacing scrutiny repugnant;—“I know that those money-bags have been storing coins and greenbacks in their safes for months. They expected this—some of them. Sly foxes, they knew the cry of the hounds long before they saw their teeth—they’re ready to give us the slip—Trap ’em and you are made. They’ve sold stocks, bonds everything and were getting their plunder together to slip over to Canada, make a dash for Europe, Mexico, anywhere—They’re too late. Bomber, if our hands close on that treasure we can take the same trip. Canada or any place might be a happier home for us than this city.”

Bomber looked nervously around. No one stood very close to them—the occasional rockets rising languidly from point to point in Carrara intimating some telegraphy between the houses, as if preparations or alarm were keeping their occupants busy, had attracted every eye. Bomber pulled his friend farther away from any chance interruption; his voice sank to a whisper, and his mouth sought his companion’s ear:

“I’ve been thinking of the same thing—But can we do it?”“Do it—I can. You know me, a bank-cracksman for years. I’ve not learned my trade for nothing. Get me in those houses yonder and I’ll find the bottoms of their long purses. Trust me.”

A cry startled them. They looked behind them, and they saw the flames shooting upward in the park—The pleasure house was burning—It was this blaze, then culminating into a vast pyre, that the frightened travellers in the balloon saw a half hour or so later.

It caused almost as much consternation to Bomber and his advisers. Their force was revealed—the increasing light brought out with terrible distinctness the formless straggling groups—their position—and in its discovering brilliancy, illumined the long slant hill, on which a few companies of men were moving. It conveyed a further terror to Carrara—the rockets ascended in sudden flight from new points. Evidently it was interpreted as a signal for the opening of the anticipated attack.

Bomber was quick to act on his part. He left the man whose sordid selfishness and cynical opportunism were already shared by himself, and ordering his subalterns to rush through the crowds, commanded them to hurry down to the hillside and screen themselves in the oblique avenues below—Nagle, Sherman, and Post Avenues—somewhat further away from this unfavorable exposure.

The conflagration had been started by the men whom Bomber had despatched to High Bridge. In rummaging about for the crowbars and picks,as weapons of assault on the closed doors that perhaps yet foiled the rioters’ plans they had upset an oil lamp, whose trailing fringes of flame had ignited the woodwork of the well dried and combustible pleasure house, and, as if responsive to the growing alarm of the great city, that frail fabric burst into a sheet of swerving and dazzling light. The men in companies and scattered arrangements of files and groups ran down the hill, and it was a few last individuals that Helen Lorimer and Amato saw as they passed overhead later. The floating habitation caused a little consternation to the men yet unequipped for the attack upon Carrara, and Bomber watched it with misgivings, while to the anxious sentinels—both men and women—on Carrara, it was hailed as an omen of rescue.

In Carrara, to which place we must now direct our inspection, all was in confusion, and in the dismay, increased every moment by new disquieting news brought in by scouts, or received over the telephone and by messengers, mingled the baleful prognostications of some special upheaval, and the long dreaded and often forewarned condition of affairs in the city was soon understood, though all the happenings at the City Hall were not reported, and when it became apparent that an attack was to be made upon Carrara itself, the householders hurried together and council was taken as to what was to be done.

Some argued for instant evacuation, others believed that an armed resistance was possible, but that bold decision was coolly shown to be impossible, unless the united men—proprietors and servants—garrisoned some one of the stronger houses and deserted the rest.Julieta Lorimer was in despair over the continued absence of her daughter, and no information could be secured from the offices down town while the few who in some way had heard of the incarceration of the inmates in the Municipal Building, and among them Helen Lorimer, and its consequent burning, none dared to tell the awful tale.

Perhaps in all three hundred men could be mustered, and these poorly supplied with arms, and resistance seemed hopeless with this number against the overwhelming odds. There had not been much time for deliberation, but such time as was allowed was well employed. Very helpful to the householders was the path of communication between the villas. It ran along the crest of the ridge irregularly set with alcoves, stone benches, and ascending and descending steps that gave it a most pleasing variety. Over this, that evening, the neighbors passed and their hurried conference was made at the home of Helen Lorimer. Hither came the family heads, young and less prudent younger members, and gathering with them a squad of the male dependents who were not all disposed to encourage a resistance to the approaching invasion.

The impending danger hastened the deliberations. There seemed to the wiser ones but one thing to do. It was a partial retreat, taking with them such conveniently removable articles of great value as they could gather, and retire together to one of the strong Norman keep-like erection on the Bolton Road, just over the Dyckman Street pass to the Hudson River. If extremities, too serious, threatened them there, escape could again be effected to the river itself immediately behind, and finally embarkation in some steamboat to be at once summoned and held in readiness.The season had so far advanced that the many steam yachts that were familiar features on the shore line of the river at Carrara were no longer to be seen. They had retired to the winter station, as at present, near Gowanus on the Long Island shores.

The last words of directions were spoken by a Duncan, the owner of the picturesque mansion where they were to find refuge:

“Shut, lock and barricade all the entrances of your homes, the more passive resistance of that sort we can devise the better. Remove all valuables, the things you especially value of small size; concentrate at my home and await relief. It must come! Should we be forced into an extremity we can beat a retreat to the river and escape. This seems the safest way. We are powerless against such odds.”

And so the hurried evacuation was made, while signal lights—the rockets seen by Bomber—arose from point to point as a constant warning for vigilance and for haste. There was much confusion. In the short time possible to be employed before the invasion began, it was out of the question to carry away the many things that each householder dearly wished to save, and the bustle and struggle of uncertainty was consequently frequent and prolonged.Doors banged, improvised barricades were formed against the backs of the entrances, windows were shut and fastened, the chasing of servants up and down the stairs, the calls of the younger men and the impatient orders of the older with many feminine sighs and exclamations were mingled with the miscellaneous and confused murmurs and more violent noises, proceeding from the opening and shutting of safe doors, the secreting of objects of especial interest—and too large for removal—in closets and cellars, while constantly mishaps, punctuated with cries and recriminations, arose and added to the panic and disorder.

Amid it all Julieta Lorimer sat in her chamber bowed in prayer for the safety of her daughter. She had quickly brought together the jewelry of Helen, and her own, and packed the treasured trinkets in a small agate-mosaic casket that now lay on the bed in front of her. All sorts of dreadful possibilities crowded in her mind, racked to the limit of endurance with fearful surmises, as happening to her child. The thought of Amato kept up her courage. She knew his readiness and courage, and she trusted his devotion.

How many wistful pictures of the past filled her mental vista of the almost forgotten years—the years of Robert’s tempestuous courtship, the birth of Helen, the increasing affection and chivalrous loyalty of her husband, the periods of travel over the fair face of the world, their mutual wonder of the beauty and promise of the girl growing up in the revolving seasons, and gaining new charms of person and mind, her splendid adoration of high ideals, and her rich and embracing love of all human kind, her distinction, the patrician courtesy, the austere ambition and her peculiar sweetness of thought and word.How Robert adored her; she saw them again and again walking together in a very ecstacy of genial communion, and, as though breathed upon her from the pregnant air, there came to her there, almost audibly, the accents of his wonder and delight as he told her of Helen’s unfolding excellence. She, whose respite from sorrow for the death of her own loved one, had only come in the intervals of her proud consciousness of the worth and buoyancy, of her daughter, now with terrified questionings looked out in the darkening night, and revolted against the sordid cruelty of circumstance if, in this vulgar exigency of commotion, that daughter’s life became the prey of accident. And as she gazed, half immersed in a praying thoughtfulness, there suddenly came before her eyes in the night the onrushing apparition of the lamp-lit balloon, in which that very daughter glided above her to safety. She had tarried long. Carrara was emptied and yet she remained. In the headlong flight her absence had escaped attention, even among her own fleeing servants.

And yet she did not stir, for now she thought of Helen’s unhappiness, the shocking discovery of her uncle’s perfidy, and the miserable disappointment that had darkened her life. Julieta sighed—she could not altogether regret it—Amato pleased her more; indeed, thus falling into reveries, she married him to her, and then the strange fervency of the desire of both to see the Pope placed in America and the new missionizing plan rose before her, and she forgot her grief, almost smiling over the Quixotic dream—for so it seemed to her. And yet no movement; she saw the thickening clusters of rockets, the frightening reminder of danger in the sheets of flame in the burning pleasure house on the hill, and the black crowds of men gathered in slovenly ranks for mischief.The lights yet burned in her room; she realized that they prevented any certain view of the outside, and she rose and extinguished them, and returned to the seat strangely oblivious perhaps in the indulgence of the luxury of her reveries, wherein she drew to her side the vanished dead, careless of remembering.

All had become still in Carrara, the shouts smothered in dull calls, the movement, the sounds of running feet, the opening and shutting of doors, the interrupted grating discords of shoved furniture and reinforced bolts and locks—all was quiet, for the refugees had crushed themselves into the Norman Duncan keep beyond the Dyckman Road. Here some guns had been distributed, a sort of garrison armament formed, scouts sent out, and a beleaguered attitude taken. The precaution was looked after of getting a boat ready at the water’s side behind them, and after some uncertainty and delay it was decided, then and there, to remove the women. Under guard the latter passed down the road to the Hudson in little companies, each with an escort of armed men. In all this commotion and anxiety, the absence of Julieta was somehow quite unnoticed.

Events were hurrying on to some fatal denouement among the rioters. The obstacles, which had delayed the arrival of their stores of guns and ammunition, and which were rightly explained by Bomber as due to the desertion of his allies who had been watching them, thus leaving them strongly locked up, inside barricades demanding destruction before the agents of the rioters could reach them, had at length arrived.The distribution of the arms to the men, the preparation of an advance party with bombs, the slow infiltration of orders to all the men and companies had consumed much time, and when at length the host moved forward along the three avenues—Nagle, Post and Sherman—the hour of nine had long passed.

Bomber had placed himself in the company of bomb-throwers, and the man who had boasted of his skill in discovering the hiding place of treasure was at his side. This little group headed the slow and irregular entrance of the men on the Kingsbridge Road. Immediately before them was the villa of the Lorimers, its marble terraces shining spectrally, and the outlined palace above them on the hillside, destitute of lights, inviting an easy assault. But Bomber feared some ambush, and he ordered a company of younger men—who had been acting as scouts for his army—to steal forward, and make their way through the shrubbery to the top of the eminence, for an observation of the measures of defence taken by the inmates. The men returned and told their chief that apparently the house was deserted. One man had broken in a window pane after forcing a shutter open, and the sound, sharp and crackling, brought no response. Still Bomber doubted, and he again ordered the men to push the examination further, and see the other houses, spy out the gardens, and return. The men dashed back, they were convinced that there was no danger in the abandoned Carrara. After a longer absence they again came back, and made their asseverations so strong that the place was deserted, that Bomber gave orders for the whole body to ascend the terraces and capture the ridge.Julieta Lorimer raised her head; she had indeed fallen into a half sleep wherein with half muttered prayers and clustering memories her bewildered and exhausted body and mind had taken refuge. She rose and moved to the windows again, almost as if in a dream, to see if there was any sign of her returning daughter. It was the moment in which Bomber’s spies were hurrying back with their reports about the deserted Carrara, the moment when Bomber decided to rush forward and take the houses.

The night was clear but there was no moon, the stars ruled the skies with a soft and remote beauty, but their effects were less apparent over the city of that coming day by reason of the spreading pulsations of the upper light, artificially created and irregularly distributed over the enormous commonwealth. This light, as Tom Ratler noticed on the first evening of his arrival was more concentrated in the lower parts of the city, but it now bathed the foreground of the scene that Julieta looked out upon, though by it flushes of brightness alternated, like shifting clouds, with intervals of penumbral vagueness. Julieta pushed open the windowed door that led to a balcony and stepped out into the ledge formed by its floor, just at that instant when the light grew stronger.

She leaned far out, and her white dress formed a contrast with the shadows on the wall behind her. She was seen by the watching men in the roadway, and for an instant Bomber suspected treachery. And then the thought arose with tempting plausibility that SHE—Helen Lorimer—might have been left alone; this outstretched figure was hers, and he might now be able, under less humiliating conditions, than before, to ask for some consideration at her hands. The stupid dream awoke in him a strange restlessness.He had not been altogether thoughtless about his appearance either. He wore a khaki uniform which became him; its contrast with the rude and often torn garments around their commander had already awakened unfavorable comment. It was regarded with some dissatisfaction. The broad unrolled soft chapeau on his head added its theatrical touch, even gauntleted hands and an unused sabre dangling picturesquely at his manly hip contributed to an impression of dashing chivalry, which he had quite serenely noticed himself. He now strode forward to the middle of the highway, intercepting the movement that had begun to ascend the terraces, and in a loud voice calling upon all to desist.

“He intended to reconnoitre the place himself. He would give a signal for the charge as soon as he had convinced himself that no rascally ambuscade awaited them. He prized their lives, and rather than risk an unnecessary slaughter would first determine the nature of the ground before them. One life,” he grandiloquently concluded, “sacrificed, was better than the loss of many strong men whose strength was now needed for the Renewal of Society, and the establishment of the New Republic.”

The grotesque coxcombery of all this struck one man forcibly, and he cursed the silly display, the bravado of an idiot.

“Why damn him,” he muttered hoarsely—and it was the man of the lock-picking skill, the man who had plotted with Bomber for the chance of interviewing the safes in the Carrara mansions—“Damn him; what good will his maundering over there do. He’s losing time and enough has been lost now to let those tin soldiers get here with their pop guns and do us for ever.Bust the whole shee-bang, rip, tear, and level it! That’s the way and then—GIT.”

Bomber was a mistake and for once the crafty Longtinsky had perhaps miscalculated. But the element of mistake was in the unsuspected fancy this apparently well chosen limb of mischief had for Miss Lorimer, which entangled with his own preposterous personal conceit made him the easy tool of circumstances and circumstances now most ludicrously related to his stupid prepossessions. Nothing can be more mischievous in unsettling a vain man’s sense of relations and propriety, not to say his more important sense of his own position, than the foolish imagination of his possible importance in the eyes of a pretty woman.

Bomber turned his back to his astonished companions, and mounted the steps with rapid strides, his own conviction growing that the white figure above him was indeed Miss Lorimer the nearer he approached the now excited and attending lady who began to awaken from the strange coma of grief and recollection which had held her enchained in a half unconsciousness.

Julieta Lorimer slowly recognized her situation, and something like an intense keenness supervened to make it all plainer to her. She had somehow failed—she now saw—to join the others when they had deserted the Ridge, and she was entrapped in her villa, and stood in the face of the storming rioters. She recalled the commotion of her fleeing friends, and she remembered she had stolen to her room, and here she had become isolated in her own sorrowful and oblivious thoughts. Her faculties were now fully awake; the terror only served to sharpen her appreciation of the predicament she was in.She did not become useless in her fear, but her mind engaged quickly and sharply in the preparation of some sort of defence. Who was this man—a figure ascending in leaps the marble steps—and why did he come alone? Down on the Kingsbridge Road she saw the massed men and women and seemed to discern their faint prolongation back into the avenues. Was it not strange—this immobile mob, voiceless, ominously still—while this man—a leader perhaps advanced all alone?

What was to be done; should she escape and pursue her vanished friends—the thought struck her suddenly that she would rush through the mansion, and turn on all the lights—she perceived the darkness of the place—it might create the appearance of relief or occupation. She hesitated—and there below her under the window’s balcony, the figure looked up to her, and she heard his voice: and noticed that his hat was removed from his head, and something in his accents made her stop, shocked with an incredible impression.

“Miss—Lorimer? I believe—I feel the awkwardness of this—position—but—time presses—I must enter your home—it is deserted—you are alone—you may trust yourself to me—it is an honor I have asked for before, and which then you saw fit to repel—as leader of a new Revolution—the revolution of the great heart of the people you may condescend to treat me—less carelessly—” the words were spoken with a mixture of impudence and menace.

Nothing had escaped Julieta’s ears and her mind worked with the marvellous concentration of memory, acting under the impelling sense of a real danger; she divined the situation;she recalled Bomber’s declaration of love for her daughter—this man is doubtless he—his name she had heard mentioned as the chief of the mob at her doors—she knew that relief was under way—that too she had heard—could she delay this aspirant for Helen’s hand?—keep him awhile—every moment might help—was she equal to the dangerous ruse? At any rate the evident delusion of the speaker must be encouraged.

Almost mechanically she asked—“Is this Mr. Bomber?” the question answered would illuminate the situation, and as she asked it she half turned away so that that gentleman might not discover his mistake.

“Yes I am Bomber, Miss Lorimer. Alaric Mitchess Bomber—I would be happy to assist you in this extremity—there need be no alarm—but the doors of this house must be opened at once—these men—” a flourish of his scabbard indicated the waiting crowds at the foot of the slope—“are in earnest—everywhere the forces of revolt are in motion—the City must succumb—perhaps I could persuade you to negotiate some sort of compromise with the Aristocracy of Carrara—Excuse me, I dread violence but this parley cannot be kept up too long—my men are impatient—”

There flew through Julieta’s mind several projects—and among them was one of startling possibilities—almost too audacious too, full of grave consequence to herself—and yet it tempted her by its amazing daring and shrewdness.She would entrap the leader of the mob—put him under lock and key—she, Julieta Lorimer!—he should be decoyed into the house, and she would agree to meet him within, and then—a smile crept into her face that was darkened the next instant with doubt; the indecision had been noticed by Bomber, and had elicited his threat. But the indecision was gone; Julieta committed herself to the rash expedient—she would take Alaric Mitchess Bomber prisoner. Shades of Longtinsky!!!

“Alaric”—in her sweetest tones—the figure started and stretched itself upward—“I will see you in the house—it is possible we can together do something to prevent mischief, and—if so—perhaps—we may—” the last words were permitted intentionally to fade away into nothingness—conveying thereby to the credulous hearer some indiscreet concession, that made him bound with delight and desire.

“Yes—Yes, Miss Lorimer—may I say Helen?—” the stupidity and dull conceit of the man made her project seem again very plausible to Julieta—“but it must be done quickly—it may be necessary for me to return to the men there”—pointing—“and make them understand—they might suspect treachery—is there no one in the house? They expected a fight and they are in the humour for destruction—you see—I will do all you advise—but——”

“Alaric,” still insinuating and delicious to the ears of the vanquished leader of revolution, “go tell them you are about to confer with Helen Lorimer, who speaks for the whole colony of Carrara—your own eloquence will convince them, or if they cannot be controlled entirely, tell them to be patient a moment—surely you can restrain them, and then come TO ME.”Bomber’s arms actually crossed themselves upon his breast with the unction of an embrace. Julieta saw it. “And we can at least devise some measure for my safety. That is but just, is it not?”

“Helen”—the voice was deep with passion and obedience as well—“let it be so. I will return and speak to them, and <i>then</i> admit—me.”

Julieta hid her face in the silken scarf about her shoulders and leaned down, whispering. “No one is here. I am alone. Come in at the front, the iron gateway through the peristyle, through the front doors. There is a great hall. Ascend the stairway to the dining-room, and then pass into a side room and await me. The side room is a butler’s pantry—you can tell it by the one light that will be alight there. Adieu, Alaric.” The old star of Robert Lorimer’s improvised show retained her skill, and her Italian hand still showed a delightful fineness. But then she had such plastic stuff to treat!

Bomber waved his hat, his gauntleted hand touched his lips. He turned and jumped down the terraces with really splendid youthfulness. His youthfulness did not desert him in the emergency of getting to the top of the gate post of the Lorimer estate; he clambered up somehow, and there in full view, with his hat removed, a few assisting gestures, he delivered his oration.

“Patriots of the New Order, we are about to accept the capitulation of the Aristocrats”—a long rising hiss, culminating in a yell of derision—“Miss Helen Lorimer, of whom we can all admit she possesses the virtues of her class,with none of their faults”—a mixed reception of cheers and hisses greeted this admission—“has agreed to see me in her own palace of art and beauty, and there I shall be permitted to dictate the terms of surrender. Miss Lorimer has had the courage to remain, while all her BRAVE”—uttered with impressive sarcasm—“defenders have fled. I only ask you, my noble comrades——” Bomber was a traitor; while he spoke these words he was secretly turning over in his mind the chances of freeing himself from these now unnecessary associates, and turning body and soul over to the abhorrent aristocrats. Here was the goal of his fondest dreams—becoming with Helen Lorimer rehabilitated, and an ornament of the class he affected to despise.

“I only ask you now to await the results of this interview; when it is finished you shall enter Carrara and POSSESS it.” He paused, and a cheer arose; he interpreted it as his permission—he was too impatient himself to return—he jumped off his elevated rostrum, and as he turned and ran again backward a voice—loud, soaring, he <i>knew</i> it—shouted in the silence, “Al Bomber you’re a LIAR.”

But Bomber had no desire to refute the statement, his heart burned with the strangest hopes, and his self inflation, his fatal aptitude for gallantry approached, as he thought, a new and marvellous satisfaction. He kept on his way unchallenged.

Julieta left the balcony; her heart beat tumultuously; the trick was bold, and desperate—it almost seemed. Would she be successful?There was danger—plenty of it—too; if he suspected; if he brought men back with him; if his hearers were not affected with the reasons for gullibility he succumbed to; if she herself could escape—and WHERE? Certainly the butler’s pantry was a well chosen place for the incarceration of the dashing leader of the New Order. It was practically a strong box—literally a safe—with iron walls and iron doors, where the silver of the family was also kept. Besides entrance from the dining-room, it possessed but one other egress, a back door, fastened on the inside. That she believed was already secured; locked, with the key removed; it was the practice so to do at night. It was to be her hands that would shove the bolts, upon the imprisoned Bomber, from the outside, of the very entrance he would use. Her limbs trembled with the agitation of thinking of the work before her, but the instant she had groped her way down to the spot, turned on the single lamp that would announce the rendez-vous and then secreted herself in an alcove of the dining-room, with her dress covered by a voluminous black overskirt she felt a singular firmness, almost a jubilant expectation. She had concluded to rush from the room at once when the deed was done, and make her way to the Duncan keep.

Suddenly she bethought her that the doors below, the great gate of the entrance also were yet shut. Gathering her dress about her, she descended the stairs, and unlocked and unbolted all. It took time, some strength was required, and her nervous excitement alone made it possible to accomplish it. Of course she became visible to one near at hand, when she pushed her way out into the peristyle, to open the iron gate of the courtyard. She shivered as she crossed the yard; her confidence almost deserted her as she saw through the trellis of the gate the returning figure of the man she was about to capture.It was indeed only by a superhuman effort she pulled down the huge bolt for he was close upon her. Bomber had detected the woman form moving behind the trellis, as he ascended; he knew it was SHE and he hastened his steps. Julieta felt her heart fail her; she staggered—in another second, before she could possibly return, he would be at her side, and the revelation would be made—she turned irresolutely to run, when an oath almost at her side, as she thought, came to her ears, and the sound of a falling body. Instinctively she turned again, and she saw Bomber sprawling on the outer steps—that unlucky emblem of authority, his sword, had twisted between his legs and tripped him. It was Julieta’s chance.

She raced up the inner steps—those leading to the dining-room—the superb banquet-chamber where we have seen Helen Lorimer dispensing the hospitality of the Lorimers—she crouched in the selected alcove, not far from the open door of the butler’s pantry, where the seductive little light gleamed secretively. She stiffened her resolution, though the mounting beats of her heart warned her of the terrific strain. She heard the rising footsteps—perhaps a little more careful since their proud owner had learned the wisdom of caution—and then something darkened the door of the dining-room. It was he. He stopped; her shuddering heart shrank within her at the suspicion that he saw her. No; he passed on, his eye had seen the interior light of the pantry, and he stood at its entrance. Why did he not enter? The strain was insupportable, the pale light shone on him, though dimly, and he seemed to be examining the place as if fearing a surprise. If he waited longer his accustomed eyes would find her out; there was a little light diffusing from the lamp in the pantry; her hammering heart, it felt so, was itself audible in the dead silence.Julieta had run a serious risk in assigning the butler’s pantry for a meeting with Bomber. She knew only its peculiar strength and adequacy for service as a prison; she did not know how it had been left by her fleeing servants. The great key which locked both the inner and outer doors was always removed at night by the housekeeper and given to Julieta. She had not received it that night; it was not in her room; she concluded it still remained in the door of the pantry, but had the INNER DOOR BEEN LOCKED? When she descended to the dining-room her first anxiety was to assure herself of the position of the key. Her heart leaped when she found it in the keyhole of the outer door, the door leading from the dining-room. A moment later the lamp was turned on in the pantry, and she tried the inner door. It had been locked. Both locks were identical: one key fitted both. They were large constructions, the key itself ponderous, and it threw three heavy steel bolts when turned.

So far the trap was ready. One imprudence was committed. She left the key in the outer door, but in order to execute her design she took the risk willingly. To insert the key in the dark, to shut the door and turn the lock would be an involved process, and in the delay, and possible bungling, her quarry might have more than enough time to break its net and escape. So the key was left; if Bomber took the precaution to remove the key before entering the ambuscade and to pocket it! Julieta would have been thwarted, and only headlong flight would have saved her.Bomber still stood at the door of the pantry, almost as if suspicious; it was thus impossible to close it upon him. He even stepped back again, possibly puzzled that the figure which, a minute before had been in the courtyard, should now have so completely vanished. If he struck a match! If he fumbled around and attempted to turn on the lights! Julieta grew dizzy at the thought. She cowered together, her knees trembling, and her hands abjectly pressed upon her face. He looked again into the pantry and—walked in. His unmeasured personal conceit had saved her.

Bomber’s eye had fallen upon the lustrous expanse of a superb silver service-tray; in the half light this immense and beautiful work of the silversmith, hanging on the wall of the pantry, shone softly, and displayed invitingly a damasked surface, and its high decorative borders. It tempted the eye irresistibly. Bomber had actually in his delight with its richness and, in a feverish anticipation of a great success, walked to it, and caught his own reflection in the polished mirror of a central oval of smooth silver. He stooped and observed that his hair had become artistically disarranged, that his cheeks glowed, his eyes—yes, he was sure of it—emitted an unusual light, and his mouth—ah! he smiled, he leaned over to the beguiling picture, when—what was it?—a sluggish thud, and the click of a shot lock!

He turned like a shot animal, his face convulsed with terror; the door was shut; he sprang to it with his strength—and it was considerable—he wrenched at the knob, but though it flew to and fro, nothing yielded. HE WAS A PRISONER. He looked around him; he leaped at the inner door and attempted to open it. Locked! No window, no crevice, no loophole of escape.“My God,” he groaned aloud, “the she-devil, the tiger, witch, curse her. Am I trapped here like a rat?”

His face grew almost livid; he raised his voice shouting, yelling, shriek after shriek in the bootless effort to call help. He hurled the silver tray at the walls, he searched them with knives but only a steel wall met his fury. And then he sank back on the only chair in the room, in a convulsion of sheer rage and agony.

And Julieta, like a flying cloud, her black skirt lost from her in her flight, sped out of the house, and on the well-known pathway, down and up steps, in front of and behind low shrubs, through archways, quicker and quicker she ran on, stumbling now, even falling, regaining her feet, and yet running on in a vertigo of excitement, the first stages of a terrible reaction.

But while this half ludicrous <i>intre-acte</i> was taking place, events outside of them were rushing on to a tragic end. The waiting army enormously enlarged by the running concourse of sightseers, the various accessions of the idle and vicious, was waiting the return of their fatuous leader. A growing discontent, with an increasing fear that some one had blundered, that time was being lost, that the police or the soldiers would soon confront them, made itself apparent. A hubbub of voices was now heard where all, an hour before, had been silent, and the presage of dangerous work before them had kept the men grave and dumb. Disintegration was evident. The disgusted safe-robber felt that, and sneered at the futile idiot chosen for this work.Suddenly a distant honk announced an approaching automobile, another and another—it was coming on fast—the coarse grating alarm sounded now almost among them. The foremost drew back from the roadway, a few wandering in the Lorimer grounds, looking up the shining stairs, slashing at the plants and grumbling aloud, turned back, so that the vehicle passed between two solid files. It stopped; the door of the limousine swung open—a tall, thin man alighted. He ordered the chauffeur to assist him, as he mounted the puffed tire of the wheel, and was pushed upon the roof of the car. He stood up, and by the lucky incidence of light from a distant electric lamp he was revealed. It was the MAYOR.

A long roll of subdued imprecations greeted him, rising with a sudden access of noise into a clamor of shouts, oaths, and yells. It increased, the suppressed impatience and their inaction, the confusion of mind produced by the disappearance of Bomber, some doubt as to their position, and the disquietude of a sense of impending failure, all intensified by their hostility to the man who had vanquished them in an industrial war, merged into an awful expression of hatred. An abstract alarm sharpened into a real rage at a concrete personality, and they stormed forward, filling the highway, surging upon the automobile, while the cries of “Kill him, kill him,” maddened them to violence. They crushed about the wagon, and guns came to their shoulders. Not a missile had been thrown; the passion of the mob vented itself in execrations.

“The Scab,” “He took our bread,” “The Carrara Stool-pigeon,” “Have him down and tear him to bits,” “Rip his glib tongue out,” “To Hell with him: He belongs there.”The seething tide, in another instant would have overturned the machine. It could not advance. It was wedged in a matrix of solid humanity; that alone saved it from an ignominious upset.

The Mayor had been silent and held his place; his arms were folded, and not a word of expostulation as yet. His attitude both vexed and puzzled the insane mob. For one instant the voices were silenced—it was the prelude to a murderous design—and then came the clear startling words, strangely almost weirdly penetrating; “Yes! You can kill me if you like. The thing is easily done—but you will lose a friend—I beg to tell you you will lose your fight—” The silence continued, the Mayor jumped at the chance and his voice rose higher with that peculiar musical distinctness and charm that Helen Lorimer had noticed when she first saw him in the City Hall.

“What have you got against me? One thing. I did my duty as the Law commanded. I put down your Lawlessness. Could I do anything else? Could you? But is that all I have done? Have I not also restrained the men who would have destroyed you so far as they could? What is your grievance? Are you starved? Are you naked? Are you unsheltered? I know that you are <i>not</i>. Labor conditions are not bad here. You are misled. You have been listening to foolish promises, you have followed false leaders. You have let envy take the place of sense. You dream of some sort of a new State. It is called The New Era; no New Era can be well considered that begins with WAR. Unnecessary, cruel, careless, brutal WAR! Any Economic idea that starts with WAR will end with it! It cannot abide. Civil disorder, Civil strife, means one thing—ANARCHY; and anarchy is not a thinkable civil state.You might as well—you who are builders—think that a house can stand which is built out of plumb, out of line—you who are plumbers that you can have health with broken pipes, and empty reservoirs, you who are cloth-makers or hat-makers or workers of any sort, that you can do your labor without tools. You preach SOCIALISM but <i>society</i> comes first, and society is superior to any theory as to how society shall be conducted. I think I understand as well as you do much that can be said for the aims of Socialism, but I understand a great deal better than you do, much that can be said against it. I too have learned things. Are you above learning also? These people,” waving his arm towards the houses of Carrara—“have done you no harm by living. Would you care to live as they do? Hardly. Cannot we take a lesson from Nature—a flat world would be an uninhabitable world—a flat Society—all alike—would be impossible. I will talk this over with you, but not here. IF YOU WILL ARGUE THIS MATTER WITH GUNS, YOU SHALL BE ANSWERED WITH GUNS.”

His voice ceased and the silence was for an instant broken by cries and questions. He had made an impression. The Mayor felt that, and he was eager to take advantage of the favorable opening. He leaned forward—when suddenly the Gun Argument, he had invoked, began somewhere back in the streets toward the Saint George Hill. There was firing—sharp unmistakable firing. All again was disorder, tumult, panic. A terrible cry smote his ears.“Clear out!—the SOLDIERS—make off!” It was true. The two regiments—the 22d and the 69th—had been partially mustered, and they were forcing their way down the three avenues towards the Kingsbridge Road. And thus unexpectedly, shamelessly, this silver voiced flatterer became to the men in front of him a deceiver—the traitor had kept them there while the troops gathered; his very words “<i>answered with guns</i>”, was the signal for this assault. The Mayor realized the awful accusation of this coincidence. He recoiled from it, his face darkened with suffering. Curses reached his ears, and then the whistle of a bullet, and he fell backward on the roof of the car.

An avalanche of men hurled out of the avenues. They were in flight. The firing came closer. Only the rush of feet was heard. Still firing, and the despairing race of men past the motionless Mayor there on the top of the limousine, that, like some slight pebble in a stream, sufficed to divide the tearing unguided current. And yet none of those who passed, bore any evidence of wounds. There were none. The Mayor, in ordering the soldiers had commanded that <i>dispersal</i> not <i>slaughter</i> should first be tried with the rioters. Fear did its perfect work. It was as the Mayor wished, but he had fallen the victim of his own imprudence; in a manner, of his own forbearance.

Soon it was all over; the troops emerged from the streets into the roadway, and the vast throngs, the little army of Bomber, the idle gazers, the haunting lines of free-booters, had EVAPORATED as completely as the patch of water on the flagging succumbs to the dry summer wind that sweeps it into space.The Mayor was carried into the Lorimer mansion; the soldiers patrolled the streets; the dispossessed returned, lights spread over the deserted ridge, doctors hurried to the side of the City’s executive, Briggs arrived, and, under orders from the Lieutenant Governor, the surplus of the soldiers marched to the east side to assist the police and the firemen, who still fought an uncertain contest with the wily incendiary Putkoff. And in the midst of the mingling confusion of renewed confidence, the bustle of return, the flashing telegrams from all parts of the city, the news of the speeding armaments from Chicago, the resumption of the whirling life of the huge metropolis, stifled into inaction for a few short hours by this flurry of insurrection, Helen Lorimer and Amato and Mr. Moser arrived, and Helen learned all.

Julieta had made her way to the Duncan Keep after her successful encounter with Bomber, and there became incoherent and dizzy with fever. She managed to tell her story, and almost the first thing done when the rehabilitation of the houses began and the colonel of the regiments came on the scene was to open the now famous Butler’s pantry. There indeed sat the raving Bomber. He was pale with frenzy. He leaped at the first man who entered, and only a sharp struggle overcame him. As he passed down the steps of the mansion, handcuffed, he encountered Amato and Helen hurrying upward, but he averted his eyes, and the bitterness of his defeat, the confession of his shallowness and vanity made him writhe with anguish.Helen had asked for her mother. Julieta had been very carefully conducted back to her home, and lay in her bed yielding to the first vagaries of a disordered mind. Helen stood at her side and leaning over the changed flushed face of the beautiful woman whispered her name, and pressed her lips upon her own. Julieta opened her eyes and her hands feebly closed over the head of her child, holding her in a delirium of joy. But the strained nerves gave way, and she relapsed into a kind of inanition awaking at times to beckon from the air some shape appearing there to her, as she called softly, “Robert, Robert.” Then Helen herself surrendered. The ordeal had been a long one, and this climax of anxiety, with the strange vicissitude of the Mayor’s extremity in her own home, completed the breakdown. Still it was not serious, and nourishment and rest would restore the strong nature to its natural estate.

The Mayor’s condition was critical; life ebbed and returned; the doctors camped at his bedside; skill and art and experience worked hard together to keep him alive, yet sensibly the flagging heart released its control, and the solemn end grew more and more certain. Then it was—the next day in the afternoon—that the dying man asked for Miss Lorimer. He knew where he was, from time to time in the brief intervals, when pain left him, his eyes reviewed the decorated and charming room, dwelling with such a depth of tender interest on all these things that recalled—how pathetically who shall say—the ONE love of his life.Helen came. The Mayor lay in the room of her uncle—the man who had separated them. It was a luxurious apartment, with every appointment perfect, and its windows opening to the west, in the sinking November day caught the last lights over the russet and gold of the Palisades. The Mayor lay on the bed, with his face turned to the flushing western sky, rent as it were, with seams of gold, as though the purple tapestry of the clouds were torn, and the gateway of the sun’s descent disclosed. Helen stood at the doorway; she had come noiselessly, and waited for her own heart to beat less wildly. A doctor held the Mayor’s wrist, timing the sullenly retreating tide of blood from his young heart; a nurse bent over him. Both happened to hide Miss Lorimer from him.

The doctor turned, the nurse moved away, the Mayor’s eyes fell on the face of the woman who there awaited his summons. It came from those blue eyes whose mystery had first enchanted her. And her own answered them. Slowly she came forward, and sank into the chair the doctor had just deserted. The Mayor looked past her for one moment at his attendants, and a mute entreaty they read in his face, caused them both to withdraw.

“Pardon me, Miss Lorimer,” said the doctor, “I must soon return.” Helen bowed her head, a gesture of the doctor’s summoned the nurse and the two went out together. Helen had taken the dying man’s hand; she held back the struggling tears; his own eyes were strangely soft and winning, the effort of this interview brought again some color to his face—but so very little!—the pitiful remnant of his wasting life—and his voice, that appealing musical voice just retained or recovered its former note. She bent her face close to his as he spake.“Miss Lorimer,” he began, but this noble girl whispered, “let it be Helen please,” and he smiled, “Helen,—you recall that talk we had in the museum,—your new museum—?”

“Yes,” she quietly responded.

“Well, here in my wandering dreams I have gone over it for hours. You have always been near me, and your voice has sounded to me almost as if it bore to me the music of another World”—his face darkened with the overwhelming torrent of his sadness, but no sobs broke from his lips. The spasm of grief passed, she pressed his hands, she wiped the stricken eyes, she slid softly from her chair, she knelt at his side.

“Helen, I go too with the setting sun—my day is finished—I thank the Everlasting that it passes thus. But let me be clear in your eyes. Your uncle drove me from this house. My pride could not brook his insult. Helen, my love has never ceased, never changed. Death only takes away its earthly dwelling place, and IF—If, as you began to teach me, there is still life THERE”—he pointed feebly to the blazing west—“if there is, there too my love will stay unchanged. Ah! the long day’s task is done, and I must sleep. But Helen you forgive me”—then with an ardor that made the girl before him pale with apprehension that the untoward passion would put out the fluttering flame of life, he added—“Helen have I still your Love?”

The woman bent her face to his; their lips met—a golden light entered the windows, it filled the room;it was so intense and brilliant that objects seemed lost in it; it dazzled as though an etherialized substance suffused the space, blotting out all definite shape, and enthralling all the senses in the captivity of one. It was so for the interval of half a minute, and then again the depleted room was established in the commonplaces of its utilities.

The doctor and the nurse had returned, and behind them came the bowed form of the Mayor’s mother, led by Mr. Briggs. Helen rose and passed to the window, her eyes straining at the spectacle of glory in the clouds. To her catholic heart and nurture it seemed appallingly cold and ruthless, this passage of a soul into the awful futurity, unattended by prayers, unaccompanied by the offices of devotion, with no sensible presentation, still to hold the dissolving corporeal powers fixed in adoration that the soul might more purely, more wisely, pass on to its new appointments, rising on the wings of aspiration.

The strength of a tremendous religious faith was hers, and in that strength her lips repeated the Orison of Help for the dying.

A half-stifled cry, a movement behind her, caused her to turn. The doctor was taking away the stunned mother of the man who had already just made his confession to her ears. Helen passed quickly to her side, took her in her arms, and brought her out of the room, passing through a crowd of men, officers of the city,—state and citizens, and gently made her way to her own chamber, with a tenderness that was itself a ministration of healing to the crushed heart, the hopeless spirit of a Mother.THE MAYOR WAS DEAD.

<b>CHAPTER XVII</b>

THE POPE COMES TO AMERICA

The Mayor was buried with splendid pomp. His body lay in state; the militia who flocked back as the alarm over the city’s dilemma spread to the neighboring cities, with the garrisons of the regular troops, who had also returned, formed his escort, and the music at his funeral touched the limits of elegiac elaboration. A sense of his excellence as an administrator, a sudden recognition of his intellectual delicacy and skill, combined with the thrill of admiration felt at his daring in confronting the rioters, the relief that followed the dispersal of the insurrection together with the repudiation of the whole Longtinsky anarchistic plot by the sane and moderate socialistic organs contributed to elevate his memory, and imbue his name with a peculiar—to some a mystifying—glory of renown and nobility.

When all was over, the huge city-state resumed its myriad activities, and for a while the political asperities abated, the differing parties vanished, and the warring sections of society forgot or dissembled their mutual hatred. The exhortations of the Mayor were printed on cards, sold as mottoes, or decorated the pages of the newspapers. He was regarded as a martyr, a sacrifice to the turbulence and wickedness of agitators and plotters, and, with something like a religious ecstacy the people idolized his memory, and before the adoration, clamor, discord, petulance and envy, for a time, hid themselves.A wonderful peace prevailed, and the magnificent metropolis turned to its avocations or its pleasures with unrestrained energy and delight.

Mr. Briggs, who now worshipped the memory of his master, became attached to a newspaper, and furnished periodic outbursts of Cassandra-like prophecy over the dark future which threatened society at large, and while they, when read aloud, had a portentous sound, and a certain rhetorical ornamentation with enough penetration to make them significant, they were generally wisely put aside as sophomoric and unnecessary. Mr. Briggs brought the Mayor’s mother to his home, and this partnership proved wonderfully consoling to the afflicted lady. Mr. Briggs lived in one of the charming colonial restorations of the Greenwich village area of the city, and maintained—though a bachelor—an establishment of considerable dignity.

Of course Tom Ratler survived his encounter with Poni, and seized the fruits of his popularity with the gentle and pretty Jessie Longtinsky and she discovered upon the death of her father, that a moderate competence was hers. The conspirators—at least the formidable Bomber, Madder, Putkof, and Poni himself—were put behind the bars for a practically life period, and no ingenuities of law were expended in proving the injustice of this arrangement. As to the rest, hundreds of whom were under arrest, a general amnesty was proclaimed, and the prisoners who had brought about the famous trial, all, except Polinck, were discharged; Polinck being reserved for another trial. In this general dispensation of urbanity and moderation none rejoiced more than Helen Lorimer.She undertook the education and advancement of the abnormal child Angelo Shimura, and especially she turned to the project she had discussed so long ago with the Mayor—her Museum—and which now might become a sort of memorial of the man himself—with almost religious ardor.

The question of the classes still remained unsettled, and Helen Lorimer as the apostle of the essential necessity of a <i>classified</i> Society, with the avenues of promotion fully created through education, and with emphasis laid upon the essential mutual interaction of all parts of Society with particular reference to the indispensable patriotism and benevolence—in a large and economic sense—of the upper classes, began again her instructions, and readings, and writings. For a time, Amato felt her intellect put aside the claims of her emotions or else—WAS IT, that in this outburst of energy and propagandist zeal she sought again in some esoteric way, the companionship of the man she deeply mourned. The splendid magnanimity of Rudolpho found no fault with this. There was a little hurt, a little uneasiness and dismay perhaps, but the gentleness of his nature, the fine heroism of his feelings, quickly restored his equanimity and he followed at her side, encouraged all her attempts, aided her designs, and so patiently and wisely obeyed her wishes that he entered deeper and deeper into her affections, and captivated her anew by the beauty of his unselfishness.It was a few weeks after the death of the Mayor that a most unexpected occurrence brought the man’s personality again vividly forward and reawakened in Helen Lorimer the half forgotten plan to strengthen the religious life of the country by establishing the Pope within it. The latter project had momentarily lost its interest, though with Cardinal Fenton, who now fully understood the inevitable union of his nephew and Miss Lorimer, it had only suffered a temporary suppression. Conditions indeed in Europe became more and more insupportable for the Church. The exorbitant so-called civilization with the still surviving colossal selfishness, viciousness and hollowness of the representatives of inherited privilege, had produced through two opposite influences an almost total repudiation of religion, looked at in large way, and as representing a national feeling.

First the strong socialistic tendencies—verging into radical phases contemplating the extermination of Society as constituted—denied the tenets of formal religion, and advised its abolition; second, the sensual and sensational inclinations of the upper classes had reached ruinous proportions. They amounted to a moral devastation. Europe, advanced in the methods of enlivening life by exciting appetite, had also become eclipsed in its intellectual aspects by the hebetude that succeeds satiety, and religion was laughed at or cultivated as a cloak to deceive the herd. The tabernacles were empty, the priests were useless, the Pope was impoverished, the prestige of the Church had vanished.Cardinal Fenton realized sorrowfully that in the protestant Teutonic nations religion yet held its sway more rigorously, than in the former homes of his own church; that Russia, somehow barricaded within a wall of barbarous autocracy and childish slavish superstitions maintained its quondam religious expression almost unchanged. To him and an advanced group of Catholic prelates in Europe and America a surmise that American influences somehow established the Church, renovated and revivified it had become a conviction. To them for some years the dream of bringing the Pope to America, of—the language was decorously veiled in its apparent significance—liberating him from the somnolent antiquities, the enthralling traditions, the half ludicrous isolation, or, to quote language used less obsequiously, a hundred years before, by Father McGlynn, of putting the Pope in the streets of New York with a stove-pipe hat on his head, had become more and more alluring.

Here in America the Church had shuffled off the encumbering follies which had grown up within her through a constant association with a system of privilege, of monarchy, of the artifices of title and place. In America, in a sweet exhilarating air of free institutions, universal opportunity, and amid fresh pure aspirations for happiness, the Church forgot cabals and intrigues and politics, and generated itself in a region apart, in worship, good deeds, lofty teachings, sacramental offices, and enveloped, at least by implication ALL, under the aegis of its divine commission. Its personnel became saturated with sense and sympathy, and no more useful men than its priests could be found in practical charity, educational enthusiasm, and broad philosophic erudition. Protestantism had almost protested itself out of existence.To this New World let the Pope come. It seemed to men like Fenton, the last chance, the last entrenchment wherein the incessant fight against Negation might be fought. And to pious Catholic patriots it seemed that so good a possession as the Pope belonged to their own dear country. On the other hand it meant the uprooting of an established identification of Rome and the church of two thousand years, something almost appalling to conceive of. Should the arc of that covenant be ruthlessly touched—even though it shook and tottered—by the modern Uzzahs of this movement?

Yet many believed the violent disruption desirable. As in a sedentary organism parasitic things, mere unavailing accidents had become encysted in the outward tissue of the Church, enfeebling it. These would be dislodged, shaken off, and left behind. They had been debilitating and even malign. Tone, a freshened vigor, a stronger, readier, adaptive stage to the new outlooks in the New World would succeed, or by another imagery, the barque of the Church launched upon a broader, brighter sea would free itself of old wrecks, floating debris, the relics of mistakes, collisions, abandoned cargoes, the barnacle growths of centuries, and sail forward to new ports with certainty and power. The encrustations of foolish practice the mere hindrances of time-honored but stupid devotions—as that of the Bambino—would be swept away, and the Church more intellectually more healthfully, with a freer scope of thought, and with No Loss of essential dogma would confront her enemies, her critics, the whole antagonistic World, still championing the claim of her divine authority.Helen Lorimer and Cardinal Fenton had often talked over all these things; for years the latter cherished the idea with a foolish fervor. Gradually the number of adherents grew. It was recognized as an inner illuminated circle, and without any formal pledges, secrecy was self imposed and sternly guarded. Entrance to the College of Cardinals at Rome had been effected, and with every renewed outburst of hostility to the Pope, not only were the convictions of the sympathizers strengthened but new converts were gained. At one time, the moment alluded to in a former chapter when Helen Lorimer and Amato met in the library of the Cardinal, the approaching crisis seemed imminent and the little Society of the Americanists, as its members called themselves, was transported with hopeful expectations.

But disappointment—a crushing reversal of all their dreams followed. The Pope caught ear of the revolutionary scheme, denied and denounced it. It became doubtful whether or not some sort of Bull might not threaten the extermination of the cult, and put on it the stigma of heresy. With this danger hanging over them everything became still, and the frightened colleagues no longer ventilated their hopes, or even indulged in the precarious pleasure of consoling each other. Cardinal Fenton bided his time. His knowledge of the crumbling state of the European religious institution was too large and keen to permit him to doubt some eventual catastrophe.Helen Lorimer plunged into her humanitarian and civil schemes. Amato said nothing. He was her faithful hand servant, always ready, always sympathetic, ever gracious, loving, willing and enchanting. So the thought of the Pope’s Importation, as it was half ironically referred to—had apparently been forgotten when the thing happened which again, by some peculiar reasoning, brought it back compellingly into Helen Lorimer’s mind; and almost immediately something else happened in Europe that actually won for it toleration and final accomplishment there.

In America the momentous influence with Miss Lorimer was a posthumous publication of a little book by the Mayor—the MS was found among his papers by Mr. Briggs—entitled MORE LIGHT. It created a sensation. None read it more excitedly than Helen Lorimer, Cardinal Fenton, and Amato. Helen detected in it some of her own ideas literally reproduced, and saw plainly a deeper influence pervading it, which she knew was hers also. The book made the Mayor live again before her eyes and live even more strangely and despotically within her heart. Amato knew this, and a sharp pain sprang through his being but he persisted with the same admirable temper, and no reproach rose to his lips, not even a trace of the almost insupportable knowledge could be discerned in his words, or his behaviour. As ever he did her bidding with incomparable zeal.

The book began with an eloquent and convincing picture of the World’s distress, the aimlessness, fragility, hopelessness, pettiness of life in millions upon millions of human beings, the astonishing and apparently purposeless fecundity of the race, the silence of the grave, the depravity and meanness of mere living—and then in chapters,fervid with wonder and despair it pointed out the extraordinary inadequacy of Revelation, even when supplemented by all the authority and beauty of the Church—and it closed with a thrilling imploring appeal to the shut and silent skies, urging the whole Earth through its manifold organs of utterance to upbraid and beseech those skies, to force their mocking canopy of blue to open and let down new vessels of Consecration and of Knowledge. It was well conceived, skillfully graded in a progression of intense feeling to its climax, and it enlisted a lyric power of expression which caught the reader in the broad and noble flow of its imagery and emotion.

Helen Lorimer had read the book aloud to her Mother—still unwell—and to Amato in the great library of her home. It was a room the reader has not entered, and it was the chief treasure of the marvelous palace. It lay in a northern section of the mansion with its roof upheld by columns of Numidian marble. The ceiling in groined vaults sprang from capital to capital and each vault held the golden picture of a constellation set in a blue background. The book-cases in square rose-wood boxes encased the columns at their bases; only at two points around the room did the book-cases rise higher than four feet, and here on both the east and west walls latticed shelves carried the books almost to the high ceiling. Everywhere upon the low cases stood marble busts and Barye bronzes. The walls were fairly covered by paintings, though they might easily have been increased in numbers without exhausting the ample space. Windows pierced the walls above the book-cases on the sides, nowhere reaching the floor, and only one entrance permitted access to this generous study.The north end of the library was surrendered to the installation of a gigantic fireplace built of a soft sienna terra-cotta brick, with a high broad marble mantle above it, on which was placed a marble <i>replica</i> of the Hudson-Fulton Water Gate, while over that on the wall above hung an entrancing painting of the East River in the Morning when the pearly exhalations of the dawn, the smoke of a propeller and the projecting cliffs of the bridge-piers and high buildings, with the tracery of the bridge cables and roads compose one of the most wonderful metropolitan pictures on the entire earth.

Sheraton chairs and tables were pleasantly disposed throughout the room, and on the tables were objects of interest and beauty. The floor was covered from end to end and from side to side with a deep cramoisy-red carpet that gave luxurious warmth to the whole room; green tapestries framed the windows, and the illumination was achieved by opaline globes from the apex of each groined vault while on the tables burned oil lamps of captivating designs in glass and brass. The room was almost too grandiose, too public and expositional in character, but its beauty was really remarkable and the first impression on entering it was memorable. The books were not numerous, many book-shelves were empty; the lowness of the book-cases precluded a very large collection.

The winter had grown cold and now, at the end of February a heavy snow mantled the Inwood ridge, and Carrara amid its leafless trees rose from the dazzling surface, an indifferent rival of its purity.The wind drove through the ranged mansions, the bleak highway was deserted, but at night, as ever, enhanced in its strange loveliness, the electrical aureole crowned the huge metropolis, this monumental city-state where hurrying feet sought work and pleasure, and vice and devotion and labor followed their designed and inevitable tasks.

Julieta, Helen Lorimer, and Amato sat in front of the great hearth and the eyes of two watched the scurrying wisps of blue and yellow light moving waywardly over the blocks of coal. The third—Helen—was somewhat behind the other two at a round table reading. The last sentence of the Mayor’s book was heard; it ran, “the Revelation, the churches, our spirits—to-day the sole witnesses to God—move in regions of persuasion exorbitantly feeble for the purposes of Conviction in this matter, and, through the avenues of prayer, by which all our needs should receive consideration, we ask, or may ask for a SIGN.

“If God answers, the world emerges into a welcome light of reassurance, and, if there is no answer, then seizing, as it were the reins of things ourselves, let society resolve into new units of action, in conformity with the demands of an utilitarian UTOPIA.”

The voice was clear and penetrating with an indefinable sweetness in it that thrilled Amato—as it had often thrilled the dead writer of these pregnant words. There was a silence, and again the voice was heard:

“Those are sad, they sound to me as dreadful words. It is the expatriation of Hope, the convulsion of despair, and that means the Death of the Soul.If only those who ask so much could FEEL; their questions would then die on their lips.”

“No,” answered Amato, “perhaps not. The thought is plain enough and as he—the author puts it—it does not really seem so unreasonable. It has a noble ring after all.”

“But,” continued Helen, “the SIGN is here. Surely you, Rudolpho Amato”—a start brought the listener’s face around towards the speaker, and it was filled with an almost strained attention—“know there can be for Us, you and me”—Amato’s eyes grew tender and grateful—“but one sign. It is the CHURCH; THAT IS THE COVENANT. To go beyond it, outside of it, away from it, is to stumble heedlessly, and to stumble too, unforgiven and unhelped”; it almost seemed to Amato that the voice grew a little hard. And yet at that very moment his own heart leaped with a great joy—it meant Helen’s return to him from the personified Memory that had so weirdly and just perceptibly separated them.

Amato looked a minute longer at the wonderfully beautiful and earnest woman and then turned to the dancing flickers before him, saying:

“Are honest doubts to be so harshly punished?”

“It is the LAW,” quickly answered the woman, and in her eagerness she rose, the Mayor’s book closing behind her on the table; “we cannot extricate ourselves from our own conditions: we are not permitted to criticise them. It is not a question of whether this is the best possible world.Rather is it a question, is this a world at all?—are we here—is anything a reality? If one allows himself to drop into the bottomless pit of Negation, HE IS LOST. Faith—faith only is the solvent of doubt and—it must be either Faith or doubt. Yes, there can be works, good works, this Man”—the voice paused an instant—“was a worker, a hard, belligerent, noble worker—works it seems to me attest to some sort of faith—but as far as the possibility of deliverance from this Order of things appears, it is only through Union with God—IN THE CHURCH”; something then happened—who can say what it was—Amato turned—the woman had sunk back into her chair—her head lay in her hands upon the table and she was sobbing. In an instant—so rapid was he that his figure passing through the room might have seemed almost moved by thought—Amato was kneeling at her side, his hand pressed upon her shoulder. “Helen?” he said.

The face lifted—a smile broke through the tears—she stood up and drew him to her—and then—she turned and left the room. Amato watched her with his hands pressed to his breast; he looked towards Julieta—she was fast asleep.

The next day the morning papers brought a new astonishment into thousands of households throughout the New World. The Vatican had been assaulted—an armed mob had invaded the gardens of the Pope’s palace, and the Pope himself had retreated to St. Peter’s wherein he was literally barricaded, while the troops of the state remained on the defensive at its doors. The event in itself was astonishing enough, but the most serious feature of the situation was the temporizing, supine, almost approving policy of the government. Catholics were terror stricken at the almost inconceivable predicament. This news was received on Saturday.The next morning at the Cathedral of St. Patrick’s, Cardinal Fenton rose in the pulpit and to an enormous audience, among whom sat Helen Lorimer and Amato, delivered a sermon of marvellous skill and boldness. The note of defiance rose in it at times, to be succeeded by invocations to the loyalty of believers, and to be followed by denunciations of Europe as brutal, perverted sterilized, and hopeless. Then came passages of extraordinary novelty. The speaker dwelt upon the forlorn and debilitating position of the Church in Europe—enveloped in a false civilization, of privilege, of title, of social selfishness, turpitude, and callous heartlessness; he pointed out the enfeebling influence of the association through centuries, with the prejudices, misconceptions, and artificial structure of a society that through interior decay presented only the glazed and ornamented shell of civilization, and was decomposing into some final product of sensuality and atheism.

Then he turned his contemplative gaze upon America, strong in its immense material power, resolute in its support of its original contentions in civic life, fortified beyond belief by the average elevation of the masses who found in it peace, education, competence, and opportunity. Yet here again the note of complete confidence wavered and there was betrayed a deep misgiving. The recent socialistic or anarchistic outbreak was analysed as the ebullition of hatred and envy as also the symptom of a deep-seated disorder—the false theory of the State as a collective organism with indisputably no individualistic rights, a formless mass of industrial units, animated by a vacuous sentiment of brotherhood, and destitute of the power of real and vitalizing progress, because the Individual was thwarted in his ambitions and denied his preeminence.He paused a moment to dwell on popular vices, on indulgences, on luxury, on the growth of pernicious habits, on the schism of self-destruction, and then quoting an old author of the Nineteenth Century—George Santayana—he proceeded “if a noble and civilized democracy is to subsist, the common citizen must be something of a saint and something of a hero. We see therefore how justly flattering and profound and at the same time, how ominous was Montesquieu’s saying that the Principle of Democracy is VIRTUE.”

From this point on to his peroration Cardinal Fenton pressed the contention that the Church was indeed the safe-guard, and vindication of Virtue; the terms he presented were inexorable, the consequences of their rejection disastrous. But with again startling emphasis he predicted the approach of the last conflict between Satan and God. He urged upon grounds of sanity and strategy and experience that the Church entrench herself, gathering in her forces, concentrating her energy, establishing her prestige within the United States of America where alone to-day, he concluded “her beauty is most ravishing and irresistible, her efficacy most transcendental, her sanctuaries most honored” and then in rushing winged sentences he plead for the Translation of the Pope—of the Vicar of Christ—“to these western shores where with the Faithful he shall yet defend this foot-stool of the Most High against the swarming emissaries of Hell.”

The sermon was literally reproduced in the great dailies, indeed printed copies of it were despatched over the entire country throughout the larger cities, and in a moment, in the interval between the setting of one sun and the rising of the next, the reflective mind of the nation confronted an extraordinary proposition.Censure, approval, comment and derision met it, but by some psychological sequence, it became an absorbing and interesting question that assumed greater and greater reality. Helen Lorimer’s apathy vanished. She seized anew upon the discarded hope, and somehow it worked harmoniously with her repudiation of the claim or plea made in the Mayor’s book. The Pope established in America should be the Sign—no other; in the displacement of his two thousand year’s residence in Rome, in the abolition of an ancient glory, in the new growth and sanctification of the Church in this free land—in these things should be seen the Hand of God. Amato was no less enthusiastic. He worked at the same problem and exerted all his art and fluency to win converts.

The sensation caused by the Cardinal’s sermon was even greater in Europe than in America where the custom of free speech was recognized. Abroad its impact upon the formalized ears of the dignitaries of the church was stunning. And yet there was no resentment. Dismay and consternation spread, but not in remonstrance, only in a heart breaking attitude of amazement that this extremity should be so boldly risked. And deeply working, the leaven of this suggestion—not unknown before to many, even well considered by many, as we have seen in Amato’s report to the Cardinal—was converting the heart of the POPE himself.

Suddenly there appeared a bull—<i>Ecclesia renovata et migratoria</i>—and the edict was issued that the Pope would abandon Europe, walk out of Rome, and transfer the seat of his See to the shores of America. Amazing contradiction, when a few months before, this same procedure had been anathematized. But Inconsistency is the secret of Progress.The dilemma was sternly stated, the whole bull was minatory and yet, in places it was tearful with lamentations, which in rolling Latin had a most august solemnity. It was a moment of violent transition. The bull, unannounced, unreviewed, calmly thrown into the faces of the Propaganda, and the Congregation awoke no terrible opposition. Cardinal Fenton by a stroke, had won. The position in Europe had become unbearable. But it was an act of superb renouncement, of stupendous humiliation; the emptying of its Glory by the Head of the Church; the stripping bare to flesh and bone of the notorious and priceless apparel which time, and devotion, and wealth, had dressed him in; for it was ordained that He come to America as a Man among men—a world-traveller seeking a new home, as of one who had nowhere to lay his head. It was indeed a prostration, a vast penance, a dislocation that at last severed the Church from its awful past, a past superb in its spiritual conquests, but a past also black with sins.

The house was made ready. It crowned the low hills of Staten Island. Everything was provided by Helen Lorimer. She hastened every preparation, with pride, with gayety, with love—for the pact had been made between her and Amato that the Pope should marry them. At last it seemed to Amato that the Mayor no longer ruled in her heart; the conflict was quite passed, and he—he alone possessed her.

And the Pope came. He came in analogy with the whole wonderful and incredible procedure. He came in conformity with just the requirement which the jealous and suspicious temperament of the country he visited, required. He came as a priest with priests, without pomp, or retinue, or vestments.The affair had been anticipated with a new delight by hundreds of thousands who rejoiced in sensations, in spectacles, in violent surprises, in the climaxes of public events. And it had been most curiously regarded by that protestant portion of the society, which, a hundred years before, would have been thrown into paroxysms of revolt, consternation, and rage, at the mere thought of the transference of the Roman See to this country. Now, at a time when spiritual power among their churches seemed so near extinction, they hailed it with pleasure, with the most cordial congratulations, and with actual endorsement. This very attitude had been in a way expected by Cardinal Fenton, who saw how immensely it ameliorated the abrupt Transition on both sides of the religious fences.

The former of these groups—those who looked for some monumental display of ceremony—was disappointed. It was all so quiet, so informal, so commonplace, that except for its momentous moral significance, it hardly stirred the surface of events with a perceptible disturbance. Nothing could have been less phenomenal or less memorable. Art and calculation had combined to make its impression, as a physical event, almost imperceptible. But the expatriation of the Pope was an extraordinary fact, and no suppression of external show could minimize its stupendous implications. It indeed marked a crisis in the religious life of the whole world.

The Pope left Rome <i>incognito</i>, traversed France, embarked at Havre, reached New York, was received by a committee, itself so inconspicuously constituted, as scarcely to receive notice in the public prints, and taken in a closed carriage to the modest mansion,adjoining a small but beautiful chapel—the whole installation devised by Helen Lorimer and Cardinal Fenton, and its expense wholly defrayed by her—on the highest hill of Staten Island.

There was really something quite abnormal and disillusionizing in it all. The Pope had even for the time divorced himself from the appurtenances of his immense establishments. The Index, the Inquisition, the Propaganda, the vast complicated machinery of extension and distribution remained at Rome. The Pope alone appeared at the threshold of the New World, coming without any artifice of show and pageantry. It had something evangelical and primary about it, and touched the imagination favorably. A wave of affection went out to the prelate, and the absence of rebuke or injurious derision marked a subtle influence propitious to the Cardinal’s project. The personality of the Pope himself helped this delightful result. He was charming, and tactful, and intellectually strong. He had already said beautiful and true things about America. He had captured her attention, and approval, long ago. Protestant bigotry restrained itself, and the sightseers and the indifferent wondered, smiled and applauded. It was all excellent throughout, and Pope Sextus celebrated his first Mass in America without remonstrance, without disturbance, almost without comment. He was indeed a Bernard of Clairvaux.

Later the inevitable happened. That vast insatiable appetite for the marvelous, and the recondite, and ritualistic, with its tremendous possibilities of satisfaction, which the Catholic Church nurtured, demanded its conditioning approval.It could only be given on one assumption, that the resources gathered through ages of ceremonial development, magnified by the most modern auxiliaries of light and music should be employed to memorialize and establish the monstrous revolution. Unless the seal of the Supernatural rested upon it, its triumph would be temporary and crumbling. Thus, spontaneously and together, the Catholic congregations throughout the country prepared a glorious and stupifying Jubilee. In it the ardor of devotion, the zeal of faith, and the pride of sect united with a discernible prophetic instinct, to make it unparalleled. An incredible skill controlled it. Cardinal Fenton and his agents were everywhere, bringing the products of thought, of eloquence, of allusion, to be inwrought and perpetuated through the myriad-tongued utterances of its sermons and addresses. And in the midst of it with the most thrilling nobility of style the POPE appeared at St. Patrick’s Cathedral, as its cause and inspiration.

Pope Sextus was a peculiar man, shaped in a mould of mental, moral, and physical perfection; perhaps the only Pope, that ever ruled the Church, who could have taken this precarious step, a step threatening such monstrous revulsions, and so inevitably endangered by collapse, ridicule, the extinction of personal prestige as the Pope separated himself from seclusion, and all the veils of splendid and historical accidents. For once the MAN stood out before the world in the habiliments of the street, as it were. He was not found at the end of long corridors, mysteriously guarded by chamberlains and soldiers, fixed beneath a canopy, and surrounded by the marvels of art. He had abdicated all that, and his PRESENCE was in the open. It was an ordeal, a shock that only the rarest combinations of qualities could meet and survive. And the Pope met it and survived.This was the magnificent fact that brought a profound ecstacy to Cardinal Fenton, to Helen Lorimer, to Amato, and the leaders of this bold innovation. But the Pope himself—he alone—made it possible.

A reporter of the New York Times, who attended the Pope’s Mass at the Cathedral, thus described him: “The Pope seems young, but that impression is dissipated when his bearing, action, voice, and eloquence are considered. At first I could only think of the St. Gabriel depicted by Guido where the young and stalwart archangel descends with his sword upon the head of the falling Satan. His type is certainly not blond, yet it is noticeably less dark than the typical Italian. His hair is brown or chestnut, luxuriant, and in light rolling waves surmounts a brow of fine proportions, in a countenance of the most pronounced beauty. It is quite impossible to describe the power of his eyes which appear always soft and tender, and yet have a peculiar indefinable penetration. He is utterly apart from the conventional pope, with a skull cap, and no hair, diaphanous features, and the filminess of ascetic depletion, imparting to him a ghostly sacredness. The Pope is probably fifty; the first impression of youth is effaced as you watch him, and, above all, as you hear him.

“The service was a pontifical mass grandly rendered, the music subdued, and full prominence was given to the celebrant, who was the Pope himself. It was a lesson in the majesty and music of the human voice. Never (and as a catholic I constantly attend mass), have I heard singing so moving and ideal. But that again was forgotten, when the superb figure—the Pope is tall—rose in the pulpit, and the same entrancing voice preached from the Text; ‘Lo I am with you Always.’
