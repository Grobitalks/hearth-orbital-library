“John Bunn to the chair,” called out the clerk after prompting by Mr. Meldexter. The man whom Angelo had pointed out as the janitor of the ill fated Settlement House, rose and took the seat of the witness, and also the oath. He was an ordinary type of serving man, perhaps a German, stolid, unmoved by his surroundings and uncompromisingly serene.

“John Bunn you are, or were, the janitor of the lately destroyed Settlement House were you not?”

“Yees Sir I vas.”

“How was it that the house over which you had charge was left open on the night of its destruction—or Sir—please be careful in answering—” Mr.Moser had risen partly to object, “or was it open that night at all?”

The man folded his arms, leaned back, and stared at the lawyer, as if to assure him that he was ready and able to answer the question, and in no way disturbed at the consequences—and, as he began his answer, the figure of Theodore Lorimer stepped forward in the aisle, and something straining and anxious came into his eyes. Few failed to recognize that the question might prove critical, and Mr. Moser stood at his chair as if to intercept some unexpected blow.

“It vas offen.”

“Were you there on the night of the burning?”

“I vas not.”

“How Sir, if the Settlement House was in your custody was it left open and you not there?”

John Bunn was silent for a moment. He too had caught sight of Mr. Lorimer standing now much farther down the aisle, so far indeed that persons afterwards averred he raised his hand slightly, and as if to catch the attention of the witness, and opened and closed it slowly. The witness hesitated; he began to speak, and looking from the lawyers to Mr. Lorimer and back again at the lawyers seemed in deep perplexity. At that instant a court attendant touched Mr. Lorimer on the shoulder motioning him to a seat.But that gentleman passed again to the rear of the court room, and, with his withdrawal John Bunn recovered his voice.

“Eet vas dees vay. I leef de House offen because I vas a—<i>told to leef it offen</i>.”

“Who told you to leave it open?” came the remorseless question. Amato and Miss Lorimer leaned forward; here was the suspected play of the element in her own ranks that had helped—or wished to help, for its own purposes—the revolutionary agitators. It had never been understood why the House was unprotected the night of its assault. The janitor—this same John Bunn—had refused to explain his absence, and he had been summarily discharged—in fact he had soon absented himself from the city; he could not be found, and his appearance here was a surprise to Miss Lorimer, to Mr. Moser. To the question of Mr. Meldexter the man returned a fixed stare almost as if he did not understand him. Mr. Meldexter repeated the question, and added “Miss Lorimer gave you no such order?” Again no answer, only the stare and a stubborn shrugging of the shoulders. The District Attorney approached the witness, and raised his finger threateningly; “Do you refuse to answer this question?”

The man turned to the judge who had wheeled round in his chair to notice him, and said “Must I answer?” The judge nodded. The man recovered his equanimity; he had made up his mind. “No Mees Lorimer gif me no such ordee. Mr. Theodore—he gif it to me—”Amato turned to look at his companion. She was pale, so very pale that the young man thought he should ask her to leave the room. But she refused to go; her clenched hands were pressed to her bosom, her wan look told her distress, and her lips compressed, as if almost to stifle a cry of misery, were also pale. Quickly she released one hand and laid it upon Amato’s, and half moaned; “Wait.”

The answer of John Bunn was a bomb that shattered some theories and raised many questions. The answer incidentally was accompanied by the scarcely apparent opening and closure of the court room door and the disappearance of Mr. Lorimer himself. Perhaps but one pair of eyes noticed his inaudible exit, those eyes were in the head of John Bunn. Mr. Moser had his turn of astonishment, and Mr. Meldexter observed his confusion with satisfaction. It half looked if this evidence was to be trusted that Mr. Theodore Lorimer—a leader in the rich and aristocratic circles of the city, a notable in <i>Carrara</i>—was himself an accomplice, and the thought imparted to Mr. Moser a momentary shock. What revelations might be expected from Longtinsky, where was the trail leading?

“Mr. Bunn” it was the sharp voice of the district attorney, “did you receive an order from Mr. Lorimer by word of mouth or in writing?”

“Mister Lorimer come to mee and say so.”

“Did Mr. Lorimer offer you any inducements for this betrayal of your trust?”

“Vat you meen? Money?”Mr. Moser had recovered his senses, he was on his feet shouting “Objected to, immaterial, incompetent, and irrelevant.”

“Sustained,” hesitated the judge, adding, “at least at present.”

Again Mr. Meldexter; “Did you see the prisoners at the bar enter the Settlement House on the night when it was burned down?”

“No Sir I deed not. I vas not there,” almost shouted the witness.

“Did you know why the House was to be left open; did Mr. Lorimer say why you should leave it unprotected?”

An objection was made and sustained; Mr. Lorimer at present was not on trial. Mr. Meldexter handed the witness over to Mr. Moser who, after a moment’s inspection of the man, declined to cross-examine. The evidence had no bearing on the guilt or innocence of the men at the bar. But to Mr. Meldexter it served the vital purpose of <i>revenge</i>; if Miss Lorimer, through her attorney secured the release of the men previously condemned, he—Mr. Meldexter—had fastened a dangerous suspicion upon her family, as collusive in the desperate act for which they were tried. Would Miss Lorimer care to see the matter through to its bitterest results? Already a court summons had been made out for the appearance of Mr. Theodore Lorimer as witness; <i>Mr. Meldexter wanted him</i>.

More policemen were called and a renewed effort made to show that the prisoners were caught red-handed—as it were—coming out of the burning House,but this evidence was somewhat shaken by Mr. Moser’s cross-examination, whereby he showed a great difference in the opinions of the witnesses as to where exactly they had seen the men. It gave the impression of great confusion at the time, and made it clear that of the many men who had been seen running from the House many might have been in the place for purposes of rescue and assistance. The boy Shimura had made it pretty plain that Longtinsky and Polinck were the perpetrators of the deed and the others the merest stool pigeons.

Finally came the time for the summing up, and the day closed with the promise of both sides that they would complete their pleas the next day in time for the judge to make his charge and for the surrender of the case to the jury. Slowly with much whispering and open comment, the great throng dispersed. Five left the court room with strange conflicting thoughts. They were Helen Lorimer, Mr. Moser, Mr. Briggs, Tom Ratler, and Poniatowsky. Helen Lorimer thought she saw the treacherous hand of her uncle in another matter perhaps dearer to her than the safety of the Settlement House; Mr. Briggs was dumbfounded with a new sensation of danger to the commonwealth; Tom was thinking of making away with Jessie now that her father was so desperately involved in the thickening perils; and Poniatowsky saw the moment near at hand when his ripened plans must be put in motion. Mr. Moser felt the pressure of an ominous dread.Surely of these five Miss Lorimer was to be pitied. The stunning disclosure—although she could scarcely believe it—had wounded her beyond all estimate. The revolting meanness of it, the shameless perfidy, the cruel forgetfulness and wickedness of it, the harsh sense of betrayal and desertion—Oh! how it made her value the earnest love of her friend; she turned to him now as a protector, as something golden and true, loyal and unswerving—these things stung and wearied her.

She was silent almost all through their return to Carrara, and when her home was reached she asked if her uncle was there. The servant told her Mr. Lorimer had left in the morning and had returned an hour before. Amato waited at her side and she turned and said, “You will take dinner with us?” and then, as if in an impulse of affection, “Please do.” Amato felt her grief. His own heart bled for her, and his indignation rose steadily as he contemplated the heartlessness that had wounded her—and yet it seemed all so incredible—perhaps a mistake. He told her so. She thanked him and together they went up the marble stairway, leading to the stately dining-room, and there—coming towards them with a hand valise in his hand, a summer overcoat thrown over his arm was—<i>Theodore Lorimer</i>.

For one instant Helen Lorimer surveyed him, not with entreaty on her lips, not with expostulation, not with protesting confidence. She KNEW, that moment when their eyes encountered, and he shrank like a cowering animal from her searching inquiry, that he was GUILTY, that he knew moreover that he had been revealed, that his name had been published before the world as a culprit and a traitor—the Brother of her Father. SHE KNEW IT.Nay; she knew more; he contemplated flight; he was about to become a fugitive. It was best so. That house, that city, would be too small now for him and her. Let him be forgotten. Oh!—but her divination ran further, deeper—it was he that separated from her the man she had loved; his pride, his arrogance, his love of caste, and now how abject he seemed! It was amazing how much she understood that moment, as if the fire of some supernatural revelation had flashed upon him, and laid him bare to her. There are such psychologic moments. Every one has experienced them.

Amato watched her. It was wonderful to see the muteness of her pain. No word, only that fixed gaze, with a rising color in her cheeks. And Mr. Lorimer said nothing. He fled before that stern silent indignation. He was eager to get away, eager to escape a public ordeal, that would have strangely humiliated him before his own niece, before the man whom he had expelled from that house, because he felt his own blood was—<i>a better sort!</i>

And yet there might have been explanations. Theodore Lorimer had acted according to a principle. He was not a bad man in the moral sense. His business instincts and his career in business had been unimpeachable. His kindness to his sister and niece had been perfect. He was the victim of a conservative and class pride. He had thought to destroy the vicious activity of the radicals, to save his family from any further pernicious—as he deemed it—sympathy with the Abyss, to kill for once and all his niece’s infatuation to regenerate the slums. It was a foolish deed; but not incomprehensible. He had played a dangerous game and lost.The destruction of the Settlement House had not disturbed Helen Lorimer’s determination to keep on at her work, and the men—to be dealt with summarily, innocent or guilty, as Lorimer thought, for an example—<i>were to escape</i>. The whole fabric of his weak, unstable, and cruel plan had failed. He had not been alone; he had confederates, abettors, men of the same stamp of feeling; men with the same egoistic idea. One shred of honor remained to him. He would not expose them. He would take the burden of his dishonor and bare the execrable shame of it, <i>alone</i>.

The man hurried down the broad stairway followed by Helen who stepped rapidly forward to the top of the broad descent. At the bottom he turned and looked back at her—and yet no word between them. His face was convulsed with misery. For one moment a tide of compassion swept over the woman gazing there at him, and seeing through him the ulterior ruin of a life. Amato crept forward too, catching the epic intensity of this wordless interview.

The next instant he was gone; an automobile already waited at the foot of the terraces of steps, and in another minute the hoarse creak of the chauffeur’s horn sounded drearily and—as it seemed to Helen—like a human cry distorted into an unintelligible and animal wail. The next she stood at Amato’s side and he lifted her face and saw that it had blanched to the pallor of death. She leaned heavily upon him; she would have fallen; he caught her and led her to a sofa. She smiled at his solicitude, but her great eyes looked at him despairingly—their bitterness of woe made his heart bleed. He felt so helpless!But the strong heart conquered, the buoyant spirit rose from its humiliation, the challenging splendor of her beauty returned, and slowly—slowly—the intrepid resolve to bear all things well, to suffer without murmur, and still to pursue her own noble purposes unchanged, <i>also</i> returned.

She rose and held Amato’s hands a moment in her own; he felt their pressure. A few escaped tresses of hair hung upon her forehead, and the breath from her parted lips came quickly. Her voice was low, and as she spoke she looked upward, so that the white column of the neck revealed its softly blue venations, and the eyelids drooped over beseeching eyes;

“Amato it was Christ’s words ‘<i>In your patience possess ye your Souls</i>.’” She wavered an instant, then she dropped his hands, and now with bowed head, the spread white fingers of her hands pressed upon her breast she left him stunned and sorrowing. He followed her a few steps, but she swept from him noiselessly and quickly. He stood irresolute a few moments, turned, descended the stairway, and left the house.

Later he returned. Helen came to the dinner, and though she was more silent she evinced her usual graciousness; her paleness faintly touched with color added to her strange beauty. She talked with less animation: she seemed to invite his confidences, and tempted him to tell them of his sojourn in Europe.

Julieta remarking the absence of her uncle asked; “Where is Theodore?”The deep eyes gazed a little wearily, Amato thought, at his vacant place and the answer, uttered without a tremor, came from her lips; “Mother, Uncle has left the City.”

<b>CHAPTER XIV</b>

THE GREAT RIOTS

It was impossible to admit the crushing mob that besieged the court room the next day. Forensic skill has always appealed to the American mind, and the duel of eloquence and argument, which was expected, would have awakened the curiosity of a far less apathetic public. As it was, the court officers, the lawyers, witnesses, principals, and a few favored auditors—among them Helen Lorimer, Amato, Mr. Briggs—were admitted quietly at another entrance, one leading into halls and exits upon Centre Street, removed from the approaches from Chatham Street and the City Hall.

When the doors at last swung open, a stormy crowd rushed in, and filled to overflowing, in a short time, the large accommodations, and yet beyond the doors clamored and struggled an even larger impatient, unsatisfied and vociferating throng. It required some rude force to eject a section of this crowd which had unadvisedly forced its way into the court room. Amid adjurations, entreaties, threats, and cries of derision, the surplus was expelled; the doors were then closed, and the multitude awaited the approach of the judge.He was announced by a toll upon a bell, and the immense assemblage rose together with the sound of a rising wind, with all those within the rail, standing until the Clerk had repeated his form of words—uttered perhaps at this thrilling moment with more expressive intonations—and the judge had gravely sunk into his chair. A few minutes’ consultation, an uneasy flitting to and fro of clerks and messengers, the bending together of legal heads in conference, the shuffling of papers, momentary short announcements, rulings by the judge, and then Mr. Meldexter arose, and facing the judge, told of his inability to serve a summons upon Mr. Theodore Lorimer, as that gentleman had suddenly absented himself from the city.

The evidence of John Bunn the previous day, as to Mr. Lorimer’s apparent intrigue with the anarchistic demagogues had been fully understood. It created a new, terrible, sensation. To the dangerous plans of Poniatowsky and his associates it proved helpful. It engendered a deeper hatred of the class, which these men said, thus attempted to lure them into a trap and actually prompted them to crime—in order to secure the vindictive denunciation and revenge of society which this same class was supposed to be so adorably devoted to, which it so righteously defended, of which it was the great moral ornament. And to Longtinsky’s views of Miss Lorimer it seemed to add particular force. Was not this Mr. Lorimer her uncle? It was all of a piece. Her pretentious benevolence was a still darker scheme for the extermination of the radicals. Of course such a perversion of meaning became current only among the irreconcilables, but it even shook the confidence of her humble friends.And among the conservatives this exposure produced an effect bordering upon panic. It placed them in a ridiculous position. It allied them with the worst enemies of decency. It overwhelmed them with contempt. The repudiation of Mr. Lorimer’s act and an almost fierce anger for it, appeared in the organs of the conservatives, and Miss Lorimer’s instigation to this was not wanting. The night after her uncle’s flight she had sat up writing and preparing a statement, communicated to the papers for the next day’s issues, disclaiming the act of Mr. Lorimer, and displaying her own sorrow and humiliation that the shameless deed had proceeded from her household. It was a quick, skillful move. It cleared her in the eyes of all honest, right judging men. It met the eyes of the Mayor who had heard of the whole episode in the trial, and heard too later, of Mr. Lorimer’s escape. He held the paper a long time in his hands reading and rereading the statement of Helen Lorimer. He wondered if he might return. HE, TOO, HAD BLUNDERED. On the day of the summing up of the case the Mayor had come back to New York. He was in the City Hall when Mr. Meldexter addressed the court.

It was impossible that some trace of elation should not color his feelings that morning, mingling with a real sympathy for Miss Lorimer and, mingling too with the flaming upward of his love and a better hope.

When Mr. Meldexter made the announcement of Mr. Lorimer’s disappearance there was a considerable straining of necks, and focussing of eyes upon Miss Lorimer and her friend. Amato seemed more conscious of this inspection than Helen. She did not resent the inquisition. She was grave and undisturbed.Many thoughts, far removed from all the occurrences about her kept her mind busy, and perhaps quickened the pulsations of her heart. At any rate the ferreting eyes were not rewarded with any display of confusion, and soon their repulse sought a compensation in the proceedings before them, after a warrant for Mr. Lorimer’s arrest had been signed by the judge.

Then began the struggle. Mr. Moser rose for the defense. His manner was suave, insinuating, and deferential, at first. He pointed out the disadvantage of his position, the inevitable prejudices he confronted, his solicitude for the State’s welfare, and his fixed desire to be just. The word stirred him to a broader flight of rhetoric, and he drew a picture of Justice in a great Commonwealth. The theme offered many advantages of elaboration; it particularly helped him to disarm criticism, when he affirmed the rights of the poorest as well as of the highest before the majesty of the blindfolded Genius that made no distinctions between the master and his servant, in her solemn and righteous adjudication.

Mr. Moser dropped his demeanor of apology. He began to become assertive, a little defiant, and finally scornful. Were these unfortunate men to be hurled into eternity to satisfy the Juggernaut of a remorseless public vengeance? Then came analysis and description; he was merciless in his derision of the police who must find scapegoats for their satisfaction;he poured forth the vials of his wrath upon Longtinsky, dwelling with effective emphasis on his secrecy, his turpitude, his impenetrable selfishness, his preposterous vanity—and then, moving softly from point to point, weaving his allusions with facts, coloring his facts with emotion, he came—as if stupefied by his own discovery—upon the boy—the little Japanese wonder—Angelo Shimura.

Ever cautious and apprehensive he drew a wonderful picture of Japanese mentality, the precocity of their children, the steadiness of their nervous temperament, and then in an entertaining narrative style described his meeting with the marvellous child whose evidence he begged now to rehearse. He had been excellent throughout. Here he surpassed himself. The note of pathos was not overstrained, and the claims of indignation not ignored. He dwelt in corroboration of his belief in the boy upon his absolute consistency—and he was not loth to express his wonder at the singular reticence, so to speak, of the district attorney’s cross-examination of this witness. He depicted in picturesque terms the boy’s strange yet beautiful devotion to his benefactor, and how still more strangely his devotion became the instrument of justice. All depended upon him. Believe him and the innocence of three of the prisoners was proven, the contributory guilt of two others revealed, the absolute conviction of one—the vile instrument of Longtinsky—only possible.He ran over this state of things several times, throwing a softened light upon the unhappy men who had been dragged into the toils and nets of an arch-villain but, turning the glare of his exposure upon the unspeakable Longtinsky. And then when he had thus left on the minds of the jury only the overwhelming impression of this man’s guilt, he swept into a higher region of reflection—and he went there guided by Helen Lorimer, whose own words fell upon her ears with an unexpected freshness. He drew a picture of the State rocking between the struggles of two parties, he dwelt on the dangers of mistakes, the immitigable contest in the advancing process of political change, between a dangerous demagoguery, insane, violent, ruthless, and the preservative principles of property, a stage of civilization attained only through centuries of persistent effort against the confiscatory violence of kings, barons, and conquerors; and now again endangered by a reckless appeal to prejudice and passion, luring on into revolt, the blind forgetful populace, whose own rights and opportunities would disappear in the seething ocean of unrest, anarchism, and spoliation.

Mistakes in justice, the condemnation of the innocent, the surrender to popular clamor of the guiltless, a careless control of our own resentment, would only jeopardize order and bring on a blind mutiny, in which the institutions of society would disappear—destroyed by its own imprudence. The lawyer made his point well, and, advancing to the jury forcibly challenged their own sense of security, if, in this case, they failed to do justice.Mr. Moser was listened to with wonder and delight—he was amazingly successful with the audience. Mr. Meldexter congratulated him as he closed, and as he opened his own case for the people, continued his congratulations with a more formal tribute to the greatness of his rival’s effort. He warned the jury against the blandishments of rhetoric, and asked for their sober unbiassed judgment of the evidence—reiterating slowly their solemn pledges to abide by that for the Truth’s sake! Mr. Meldexter was not an orator, nor did he possess the art of limpid and entertaining discourse, but he was shrewd, raspingly cynical, and bright in argument. He saw of course the point of attack,—the Japanese boy’s story—and on that he expended his skill, against that he launched his batteries of sarcasm; had this remarkably well-drilled child told the truth? Did he care whether he told the truth or not? How did he know that the prisoners had not assisted in setting the House afire? He had left the place with Longtinsky before the explosion. In that time what had happened? Bingo and Fabrizio certainly carried the instruments of destruction to the House, the other men had been sent for, Polinck and they were there together engaged—perhaps with different degrees of intelligence—in the work of destruction. They had been caught by the police red-handed. That they had never been seen by the boy at Longtinsky’s conferences—assuming that he told the truth, assuming even that he was at these conferences at all—proved nothing, least of all their innocence.The facts must be reasonably interpreted, they must be interpreted by Common Sense—no distortion springing from a neurotic fancy, lodged in the deformed body of a Japanese infant should be allowed to distract their—the jury’s—mind. These men were known to Longtinsky, they were birds of one feather; this precious sneak of a boy knew their names and recognized them as members of the plotting fraternity, in which his own improbable romance placed himself. They had been found guilty by the condemnation of one jury of honest and devoted men—let not the sophistry of eloquence nor the depraved insinuations of a diseased and treacherous puerile human freak disturb that conviction, and subject the State to the ridicule and danger of caprice and inconsistency.

Remember this boy so beautifully extolled by the defense; what confidence would honest men put in the story of a self-convicted ingrate. Were Longtinsky and his confederates such assured idiots as to allow this creature full access to their deliberations? Could anything be imagined more improbable? Regard the whole tale in the light of their own experiences as men of the world and see its utter futility!

This destructive criticism fell upon attentive ears also, and was given its due weight, so that when the pleas were finished and the day—which had grown dark and rainy—had well advanced into the afternoon, the audience felt itself uncomfortably incompetent to make up its mind. The same puzzling uncertainty doubtless would afflict the jury, and a disagreement was promptly predicted. Helen Lorimer and Amato felt the singular sway of the adroit counsel, and, except for their anxiety, would have acknowledged it as a new proof of the futility of history. It would have, under other circumstances, amused them.Now they scanned the impassive faces of the jury for some intimation of their secret thoughts.

The next instant the Judge arose and the Jury—rising for a moment from their chairs, which they resumed upon a motion of the Judge’s hand—turned their eyes upon their legal monitor with an expression of profound, almost appealing interest. From his lips they expected the advice and direction that might lead them to a right decision. It seemed almost as if the entire assemblage shared their expectancy. Not a person left the hall, and in a deep silence the solemn utterances of the judge sounded almost weirdly distinct. It was noticed that the prisoners—all except Polinck—evinced an animated attention. Their hearts were not beating with more nervousness than did the heart of the woman who had made herself their champion, and who also realized the critical moment, and to whom the whole episode had its political philosophical consequences, written in large characters before her eyes.

It seemed so evident that the weight the judge would assign to the testimony of the boy Angelo Shimura, would be the pivotal point of the trial that every ear was strained to hear his word at this part of the review. A silence of portentous significance fell upon the great throng as the dull November afternoon darkened, and heavy gusts of wind carrying rain splashed the windows, whence the trickling currents just audibly mingled their murmur with the slowly and almost sternly uttered sentences of the charge.Helen Lorimer’s whole enthusiastic temperament was now aroused. She had taken up the cause of these men with ardor; the trial full of color and situations had excited her imagination, and her heart anxiously—hungrily—yearning for alleviation through her mind’s interests, found in the dramatic progress of the trial a wonderful assistance. She had staked so much upon it; it was in a real way her own splendid effort to avert injustice, and there was a propagandist’s zeal in her industrious interest. She had followed the trial with intellectual fidelity too, and her conferences with Mr. Moser were always helpful to that adroit lawyer.

And—there was something more—there was the faithful sympathy of a friend, the loyal agreement of her handsome companion. This was not an inconsiderable joy. Amato was winning his way, and his patience, his quick insight, his endless ingenuity, above all his sincerity, and the really delicate culture of his mind impressed her, and the impression grew warm with a friendship that it would have really made her sad to break or lose. Amato on his behalf plunged into the trial with zeal. He knew the evidence, argued the points, felt his eagerness increasing day by day to see the end, and responded instantly to all the diversions and evasions of the lawyers.

This mutual enthusiasm brought on an inevitable approach to confidence with Helen Lorimer, and both—one with anticipation, the other without suspicion, at least without analysis—continued it to deeper and deeper consequences. Both had been present each day at the trial, and they had been welcomed by the great crowd as a social feature that added to the trial’s importance.Representatives of all shades of opinion and standing were present, and the trial began somehow to be regarded as a political crisis.

A sudden increase in the rising storm outside, made the judge’s words almost inaudible to those against the wall of the court room, and they engaged themselves in the corridors pressing forward down the aisles until, almost unobserved, each was filled, and curiously the attendants desisted from any effort to clear them. Helen and Amato, the lawyers, the few witnesses within the rail, the jury and prisoners, the multitude, heard these solemn words:

“And the evidence of the Japanese boy Angelo Shimura is crucial. Your verdict must be almost solely determined by the credence you are willing to concede to that testimony. The boy is qualified by age, by evident mental qualifications, to give testimony. Its value is to be determined by its consistency, its resistance to the demolishing attacks of the cross-examination, and by such support as the other evidence in this trial afforded it, or by such contradictions as it has suffered from the same. This evidence is peculiar—” and then carefully, with impartial acumen, the judge took up Angelo’s story piece by piece, pointing out with cold indifference—so Helen Lorimer thought as she felt her heart rise and sink again—that “the circumstance was almost unprecedented of a boy’s precocious constancy, and his secretive habit, binding him to such a patron as Longtinsky, being known—and an inference prejudicial to his veracity might on that account be entertained. And yet again, looked at as evidence, the story had borne examination, it had not been actually contradicted, and remained a substantial factor—indeed the one overpowering feature of the trial.The jury’s duty was to carefully examine this testimony, to absolutely place it in the foreground of their deliberations, and if they believed it their verdict was to be unmistakably one of acquittal at least as far as it exonerated some of the prisoners. But there should be no extension of its application to others.” A few less relevant directions and the grave judge bowed to the jury, and dismissed them—just as the storm outside with sudden fury smote again upon the windows and the startled audience rose to their feet to leave.

And now there was a crush to the doors, and the crowd with oscillations to and fro, began their tumultuous exit. Helen and Amato remained seated. Mr. Moser was with them and invited them to seats within the rail, where other persons had gathered, hoping that perhaps a verdict would be soon reached. This anxiety was shared by a number, and while, for different reasons, they all regarded a decision as probable within an hour or so, it was Helen Lorimer’s intense personal interest that kept her there, persuaded too by Mr. Moser to see the denouement of the great trial.

And while they sat together, talking in low tones, events were happening outside of the Court House which would soon precipitate the greatest social crisis the huge city had ever known. The Mayor had returned to the city as we have recorded. He had followed the daily reports of the progress of the trial, and had timed his return almost to the moment when the opposite counsel began their pleas. It was indeed a little before that, as Mr. Meldexter asked for the arrest of the absent Mr. Lorimer, that Tom Ratler was whirling—the second time that day—the Mayor to the City Hall, where Mr.Briggs awaited him—impatiently walking up and down the historic Governor’s room—staring out of the windows, and muttering words of disgust and anger. His eyes eagerly perused again and again a paper which he rapidly folded and unfolded, and which had the effect of eliciting a running comment:

“Yes, of course—just as I supposed—the blow is well timed—and the troops away!—but it can’t be so serious—a little contraption—a small eruption—and yet, damn it—if the Mayor hadn’t run away—impracticable—head-strong—visionary—we may be in for it now. Ha, here he is!”

The last words were directed to the figure of the Mayor passing up the steps of the Hall, while a portion of the escaping throng from the Municipal Building standing in the slashing rain with outspread umbrellas, watched him for an instant, and then turned and mingled in the running, stooping, dodging lines of people hurrying against the vicious wind and water that slanted the trees, and whirled and spun in silvery threads about Printing House Square, and the tall façades along Park Row, and over the towered structures between Broadway and the Hudson River.

Tom Ratler rushed his machine into a garage on Chamber Street, after being told to be instantly ready for the Mayor’s return. The Mayor burst into the Governor’s room. He met his Secretary, shook his hand and pushed into it a paper which he pulled from his pocket, saying: “I received that this afternoon. What do you think it means? Anything?”As he thrust the unsigned paper into his secretary’s hand the latter saw in a second that the Mayor was the recipient of a warning identical with that he himself had just received and read. The writer of both was undoubtedly the same person; he had addressed one letter to the Mayor at his home, the other had been sent to the Mayor’s secretary. The identical letter was calm in language, and possessed a certain internal stamp of sincerity and veracity from its very calmness. It ran:

“On ... an attempt to overthrow the government of this City will be seriously made. It is not a trivial outburst. It has been well planned, will be well supported, and unless you meet it with force and severity will effect enormous damage to the city. It will begin by an open insurrection and by an attack upon three points of the city—the Municipal Building, Carrara, and the Shipping along the river fronts. The conspirators are well armed. Treachery has supplied them with arms from an armory in this city. It is the trump card-play of Socialism. Red placards throughout the city will announce and defend it. Remember! it begins on the afternoon of....”

And while they thus read the warning, throughout the city in the veil of the storm, the placards were indeed posted, as though sown upon the fences and the sides of building and on the pavements by that tempest that now began ominously to rise and roar around them. Those placards, the effrontery of Longtinsky had already planned, they were printed in the office of The Beehive, they were the frothings of his bittermost hatred and envy, and the malign fury of their denunciations summoned the nether tides of discontent, and the great silent columns of the despairing to break forth into RED REBELLION.The Mayor and Briggs looked at each other for one moment. Something in the face of his secretary convinced the Mayor. Briggs mechanically held up for the examination of his superior the paper he had received. Then the Mayor hurried to his table, saying as he stood there, “At any rate action must be quick. Send all police reserves here. These blind hints must be our guide.

“Telephone the police. Summon out the Fire Department and let them patrol the water front; every man armed; raise the two remaining regiments of militia now in the city—the 69th and the 22d; rush them to Carrara.”

Mr. Briggs jumped to the phone. In another instant the police army would be marshalled. The Fire Department was ordered out pell-mell by the same voice, and it shouted commands to the Colonels of the 69th and 22d regiments to assemble their men within the shortest possible time at their armories. The orders were understood to come from the Mayor—explanation would follow—instant obedience was imperative.

In the meanwhile the Mayor dictated to an assistant a short statement—one was sent on the wings of the wind to the Chief of the Police, another to the Fire Department, and a third and fourth were despatched with lightning speed to the colonels of the regiments. The forces of defence were already acquainted with the nearness of an unknown danger—the machinery of the city worked with instantaneous swiftness—but though summoned the battalions of Order could not instantly be mobilized, nor was the mere notice of danger sufficient to give intelligence to its leaders.The plans of action were unformed. Resistance to an attack was the only clear notion entertained—but attack, Where? How? and When?—; the unsigned paper in his hand was the only clue the Mayor possessed. Had he been recreant, careless, had he suffered his suspicions—frequently appealed to—to sleep, and now was his great charge, the beautiful city of the centuries to undergo disparagement and disaster? The Mayor clenched his hands and sprung to the windows where the raging elements seemed to gather to protect the mysterious treason about them. Tears almost forced themselves to his eyes, and through the tears—somehow palely seen—came the face of Helen Lorimer.

Gazing blindly before him the Mayor suddenly saw the flying figure of a woman mount the City Hall steps, and he divined her mission. Almost the next moment she stood before the Mayor, streaming with the rain, breathless, with pale cheeks framed in the folds of a shawl thrown around her head. It was Jessie Longtinsky. Mr. Briggs recognized her. He knew her message before her lips opened.

“Sir—Mr. Mayor,” she cried, “<i>to-day</i>, this evening—<i>now</i>—your city will be in flames. Poni, Putkof, Felix, Madder, Bomber—will do it! Five thousand men are ready. They say so. Oh, Sir—I am Jessie Longtinsky, I have heard all. Do something or you are lost.”

Her stumbling words had scarcely been heard before the sudden thud of an explosion sounded in their ears—it seemed crashingly near them—but while their ears thus recorded the actual impact—something blindingly bright shot through the window at their side.The group fell apart while the sinister missile sizzled and expired at their feet, put out by the rain that followed through the riven glass.

Indeed things beyond the Mayor’s or his strenuous secretary’s observation had been hastening to desperate conclusions. With Longtinsky’s arrest the conspirators developed a singular alacrity of action, and a much more wonderful unanimity of plan. Longtinsky had been right in one thing. The work of his organ had been effective, and the conspirators found a serious state of discontent pervading large masses. Poni discovered to his increasing wonderment that a small army of enrolled men had been formed by the arrested editor, who, suddenly, to Poni himself, loomed up as a real general. He found also that Longtinsky’s choice of his assistants—Putkof, Felix, Madder, and Bomber, had been excellent. They were desperate characters with brains, and a fierce determination to wreck their revenge for fancied wrongs—wrongs which Longtinsky’s subtle tongue and pen kept ever before their eyes. It was a prearranged plan that before the possible arrival at a verdict by the jury, which all the plotters, and principally Longtinsky himself thought would liberate the prisoners, <i>all</i>—except Polinck, and thus concentrate the State’s punishment upon him and Longtinsky—before this verdict was reached the outbreak should be begun. And it was. The fact was most significant of the helplessness of even the most perfect system of police supervision when craft and secrecy unite into a perfect machine for the propagation of revolt.The armies of disorder for once were trained, and their discipline was dangerous. They came together from their hiding places in groups and individuals, gathering silently like personal accidents in the crowds of the city. From the cellars, the tenements, the outlying districts in Long Island, and in the Bronx, from factory, and street, from the corners, and the wharves, the yards, and the saloons; almost as if by some poignant magnetism of motive and fore-knowledge, they gathered. There were three hosts; assembled with instantaneous precision like those investing and ragged tides that surrounded the Constitutional Assembly in Paris far back in the days of the Revolution. It was indeed a lesser sort of Revolution. Riot perhaps more than Revolution, but yet stirring deeply in it was the essence of Revolt. Briggs had long ago seen that, but as Carlyle wrote, Revolution “is a thing nowhere to be laid hands on, to be clapt under lock and key: where is it? what is it? It is the madness that dwells in the hearts of men. In this man it is, and in that man; as a rage or as a terror it is in all men. Invisible, impalpable; and yet no black Azreal, with wings spread over half a continent, with sword sweeping from sea to sea, could be a truer Reality.”

Its materialization was so rapid and forceful that it succeeded, and the City was unprotected. What the Mayor had done was a measure of safety, but a measure necessarily tardy and confused. The police hurried their platoons together, and from station to station the recruits came to the Headquarters, where they were despatched in regiments to the City Hall.Already the Municipal Court Building on Center and Chatham was surrounded by the mob, and in the black and maddened multitude in the stormy afternoon were Felix and Madder with explosives, with firearms and with the torch, and with them too was Poni, who had discovered that Jessie had left the Longtinsky home.

He knew well why. She had discovered the plans of the conspirators, and would betray them and—why?—why?—it turned Poni’s face into the savagest snarl of hatred—because of Tom Ratler! The thought made a madman of him, and the thirst of murder swept over him. He cursed his luck; “let him meet that white cur, and he would butcher him like a rat—Ay—he would do worse; he’d strangle him to death by inches; Damn him.”

It was from a spur of the thickening force around the Municipal Building that the rocket, that fell at the feet of the Mayor and Jessie Longtinsky had been projected. And now—upon the deafened ears of the little group in the Governor’s Room, the loud thunder of explosions fell. The attack had begun. The imprisoned men and women, who were yet waiting for the verdict of the trial, had suddenly realized their danger when an attendant came running into the court room, telling of the strange appearance of the square around the building, and telling too their fears, that some attempt at rescue was intended. Some escaped at once, while the heavy doors were closed, and despatches were sent out for help and the rest—among them Helen Lorimer and Amato—remained. The judge was himself among those thus at siege. The prisoners were in cells in the basement of the building, and Longtinsky among them heard the detonations which promised him release. His friends had understood, the effort was already on way.His face lit up with a fanatic joy, and again those painted dreams of power and success and dominion swept him into a furious delight.

Felix, Madder, and Poni had organized a mass of strong men, who were their immediate supporters, and these had forced back the surge of silent beings about them, while the leaders themselves shot the bombs against the doors of the building. The explosions were terrific, yet somehow their violence was wasted upon the doorways alone and the clouds of broken stone and splintered bricks fell upon the heads of the crowd. The huge iron doors yet stood.

The frightened and blanched group in the court room were standing together when the sound of a bursting bomb crashed, and then it seemed as if a shout of exultation arose, and with it the fall of some heavy object announced the breached defense. And at that desperate moment Helen just noticed that the storm was abating and the rain beat no longer upon the panes of the court room windows, the next a sharp command struck her ear: “To the elevators; the top of the building. Wire for a balloon. Up! Quick! the mob is breaking in! For God’s sake, hurry.”

It was true. After the first ineffective attempt the men grown bolder crept a little closer and threw the bombs at closer range. Their effect was tremendous; the great ornamented doors with their metal knobs and grinning escutcheons shuddered and fell in. The mob broke out in cheers, and rushed forward—but not far. The herculean men chosen by the leaders still held them back, waiting for the orders from Felix and Madder and Poni to advance. Some of those inside noticed this with terror. A disciplined mob was a thing of dreadful import.But on the inside there was still resistance. The marshals and wardens, guards, and keeper, had hastily armed themselves with such weapons as the place afforded, and they were not ineffective. But one door had actually yielded, and this had so fallen as to make a parapet across the entrance itself. It was a danger zone of real extremity. Behind it the crouching defenders stretched themselves with the muzzles of their guns over the harsh edge of the prostrate parapet.

And in the meanwhile radiating intentions of skill and discernment had become apparent in the ordered mob. Madder ordered a band to seize the subway station at the bridge, and the City Hall, and to break up all traffic, coming either way. His purpose was obvious. He desired to keep back the chance of any relief, and to divert the crowds for the upper city to other lines of service. Nor was this all. He derailed a train on the Elevated and effectually blocked that avenue of movement. Then came the shout that the police were advancing down Centre Street in military form spreading across the street from curb to curb. In an instant, a small company tugging at some dragging object in the swaying mass pushed their way to the head of that street, and confronted the new arrivals with a small rapid firing gun. It was the only piece of ordnance the rioters possessed—and it was not revealed to the hurrying police as they came solidly up the low hill, until they were within the circle of its deadly fire. Even here precision prevailed. A stern hard faced man, a deserter from the army directed the gun’s use, and the timing of its discharge was chosen with deadly coolness.The police were not really aware of the hidden weapon, until, uncovered, it poured its deadly fire upon them, and almost instantly it was followed by a discharge of riflery from the hands of a long front of rioters, who with complete composure ranged themselves along the street, and aimed their rifles at the disordered and flying police. Many of these latter took refuge in doorways, and under porches, and darted into the side streets. They had drawn their revolvers and a sharp rattling volley of bullets, punctured the crowd before them, but with no serious effect. This repulse of the police delighted the mob, and almost as soon as it was accomplished, Felix, Madder, and Poni, had demolished the protection of the iron door, behind which the defenders of the Municipal Building had screened themselves.

Two well directed bombs had overthrown the door, and had scattered its little garrison, who dragged with them two dead men, and one, badly wounded, while the rioters rushed forward after a minute’s hesitation, with yells and cries of victory, chasing the guards through the halls, swarming into the court room, and entering the stairways that led to the prison cells below.

When the police advanced up Centre Street a detachment had passed from Centre to Chatham, and another from Centre through Pearl to Broadway. The former was intended to enclose the rioters, as the Centre Street phalanx had engaged them, while the Broadway squad was sent to the protection of the City Hall.The first failed entirely in its object, as the singular foresight and preparedness of the rioters was again evinced in the stationing of a body of men in the street, and along the elevated railroad tracks in Chatham Street, who used once more the guns, rifled from a city armory, upon the guardians of the city’s order. The little prominence at Chatham Square was well held. Drilled men, and fairly armed, seemed suddenly all over it. The hillside on Centre Street was clear, not a figure stirring in it, and only far away at Grand or Canal thick moving bodies of people, and the daunted policemen, while the grim spectacle of a few lifeless bodies caught the eye on the street near at hand, where the murderous volley met the approaching officers. The detachment of policemen intended for the City Hall had already been intercepted by an order to wait and receive guns.

The surprise in Centre Street cautioned the captains not to lead the men helpless against the armed mob. The City Hall remained undefended and the mob leaders had not yet thought of its capture. The Mayor yet stayed with the clerical force, some aldermen, and the heads of a few city departments. All there was consternation and panic. The night was now fast settling down on the city, the electric lights sprung into activity, and the pale aurora above the city sent its illuminating and spectral radiance over the streets. The rain had ceased, but a dull clouded sky prevailed, and the raw wind began to rise chilling and dispiriting, in which a few forlorn and unaccompanied snow flakes made their appearance. With the Mayor was Jessie Longtinsky, and Mr. Briggs. Tom Ratler was not yet summoned from the garage. The Mayor expected protection from the police, and he would not leave the Hall.Through the open windows came the frightening sound of the repeated explosions, and afterwards the more awful notification that the rioters were engaged in battle.

Already throughout the city confusion reigned. The news of the desperate uprising spread from mouth to mouth, the wires burned with their message, and from post to post the startling rumors gathered terror and exaggeration as they flew. The vagrants of the city, the unemployed, and the mischief makers of all brands and inclinations, flocked to the scene of the disorders, and gave a deceptive appearance of strength to the mutiny. Longtinsky had planned an attack on the city at three points, so that the composure of the citizens might be the most tried and the forces of defence more divided. It was a bold plot and it was successfully accomplished.

Putkof, the Russian, worked along the river fronts, and started a series of bad fires which, fanned by the wind, spread with terrifying rapidity. To these points the Mayor already had directed the firemen. But their efforts were confined to saving the neighborhoods, where the devastating flames roared and hissed, sending upwards volumes of sparks, and throwing upwards too the horrid red spectres of incendiarism. Bomber had been sent up town to lead the assaults upon the houses of Carrara, and where the villa of the Lorimers raised its marble roofs, <i>there</i> the insatiable Longtinsky commanded them to wreak their utmost powers of destruction. There too the Mayor had ordered the two regiments of militia now left in the city—the 22d and the 69th—but the slow process of assembling the men allowed the rioters to get the start, and commence their onslaught.Despatches to Chicago whither the militia had gone upon a vast holiday parade were hurried away; but the distance was great and a day at least would elapse before relief from that quarter could be expected. Even the forts of the City were without garrisons. This was known to the leaders of the insurrection and they hoped, buoyed up by the mad promises and delusions of Longtinsky, to receive some monster accession of strength from the people, and that a contagion of revolt would come to their assistance. At any rate let them destroy, pillage, and then, if the worst was to be feared, concentrate and retreat into the hills of Westchester and parley for POWER! This was his fanatical promise, and now that they saw the Municipal Building in their hands, the city emptied of defenders, the police repulsed, the river fronts streaming with flame and smoke, and heard too that the attack upon Carrara had begun, these madmen suddenly felt that their hopes would be realized.

Yet, already in answer to the Mayor’s explicit appeal, soldiers were forming in line and boarding the train at the stations in Chicago, and the practising garrisons ordered home from Montauk. The Mayor and the occupants of the City Hall knew of the extremity of those in the Municipal Building; they could easily see the state of things there from the uppermost windows of the City Hall. The Mayor’s first thought had been for the safety of Carrara, and thither the regiments had been ordered.It was expected that the police would be able to dislodge the rioters, and when the discomfiture of the police was learned, when the determined and prepared attitude of the mob was seen, its undisguised control by thinking leaders, it was realized that the moment the prisoners—and Longtinsky among them—were liberated an attack on the City Hall would follow, and it also seemed pretty clear that its fall would be a matter of a few minutes only, unless help came. The telephone wires, worked desperately, brought no answer. The continued absence of any police for its protection awoke the worst apprehensions at first, until a message from the Police Headquarters arrived, explaining the recall of the squads sent down Broadway, and their slow equipment with guns.

Scurrying orders sent through the city had so far failed to secure the necessary armament. In the confusion, from the suddenness of it all, with the plain evidence of preparation, and of some associative plan, involving large numbers, the Mayor and his advisers felt powerless, until an actual physical resistance to the rioters could be summoned. Mr. Briggs wanted the Mayor to leave the Hall, and unite himself with the troops at their armories; this the Mayor refused to do; his place he thought was in the City Hall. Indeed he would confront the mob himself. If there was no help he would undertake to speak and argue with them.

This Mr. Briggs protested against. It was a confession of weakness, it was courting death, it meant nothing but disaster.And during this time, while the men debated, anxiously watching for the promised succor, the rioters were pouring into the Municipal Building. Armed with crowbars, with wooden beams, with stones, with almost anything, they battered down door after door, following the guards into the last corridor in the basement, around the prison cells, where the faithful wardens gathered, ready with their lives to defend their charge. It was a frightful extremity. A handful of men against a host, and yet they formed silently in a line at the foot of the stairs which descended into the basement, where the cells were built.

The agitated and blanched faces of the prisoners were pressed against the bars of their cages; a few lights only were turned on, and in the half illuminated gloom the reflections from the gratings, a glint cast upward from the barrel of a gun, the wan spots of light on the ceiling above the lamps, seemed to weirdly accentuate the awful suspense.

The keepers, twenty in number, were well armed, with magazine rifles, and they had ammunition. They were trained men, utterly fearless. The stairway was narrow, too narrow for three men to descend it abreast. The keepers thought the chance a fighting one. They might hold back the mob for some time, a dozen dead men would choke up the entrance; the police were doubtless rushing preparations for a fresh descent; they had arranged a firing line of six, into which by turns they would step, as each set exhausted their shots;the lights left were nearest the stairs, so that the faces of their assailants would be seen, and they were themselves screened in shadow further back—and then—at the last gasp—there was a fair run for safety through some passages to other cellars, to area-ways, from which, by ladders—already there to be put into use—they might reach the street outside and escape!

It was a bold scheme, bold to the verge of desperation, but it bore some promise of success. As for the prisoners, one of the wardens stood at their cages with a gun—it was no idle threat—<i>a sound</i>, and the man making it was a corpse.

And now the raging torrent was heard above them, cries, execrations, the tramp of feet, the breaking of doors and—they strained their ears at the sound—something like the roar and crackle of flames. Another crash—a shout, a titanic rise of laughter, and then immense tumult of voices in which rose contentious cries: “The jury—the jury—Hang ’em—Hang ’em Hooray—No—No Keep ’em. Let the boys they tried have a <i>go</i> at ’em—Oh, damn it—hang ’em. What’s the use?”—then a sudden silence.

The wardens wondered. It was true, the jury forgotten in the tumult, had remained locked up, and now so uncomfortably relieved from their imprisonment, were in the hands of the mob. Still no sound—a low repetition struck their ears. Some one was speaking, perhaps in expostulation, perhaps in command. Then silence again—and then a storm of running feet and more cries: “This way—to the cells—we’ll break the boys out. This way! Here——”The wardens tightened their hold upon their guns—raised to their shoulders, their muscles set in their necks, and their lips closed hard. The man at the cells also raised his gun and speaking to the figures who, motionless at the confines of their cells, had simultaneously glued their eyes upon the narrow stairway, said: “One word and you’re <i>all in</i>, any one of you.” Longtinsky alone was not at the front of his cage. Somewhere in the half discovered background of his cell, he sat upon a low stool, silent and benumbed.

And now they came tumbling down the steps. The door was full of figures. Crack—crack—a flash of light, six flashes of light and as many men fell forward, stark dead. The mob by reason of the steep descent could not see the wardens, could hardly divine what obstreperous obstruction they had encountered. The cries redoubled, those in front pushed back, those behind forward; in the confused and contradictory efforts many fell.

The wardens waited. Not a man in the struggling heap was armed, which was surprising enough. Sticks only were in their hands. It would have been a pitiless slaughter to have fired then, and no one had yet entered the darkened cellar. Suddenly to their left in the place itself, a hoarse wild cry—a cry of mingled insanity and fright came to their ears:

“Save me—Save me—Longtinsky is here—here—Save me!”

And then a shot, the groan of death, a fall—Longtinsky lay a corpse on the floor of his cell.But the wardens never turned: their eyes still measured every detail of the daunted multitude on the stairway, and the vigilant watchman paced slowly along the cells, with no trace of the ominous scowl that had darkened his face as he heard Longtinsky’s voice. Silence for an instant—and then again a plunge forward of the crowd, and three big men shot into the space before them. Once more a consentaneous movement, the crackle and report, and all three fell back, dead, almost heaped together in the doorway.

Awful cries arose, and in the fierce declamation the stolid guardians kneeling on the pavement, as yet unrelieved by the waiting marksmen beside them and at their back, could distinguish with a shock, that perhaps was only registered in a quicker heart beat, the savage threat of:

“Bombs; Bombs—This way—Clean out the Rats; Blow them to Hell!”

Again there was a cessation of the tumult, and some strong voice was heard cautioning; perhaps the rioters realized that the prisoners would be blown to pieces too with their imperturbable keepers. It seemed so. The stayed crowd on the stairway scrambled back—and—peering forward the wardens saw the steps almost empty save for the grewsome burden of the dead remaining on them. Perhaps five minutes passed; then to their dismay they saw smoke curling and floating in the basement room, and that rushing distant sound, least perceptible before, as of the swish and pouring rage of ascending fires came nearer. It was indeed Fire—Conflagration; now the smoke began to gather in light veils and films, and it was rapidly thickening. The rioters had left. Doubtless they expected the wardens would be smoked or routed out, but how about the prisoners?how about Longtinsky, whose liberation had been the motive of their attack? There was another guess; the fire had been started somehow in the ungovernable orgy of violence, and the rioters had deserted the spot, careless now even of the fate of their friends.

But the wardens had little time to think over any plausible answer to these questions—little more than enough time indeed to shackle the prisoners, handcuff them, and retreat to the area-ways on Court Street, whence they could reach the street up the raised ladders. And when they crept up the ladders, climbed over the iron railing to the street, to their stupefaction the street was deserted. They, huddled with their prisoners, were irresolute. The flames were raging in the magnificent structure they had left, smoke and sparks were pouring from its broken windows, sinuous trails of light slipped from story to story, a roar burst from the engulfing fires leaping up the shaft with the stentorian might of the air’s pressure, and high up towards the very summit of the building, black columns of smoke, dimly smitten with reflected light from the ravenous blaze far beneath, rolled tardily outward—and there—Good God! could it be possible!—the wardens, in the ghastly illumination, saw on the edge of the roof—but just discernibly—gesturing figures. Those figures were those sent up to the attic in the elevators, and among them were Helen Lorimer and Amato!

But there was scant time for deliberation: shots were heard in rapid explosive succession. They seemed to come from the west, from Broadway. The men hustled their prisoners over the street, surrounded them, and marched rapidly to Centre. The shots were more distinctly heard, and with occasional hoarse throated jeers. The City Hall was beleaguered; the fight was for its possession.That seemed certain. The wind was still blowing, though less violently, and the rain had ceased, the clouds were rolling together into denser masses, and here and there a star revealed the cleared sky. Starting on a trot, forcing their prisoners on, the squad hurried down Centre Street toward the police headquarters at Market Square and Broome Street. One or two looked back at the deserted towering pile; it was emitting red forks of flame from its windows, and murky clouds swayed over it, streaming off in a stupendous blanket that took fantastic outlines, beneath the glow of the electrical aurora—unchecked by the disorders—that yet bathed the tall buildings of the city, with its delicate refulgence.

The bitterest fight had already begun at the City Hall. Thither all the resources of the police had been concentrated; the first detachment, recalled for reinforcement and armament had returned to the Hall, prepared to defend it, and to expel the rioters from the Municipal Building. They formed practically almost a regiment. Their dread had been the rapid firing gun of the rioters which had already wrought such destruction among their comrades in Centre Street. They came down Broadway, after their recall and replenishment with such guns as the department could assemble, and had hastened across the City Hall park as the rioters forced their way into the Municipal Building. They were seen, of course, but they effected a lodgment in the Hall, and prepared to defend it, being there placed under the command of the Mayor. The mob at length ejected from the Municipal Building by the raging storm of fire and smoke that now filled it, had assembled outside, their swelling tumult being each minute augmented by the additions of new arrivals of loafers, curiosity seekers and sympathizers, the wild, ill disposed scum of the city.This disorderly multitude filled the spaces around the Chatham Street Hill, swarmed over the Printing House Square area, crowded into Spruce and Nassau Streets, and were pushed up against the northern and eastern ends of the City Hall. A recoil of those occupying Chambers Street from the police had made a fierce jam about the Record Hall, and it was then that the leaders, Felix, Madder, and Poni, determined to actually precipitate a battle with the police in the City Hall. Their first intentions had been to liberate Longtinsky, and then rush the City Hall, and install the agitator there as mayor. This had been planned already by Longtinsky himself. There were almost one thousand men, and the Maxim; with these they might capture the Hall, and the Mayor, who now they had learned was within it. But they felt the need of a directive, formative, and speaking mind. Proclamations and literary appeals were not in their line. They recognized already the danger of disintegration. Something must be done at once which would keep their forces together and win them recruits by success.

“There are ink-slingers enough,” said Madder with an oath, “if we only make the place safe for ’em.”

“Well, we’ll need ’em,” said the handsome Greek. “We may kill people, but we can’t make ’em see things our way, unless we teach ’em.”

“To Hell with teaching,” broke in Poni, whose passion grew insupportable as he thought of the capture of Jessie by the hated Tom. “Get what we want and teach afterwards.”“Yes, I know,” grumbled Felix. “But it’s win or hang with us; and just killing won’t fetch it. Reason has a hand in the game too. Longtinsky could have wiped their eyes with a little hot air. You know that.”

“Come! Come! Right about and take the Hall! The Mayor’s there. Perhaps,” Madder added with a sneer, “we can persuade him to say something for us. Eh?”

Just then a clamor arose from the ranks of the little army, who were still gathered in front of the Municipal Building, somewhat split up into squads by the pouring and surging throngs around them, and only recognizable by small red ribbons tied in their hat bands, their only uniform, though the possession of a gun by each of them told more plainly their confederation. This improvised army was the whole strength of the revolution, together with the mass controlled by Bomber, and soon to be engaged in an attack upon Carrara at the north of the city. Rude and undisciplined, yet the mere fact of their subordination to orders made them formidable. They had become impatient with the delay at the Municipal Building, and the news of the arrival of the police armed with guns at the City Hall daunted them. The bolder spirits saw at once that action was necessary, and it was their cries that now sounded in the ears of the leaders who were parleying together nearer the Chatham Street side of the square.

The cries were answered quickly enough. Madder ordered the men with the guns to form and surround the Hall. He stood for one moment revealed above the shoulders of the crowd, standing on a settee. His harangue was short and rough.“Boys, we’ve smoked the rats out; everything is ours; All for everyone and everyone for All; Short shrift for the satin coats. Take the Hall; the Mayor’s in there. This direct appeal was greeted with shrieks, imprecations, shouts, and groans. Indeed it was. The rioters were now thoroughly inflamed. Their success with the Municipal Building, despite the loss of their friends, the prisoners, had driven them mad, and the easy access to liquor, begun by the breaking in of a few saloons, had added the fury of an insane insolence to their zeal. This with many, but there were many more to whom this effort meant a serious and heroic struggle for the overthrow of a wicked and cruel State. Longtinsky had instilled the sullen rage of his own fanaticism in many, and in many more this rage was mingled with strange delusions of happiness and ease.

The day had progressed and the tolling of the belfry of St. Paul’s announced the hour of eight; there had been no interference with the lamps that converted the park spaces into vivid escutcheons on the dark neighborhoods, and the streets into gashes of light.Crowds of adventuresome men and with them—by the purest caprice of contradiction—a few ambitious and careless women had come down town to the City Hall, and occupied spots of observation, while many more, emboldened by their fears of danger to property, had hurried to their stores and office buildings, gathering with them such improvised forces for protection as could be hastily summoned in the increasing tumult and dismay of the whole city. These were not ready to become helpful; their intent was only self-protection.

The force of the rioters led by Madder, Felix, and Poni, marched out into the spaces about St. Paul’s and Broadway, about the railed end of the City Park, and also formed up along Park Row, Barclay, Warren, and Murray Streets. The battle ground was to be the park space in front of the City Hall. The Maxim rapid shooter was brought forward and the men sullenly pressed up into the park, pushing the Maxim before them, in a somewhat ludicrous confidence that its appearance would stampede the garrison of the City Hall. They could see the stalking figures of the mob running from point to point, and firing carefully at the chances that the windows of the Hall afforded, where crouched the police, who responded with easy assurance, and not without effect.

A reeling form sank constantly to the ground in many directions, and this unfavorable balance of disaster drove Madder wild with doubt; he knew his men would not long stand the murderous depletion. Already the improvised auxiliaries behind the drilled men had broken and fled, scattered over the neighboring alleys and doorways, in which safe refuges they stared out upon the irregular frontier-like duel.Madder’s men were taking advantage of the stone rimmed fountain in front of the Hall, and from this screen the bullets ripped and whistled, and often entered the windows of the Hall within whose walls also dead and dying bodies lay.

Madder ordered the men to form and “rush” the Hall. With a yell they sprang into the lit space on the roadway and ran forward to the steps of the entrance. The movement had been unexpected, and the fire of the police had for a moment dropped; this cover enabled the rioters to reach the steps, and, for a short distance, to mount them. But where the insurgents had forced themselves, the aim of the police, guarding the windows, failed in precision and was wasted. Others had clambered to the narrow lintels of the windows on the second story, and sent withering charges down on the men beneath them, and more destructively enfiladed them from the wings.The fire of the police ceased entirely as they recognized the peril of their comrades. These men chosen from the strongest and bravest, had actually sallied from the doors of the Hall, armed only with clubs. They fought like demons, smashing their sticks right and left, and devastating the throng, within whose yielding mass they soon found themselves enclosed. It was a devoted and foolhardy risk.

With almost magical swiftness, maddened by the very sight of the “blue-coats,” the lines behind the advance which had fallen somewhat apart from their advanced comrades, sprang back, and in an instant the policemen were imprisoned in the surging ball, from whose surface rose and fell the night sticks of its desperate prisoners. Another instant and the crowd dragging their prisoners with them broke to the sidewalk, and then Madder’s powerful voice yelled: “Stand back and Shoot.”

Fleeing on all sides the mob freed itself from the devoted band of the police, and they were seen staggering and crushed together, daunted by the sudden desertion of their enemies. Then, almost before they realized their peril, the assassinating fire of the guns all around them, and in the very faces of their friends, blazed upon them, and they fell one by one, in twos and threes, while a few dazed and exhausted tried to regain the entrance of the Hall. It was a signal massacre. Its record was a heap of dead bodies on the steps, and here and there a twitching figure yet unreleased from its mortal sufferings.The windows of the Hall reddened again with the discharges of the guns, held by its defenders, but now the rioters, exulting over the destruction of the little group of their assailants returned, and drove up the steps of the Hall, almost unchecked. The gates through which the police had emerged, as they beat down upon the mob, were yet unclosed, and they were reached by the first of the ascending throng, before they could be shut. Quicker than thought these men shot in upon the inner court of the Hall, in spite of the fire directed against them from the doors and the stairways. Physical mass asserted its mastery; those outside pressed upon those within, and, though dying and wounded, the police could not withstand their mere mechanical pressure. Then sounded again the terrible voice of Madder:

“Bomb ’em, Bomb ’em.”

The cry made the stoutest heart quail, it took away the breath of the crowd itself and the panic seized many. An interior revolt developed, some trying to escape, others hurling themselves against them in their efforts, while shouts, groans, a fusillade of shots, made an indescribable confusion. Those familiar with the present City Hall will recall that the visitor enters a room-like space cut up by columns beyond, which, in the well-like rotunda, rises the circular stairway with its narrow marble steps and iron hand rail, its two arms sweeping right and left from the initial landing,which is raised some ten steps from the floor of the entrance. The order thus given was a cry which bore all the vocal marks of rage and defiance; it was taken up by a hundred throats whose hoarse and various accents of terrible execration made the next moment loom large and hideous to all their startled hearers. It was then that the Mayor shouted back:

“Shame! Hold back. Listen to me.”

“Why this madness! Take me if you will. Kill me if that is your wish, but stop this insane murder!”

The bombs were not thrown; at least the attempt came too late. The man raising the bomb was struck senseless; a dozen or more lifted guns were knocked from the hands of their holders, hither, thither, the baffled insurgents were driven, pinioned, beaten into insensibility. They seemed like a living whirlwind, from whose mad moving surfaces a thousand arms, in simultaneous thrashing, hit right and left. New companies had been armed at the Police headquarters. They strode upon the scene from Centre Street, from Broadway, from Spruce and Nassau, encircling by anticipation the little boiling tempest of disorder.

Then it was that Poni who had not led in the attack rushed to the Machine Gun. Apparently it had been forgotten. Gorged and yet powerless, its volleys of leaden pellets crowding to its bore each waiting for the click of separation to become a faintly jubilant assassin. Just let a human hand start off its awful alacrity and, perhaps, it thought, a different story will be told.So too thought Poni, still cursing the bitter luck that had lost him Jessie. In all directions the mob were now fleeing; the men hitherto inactive that had been sent to the defence of the offices on Broadway made themselves also into lines of marksmen, and threaded the panic-mad multitude with killing bullets. Poni approached the Maxim with the glee of desperation. If now he could meet his rival the satisfaction of killing him might sensibly lighten the grief of losing Jessie. And yet Tom was there all the time. After he had left the Mayor at the Hall, Tom had run his machine into the garage on Chambers Street, locked the doors, and actually mingled with the growing crowds which then surrounded the Municipal Building, thinking that he might enter and hear the results of the Trial.It did not take him long to see that mischief was uppermost in the hustling multitude, and it was only a few minutes later that the attack on the Building began.

Tom hastened towards the Hall thinking only of reaching the Mayor and telling him of the conditions. Tom had started to reconnoitre for himself and, in the nature of things, as Chance or Destiny arranges the contact of opposites, he came on the scene, just in time to meet his antagonist. He and Poni were almost alone in the empty spaces in front of the Hall.

Tom had not been far away, but in the night—bright as it was—and in the confusion, he had never noted the solitary figure underneath the trees, watching with himself the raging conflict at the doors and on the steps of the Hall. <i>Then he SAW him!</i> Something like a hiss escaping from his lips, as though steam had gotten loose from too hard a confinement, was the only note given of recognition. His hand left the machine, and probed the breast pocket of his coat. Defence was his policy just then; for Poni had stooped and was creeping towards him. Tom had no weapon; the knife was a significant advantage. Poni straightened himself, Tom saw he was gathering all his strength for a mortal dash, and he too stood erect, with a slight wavering motion from side to side in the interests of evasion, when the dash came.

The dash came, and Tom, with a cat’s agility leaped to one side, but followed by Poni who too had showed an incredible flexibility. Tom dodged, so did Poni. The two shooting figures became clear in the lighter squares, and were partly hidden in the shadows. Poni was breathing a little hard, Tom thought; he made the mistake of cursing Tom too often; it was a waste of breath, and to both breath was the subjective element of safety. Then, too, Poni’s lunges missed, because of Tom’s quickness and coolness. But <i>there</i> was Poni’s implacable hatred; it fed him with a strength past comprehension. This mutual detachment could not last long; Poni had cornered his enemy against the railing of the park itself. The thought flashed into Tom’s mind of butting the raging beast before him down to the ground, and running the risk of a knife wound. The dreadful blade flashed and turned in the light; he feared it. Again he sprang aside, and with a yell Poni struck his shoulder, and the bitter edge entered his flesh. It was a bad injury, but not so bad as to disable him. The tear and smart turned him <i>too</i> into a tiger. His hands closed about the throat of the demon, now yelling in his ears, and they came almost together with a hideous compression. And yet sense was left, and in it hate. He struck again, for the knife had never left his hand and his blows hit. Suddenly Tom loosened himself and KICKED. Kicking was elemental and irresistible with Tom. It had once saved him against an infuriated stag. It saved him now from something worse. The stout legs remembered their old power. Poni spun like a waif from Tom’s side and—his knife flew from his hand. AN UP-TO-DATE RESCUE

Ned Link was almost sleeping—dozing certainly—in the big waiting room of one of the Zeppelins where he had been engaged since that astounding trip on the Fourth herein veraciously rehearsed. He was sleepingly ruminating over the possible arrival upon his domestic hearth of a smaller Ned Link when the door of the apartment incontinently burst open and a frightened man stumbled into the room.

Ned recognized him as one from a near-by telegraph office; he seemed out of breath, indeed his disorder roused Ned into a sitting posture of attention.

“Well, old Man, what’s the row. You look as if you had heart failure. Ennything up?”

The gasping figure caught its wind once more, and stuttered, “UP! Well I guess Yes. Riot and Bloodshed. That’s what’s UP. You’re wanted—or SOMEONE and quick too I can tell yer.”

Ned jumped to his feet. The man handed him a telegram. Its contents were startling enough. It read:

“Hurry up a Balloon to the Municipal Building, Court Street, City Hall. Rioters! Party marooned on roof. Building on FIRE. C. Q. D.”Ned’s experience in various capacities, some of which the reader recalls, had induced in him a preternatural composure under trying and nerve-unsettling circumstances, but it was evident that he was now a little disturbed.

He whistled in a tender low way that always marked a crisis in his perplexities:

“When did this telegram come?”

“This minute, I wasn’t——”

But the sentence was not completed. Three messengers burst in at the door, each almost speechless, and each ambitious of priority. They showed exactly the same marconigram. Well! there must be action somehow. Ned called up the foreman, ordered the shaft opened above the storage stable of the huge dirigible, and after choosing three assistants who stayed below in the coach mounted in his seat on the pilot. It might have then been noticed by any one to whom Ned’s previous exploits were familiar that a ladder-way of steel ropes led from the pilot to the passenger coach. A lesson had been learned.

Slowly, with its irksome style of getting under way, as if reluctant to break its rest with a new struggle in the wide oceans of air to which it soared, the big Zeppelin rose between its guides, the motor fans began their whizzing revolutions, and the four messengers strained their necks watching its straight ascent. It was just eight o’clock.A few minutes later the watchers relieved their necks, for the balloon ship was swiftly gliding southward in the yet squally and uncertain lowering night, at first a dark shadowy bolide noiselessly veering right and left, then suddenly, as Ned turned on its light, an aerial pyrosome flashing in the upper sky.

Ned had prepared for emergencies—he revolved the affair in his patient mind. Some people, he thought, driven to the top of the magnificent sky-scraper—he knew it well—couldn’t get off on any adjoining and level surface for there were none—but how, it puzzled him to think, had the huge place taken fire—what did it mean—rioters? Eh,—Tom had been talking that—Sure, but then—Well, he had his job cut out all right just now—and he knew it meant trouble—he couldn’t settle his gas-bag down in a sheet of flame or—for that matter in any much heated lake of air—couldn’t do it nohow—everything would expand—and—BUST!

How was the wind?—blowing—was it blowing at all—Yes—a fairly stiff breeze from the north-west—it was clearing—Well, he could get on the lee side of the flames—perhaps?—he had ropes and baskets, the men certainly could windlass ’em up—it looked that way—if—there weren’t TOO MANY—Confound it—rather risky business—but—good pay for the Consarn—and for himself?—well—He wasn’t saying anything—that was a matter of duty—yes, damn it, DUTY—And yet there might be some “dough” in it—he was not above considering life-saving a thing of business—and—Reward—not that he wouldn’t do it without pay—of course—NOT A SHINER—SURE!Ha—he saw it—the smoke away down there—on the jagged sky-line. The spectacle was not reassuring.

Amato had slowly made his way to the north-west side of the huge structure, and could see down into Court Street. From the south side upon which he emerged at first, when he left the room of the wireless operator, he beheld Chatham Street blocked with people, a surge of heads massed into individual obliteration far south into Nassau Street and Park Row; he discerned the incessant shifting of squads of men immediately at the foot of the building; he had already heard the detonations of the bombs, which broke in the doors—and, at the moment, a tempest of cries rose weirdly to him, and there was light enough—that supplied by the street lamps—for him to make out the massive inrush of the rioters, as they continued to shove through the apertures of the entrance.Before Amato had reached his dizzy aerie of observation the first section of the rioters had begun the work of incendiarism, careless of all consequences. It was done, as the guards defended their prisoners in the vaults. Oil and combustibles soon displayed their demoniacal powers, not averse, it might seem, to have escaped incarceration in lamps and the innocent vessels of trade, laughing with hiss and roar, in their new orgies of disaster. Spirts of flame, wide surfaces of tortured tongues of fire, explosions scattering fire-brands in all directions, to become new centres for the rising fury of heat, quickly made the great court room a furnace of death, and expelled headlong the panic-stricken invaders.

For many minutes Amato studied the exciting scene, leaning over the balustrade in the cool night air, centered almost 200 feet above the tumult—and then—<i>he saw the flames!</i> They smote against the glass windows, and leaped through them with sharp cracks of rupture. He could see the reflected glare of their mounting waves upon the faces of the receding multitude—now stampeded towards the City Hall, following a large, somewhat straggling regiment of armed men. This was just before the assault upon the City Hall.

There was no doubt the Municipal Building was on fire. The wind had risen, the rain was suddenly abating, the fanned furnace beneath him would increase into a volcanic violence, and the writhing iron supports, the columns under the rising floors, would be twisted into cork-screws, and the building would fall! Even as he speculated upon this—a roar reached his ears, the crash of a fallen floor whose iron work had crumpled in the fierce heat and pulled it down, offering another mass of fuel to the ravenous element.It seemed indeed to Amato that the building shook, though his excited fancy might just have erred in this. He then went on the north side, and scanned the light aureoled city, looking down the terrific cliff of masonry to the street. He shuddered to think of the inevitable ascent of the fire, as it marched from story to story, and the yielding strain of the warped and racked supports, undermining the building, which might sink or fall. It would take time, perhaps there was not enough material to feed the fire—the floors might hold out, and caged within its walls the fire might quench itself for want of reinforcement.

Amato shook his head mournfully, he half believed their doom was very near indeed! There—again—a crash, and another vibration like a thrill of agony, in the searing column, seemed to sway his feet. He looked long over the sheer precipice—with rope enough they might be lowered to the street, and some one—the strongest—himself—might be the last to slip over the parapet, and slide down from window to window! It was an appalling risk, and his heart halted in its rapid pulse, as he thought of Helen Lorimer entrusted to this terrifying hazard. A queer illusion seemed to master him, and his head turned and he supported himself for one moment groaning aloud at the awful extremity a chance had brought them in.

Then he heard shots, many of them—like a fusillade—he thought he heard screams, but the wind carried them away. There was no doubts about the shots. Again the cracking reports came thinly to his ears. He looked down again—little flames were licking, far, far, below, the window frames, and smoke in floating curls, like eddies in a stream, rose past him. He supposed the firemen were busy elsewhere.He could see to the east, quivering, rising and sinking, phantoms of reflected light, on the sky—but yet very faintly. The firemen <i>were</i> busy. Putkof had them engaged at the river front. And thus he watched for a half hour or so.

Suddenly his eyes fell upon a little galaxy of light moving like a huge <i>ignis fatuus</i>. He wondered. It grew bright. Could it be a balloon—was it theirs—the relief balloon they had called up? Perhaps! Watch it. It waned and waxed, now eclipsed behind a high tower and now emerging, and always brighter. It was certainly coming towards him, Amato thought. He traced its sinuous, up and down, right and left course, with fascinated interest. It came on, its bulk was visible—high up in the air; it passed over his head, shutting out the stars like a monstrous hand. Then it whirled about and rose higher. He shouted:

“Here we are! Here we are! Come back!”

His cry was heard in the operator’s room, and the people there led by Miss Lorimer came out in the gallery, and joined him, some shrinking from the gaze over the parapet that seemed just leaning against space.

“Do you see anything?” said Miss Lorimer, “the operator has just caught a message telling him that relief has been sent to us.”

“See! Yes, indeed! Can’t you see that dancing globe above your head?” and Amato pointed to the balloon, where Ned Link sat, looking down too on them, as the air draughts flung him away from his destination.For now, by some reinforcement, the fire had gained an incredible impetus; long forks of yellow lurid flames were crowding to the windows on the south side. The windows went in by dozens, their splintering tinklings of broken panes lost in the succeeding roar of the flood of fire pouring out. The floors were falling too, and actual shudders strained the building whose very top at one moment, to Amato, seemed to describe an arc.

And now the balloon swept round and again vanished, and again came into view, while all the watchers called and shouted, even the judge bawling with the rest. Helen Lorimer laughed—Oh, just a little!—the group, screaming and gesturing, shaking out handkerchiefs, even coats, had there, over the furnace of white heat rising to them, a ludicrousness that, for one brief spasm of forgetfulness, amused her.

But another shock came, and another, the building rocked, the two women gasped and swooned, the men had white faces, the Judge reeled against the parapet. It was unmistakable, Amato watched the finial on the apex. It described a circle, and it was a larger one than before. He looked for the balloon. Helen was at his side; he felt her hand on his shoulder; he pressed his own over it and whispered:

“Oh! we shall get out of this. Are you afraid?”

“Yes—I am, but not terror stricken. There is a difference.”“I understand you—there is—but some things can’t happen! <i>You</i> cannot be hurt!”

He turned to her; there was a light in her eyes that shone into his own, and his heart beat fast, a tide of blood had entered her face and made it more beautiful. He forgot everything, the danger, the awful crisis, the place; for an instant, that carried it in an eternity of bliss, he felt her submission, her peace was in his hands, and he felt it somehow wrenched from her by fear, and yet not by any stupefaction of dread; fear, perhaps the strange solemnity of a portentous predicament, had only broken the chrysalis of silence, wherein—<i>he knew it</i>—lay the formed powers of her love for himself. The shouts of those around him awoke him; he looked up. The huge air-ship was close at hand; it passed immediately over the apex of the building, and from it he could see the dangling ends of ropes, and affixed to them hooked irons, like anchors. He turned to her again, she leaned against him, the hand upon his shoulder moved softly around his neck. He freed himself gently, and took her arms within his hands; their faces came closer; Amato’s lips moved, Helen’s ears caught the whisper:

“The beginning of such joy CANNOT end in death! We shall be saved!”

“Look sharp there. Catch that kedge and fix it,” the voice came from the skies, and on the top of it came another shock or explosion or both; the building rocked again, to some it seemed that the roof on which they stood, wavered and reeled, from somewhere a block of stone slid from its pediment and dropped to the street; Amato heard its thud on the pavements. But he was only thinking of the orders from the skies. He seized the swinging ropes.He found their ends weighted with grappling irons, like the flukes of an anchor; he called to the men, and Moser and Meldexter came to him from the distant end of the gallery, and behind them the rest. There were in all five anchors; these the men fastened as best they could, holding them in place on the arches of the gallery and calling to Ned to pull on them. Ned pulled and with a long snapping rip the arches gave way, splitting the railing of the gallery, exposing lengths of it, from whose naked edges a few shrunk away, dreading the startling depth they confronted without the saving intervention of the fence.

The balloon drifted away and the hearts of all sank, while the two women sobbed and shrieked. It was disconcerting. But a voice came back, shot from the sepulchral cone of a megaphone:

“Stand to! All right, I’ll be back! Set the anchors inside the windows.”

The thudding action of the fans were heard, and the gigantic bolide swam upward, preparing for another descent in one magnificent sweep. Amato took occasion to look down and measure, if he could, the extent of the building’s dislocation. The information vouchsafed by this inspection was terrifying enough.

He could not see the street! There was a bulge in the side of the building—and he detected a confusion of murmurs coming up to him, as if from the lower floors, a complication of movements, of breakages, strains, and slow surrenders of stanchions, girders and pillars, had set in.The roar of the flames was heard, but the wind, now losing strength, still kept the flames and smoke driven to the south, yet the fire was mounting—it must be—sheets of redness blazed momentarily in the air below him accompanied by low explosions. Amato dreaded the greater ones, those that announced some falling floor, and almost betokened the overtaking of stored combustibles or explosives.

There!—a tremendous racking reverberation sounded below, and, after a pause, another—and distinctly the building moved! Amato caught hastily at a support, and terror stricken looked up again for the balloon, while others about him breathed in sighs and gasps. The balloon was coming in again on a long sweep, and its daring navigator intended to bring it very near. The other men saw it; there were eleven all told, including Amato, and the wireless operator. The windows opening on the gallery, and fortunately at its level had been kicked in, and they were found to be the openings to a sort of attic. There were no lights now; the operator, when they reached him after their flight in the elevator, had kept at his task with only an oil lamp and a candle to help him to see.

But these were brought round from the south side, relighted in the garret room, and while five of the men entered this chamber, the other six awaited the descent of the balloon to catch the ropes and anchors, pass them to the men inside, who hoped—obediently to Ned Link’s suggestion—to find some means within of making them fast. Once moored, the men in the balloon could draw the air-ship down, and the embarkation of the marooned company from the roof be effected.All stood waiting. Helen Lorimer had joined the two women, and her composure—outwardly perfect, though her heart beat fast with doubt—soothed and helped them. Moser, Meldexter, Amato, the two reporters, remained on the gallery, the three other men went into the attic, the elevator boy stood near Amato, ready to assist. The search of the men inside the attic for means of “tieing up” was unrewarded. The garret-like space was almost destitute of furniture, and presented no irons, links, staples, frogs, or even girders, for holding down the balloon with the anchors and ropes. There were however, some long timbers, joists, or beams lying on the floor, as if remnants of unused lumber from the first construction. The wireless man, coming through a window again to the gallery called out despondently:

“Nothing here but sticks of wood.”

“How long are the beams?” asked Amato.

“Oh! twelve feet perhaps.”

“Well, tie the ropes to them, put them across the windows and let them purchase on the walls, across the opening of the windows.”

“Well said—the very thing. Look out! here she comes! Catch hold. For God’s sake don’t lose her,” and the wireless tumbles again back into the attic.

The balloon was over them, Ned, backing her down with great success.The ropes were eagerly seized—and held; Amato saw Link peering from the “pilot,” and the crew’s heads over the sides of the compartment’s deck. As quickly as he could Moser explained the scheme of fastening inside the garret, while the ropes and anchors were flung inside and the ropes paid out for length. They were taken in at three windows, two at two windows, one at the third. The tieing up inside was a matter simply of winding them round the beams, and driving the anchors—so well as the men could—into the wood. Link kept shouting orders—and said—he’d break off the ends of the ropes on the balloon when he rose, and leave the anchors in the doomed edifice—just then he was holding everything taut.

Doomed indeed the fabric, they were on, was. Its increasing danger was frightful; the whole stupendous mass was leaning! Its interior had not proven fireproof in any sense. The materials of its construction were for the most part combustible, being wood encased in stone walls, and held up by an iron framework. Few of its floors were of brick—it had been a flagrant example either of misconception, or carelessness, or of some shameless corruption. Many of the rooms were sumptuously furnished, many sealed in splendid oaken walls, and into this labyrinth of ignitable stuffs, leagued with hundreds of square feet of thickly painted, or oil encrusted walls, the fire made its way with exulting ease. Nothing could withstand its horrible violence, the immensity of its ruthless power—only its possible suffocation in the riotous profusion of things combustible—through lack of air to feed its own ravenous vitality. And that could not be.The men finished their work in the attic and came out through the windows to the group, now all gathered together watching the slowly lowering balloon. The ropes and anchors held, and Meldexter led a feeble cheer as the hope of escape brightened. Ned was hauling on the cables, and so far nothing yielded; only one thing kept Amato in suspense, the recurrent jolts of the building, as it seemed to sink or fall together—he hardly knew which it was. With each jolt the balloon swayed, feeling the drag on the ropes. If they only held! The crew was cautious, Link understood the danger; all waited, and ever and anon as Amato’s ears strained to note the reactions at work in the building, he heard the firing of the engaged forces around the City Hall, though he thought its early vigor had waned.

“Hold fast. Lower the crib,” it was Ned’s voice. The creak of the windlass on the balloon’s deck ceased, and over the railing of the compartment descended a basket large enough for one person.

“Ladies first” called Moser, as all crowded towards the welcome vehicle of safety.

One of the two women was lifted in the basket, and it was slowly hoisted, disappearing above their heads or just seen as a swinging spot until it came to the balloon. The balloon was kept floating at about fifty feet above them; Ned did not dare to approach nearer. How long the wait seemed to Amato before the little vessel again swung into their hands, and the second woman was launched upward to life and the world again! It was slow business, and the explosions increased, and the heavy structure swayed. Was there time for all?Amato glanced about him, the light from the balloon’s lamps made it possible for him to finally see the pale faces of the men; he saw too that Helen Lorimer had crossed to him, and was at his side. She had refused the first proffer of the basket, and said she would go last. It was now her turn; a second time the basket returned, and all held tight. Moser and Meldexter spoke together:

“Miss Lorimer, we are ready.” The smoke was coming up to them, and even spits of fire and a shower of sparks at intervals burst around them; these Ned feared. His voice sounded from the pilot:

“Look alive. Let the men come double.” Miss Lorimer did not stir; But no one was kept waiting, she spoke very clearly “I will go with Amato!”

Amato felt his heart stand still—No—Madness—She must go. He too spoke clearly:

“Helen, for God’s sake don’t wait! We will all come. But now—delay is fatal—take the chair.”

“Come there” it was Ned Link’s voice, “This is no time for kidding.”

Amato seized Miss Lorimer’s hand; what had occurred flashed upon him as her Acceptance. Strange to be introduced to one’s perpetual happiness on the edge of the chance of Death! He helped her to the chair, and it went upward as a fresh uprush of sparks and blinding smoke issued even from their feet.That was an awful instant, but to Amato its terror faded and—as though he trod along some earthly vale of flowers in the very midst of applauding friends, with his soul’s desires satisfied—he forgot its very existence; he dreamed:

“Rudolph, I would rather wait. But I will go. Be sure to follow.”

The whisper was in his ears, as the basket rose for the third time.

No one spoke; the men were by themselves; there was a possibility of panic; Moser felt it, so did Meldexter: they drew together, there had been a sort of scuffle between the reporters, and the court stenographer had showed a suspicious excitement. The Judge had intervened in the quarrel between the reporters, and pointed out the danger of any folly; there was but one way out of the crisis; if they lost their heads, they lost their lives. Stilled for a moment, the group watched for the return of the basket, and it came back quickly. As it approached the two scufflers rushed past Amato—they were on his side—and seized the cradle; they were white with terror, and at its side they struck out, the taller pushing the smaller man off, as he fought with Moser and Meldexter to keep possession of the basket. Amato had been almost hurled from the gallery through one of the open spaces made by the previous tearing off of the gallery wall. His recovery was just quick enough, and the next moment, discovering the predicament this loss of self-control placed them in, he dashed upon the tall combatant, seized him by the coat collar, and flung him off the cradle. He stumbled upon the smaller man, and the three fell together in a heap upon the gallery space. Amato was on his feet in a trice, and as the other two rose, he knocked them both flat again on their backs.The lesson was deserved; it was remembered as well.

During this incipient brawl Moser and Meldexter got the Judge into the chair, which again swung off, and was pulled up. After that there were no more interruptions. Discipline did its perfect work, but there was good reason for speed. The smoke was increasing, and the rain of sparks, not unmixed with heavier elements, fell about them. In the next load the two reporters and operator were placed, as the other men feared for their collapse. It was now actually getting hot, and Ned, watching everything, was shouting for greater haste. The huge inflated bag veered and tossed and strained on the ropes, and the imminent danger of its freeing itself grew more alarming. Again the basket descended, and this time Ned ordered that two men and the boy get in:

“Get in sitting on each others’ lap, and hold fast the guide rope;” thundered the impatient Ned. It was done and the three arrived in safety upon the balloon deck. Once more the cradle shot down, and there were now only the stenographer and Amato left. They would ascend together. When the women were sent aloft some little pains had been taken to insure against the possibility of their falling out, by fastening them in with binding cords, and this had consumed a little more time than was quite consistent with the urgency of the rescue. The men had abandoned this precaution. Amato being the larger man told the court attendant he would seat himself in the basket first, and then the stenographer should place himself in his lap; both were feeling the effects of the stifling smoke now shooting up from the crevices on the roof in spiral volleys.Suddenly as both had seemed ready for the lift, and had shouted to the men above to draw them up, the building lurched, its great summit slipped away from their feet, and thus, slowly sliding over, was dragging the balloon with it, while the basket caught at an inclined angle against the gallery railing, was tipped, and spilled its human contents out upon the gallery space. Amato felt the heat of the copper surface, and he heard as well, the command shouted from the balloon:

“Let go all! Rise!”

The basket was still there and it was attached to the air-ship. As the anchoring ropes dropped, the basket rose following the upward surge of the balloon. There was one chance for both. The stenographer cowered at the feet of Amato screaming in a mania of terror. He could not leave the young man; he felt his arm and wrist would stand the tug of that suspension and lifting; could it stand a double weight?
