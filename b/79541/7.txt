“Here he was miraculous. His self assertion was most puzzling but it was ineffaceable. He moved from appeals to historical description, and from these to argument, and then decorating everything with poetry, he threw all aside, and revealed himself as The Vicar of Christ on Earth. It seemed appalling, the intensity of his confidence, and yet more amazing it seemed convincing. As he retreated to the altar, an anthem of Hayden’s broke forth in delicate and joyous strains, its musical pulsations wavering over the prostrate multitude, who were about to receive the Apostolic Benediction while the subtler fragrance of the incense lingered through the church, and the windows blazed their illuminated suggestions of sacrifice and worship. As the dazed disciple said, it ‘seemed good to be there.’”

This hap-hazard reportorial encounter described correctly the feeling created in the gigantic multitude that thronged the edifice. And all this was followed by a most beautifully conceived Bull, issued from Staten Island which, as with the finger of an act of transfiguration rooted the Pope and the <i>Idea of the Pope forever</i> in the New World.

And then rising with thrills and transports of pleasure from one surprise to another, the staring populace of the great City-State were invited to consider the marriage of Helen Lorimer to Rudolpho Amato, celebrated by this same democratic and transported Pope. It was a climax. It came at a moment of precise effectiveness. Never had Miss Lorimer risen to a higher power for good than since the Mayor’s death, and the end of the unsuccessful socialistic revolution. We have already seen how her own class struggled—and honestly—to create a beneficent and democratic aristocracy in the state. This was continued.It was one of the dearest dreams of Helen herself. She arranged and animated its advances. Her action in the defense of the suspected but innocent men, caught at the fire of her Settlement House, had given to her a renewed and an almost impregnable popularity and vogue. And this was supplemented and augmented by the splendid gift to the City of her completed Museum—completed one year after the death of the Mayor, and on the anniversary of the insurrection. The New Museum was placed exactly where the old Settlement House had been.

It was a square building with a flat roof, and possessed seven stories. The roof was fitted out for recreation, and its height supplied a healthful and cool resort in the hot summer months; the two floors below it were kept for meetings, classes, libraries, and a <i>creche</i> for working mothers. The remaining floors held the Museum, and it was developed in the line of Helen’s own explanations to the Mayor, in that first night of their meeting at her home. It was an objectified Gazetteer, with more history, and more allusion, and a mild didacticism interwoven. Civilizations were depicted, and contrasted, and much of their forlornness was not forgotten, the lesson of progressive betterments being shown, and that gradual emancipation of the race from a debased selfishness, but the growth of all such altruism was regarded—a little narrowly—as the fruit of Christianity.

On that score Miss Lorimer would abate nothing. It was an unique and most original spot, and so well imagined to combine pleasure, and usefulness, and instruction, and help—for in its lower basement it had a dispensatory—that it was instantly thronged. But Miss Lorimer intended to attach to it a proprietary interest.No one could enter it and use it but the Members of the Order Club. Everyone could belong to this club, but its membership came under the scrutiny of its directors, and they were elected. Every head of a Household paid five cents a month for his family, and all others one cent. The scheme worked well, and the people took a pride in it, and in their memberships. Miss Lorimer endowed it, and put well paid curators in it as well. Its expansion was further provided for.

It was the night before their wedding that Amato and Helen were together in the great library of her home. The winter cold had hastened somewhat, and the fireplace was filled with flaring logs. They had been sitting together in front of this cheerful light. Helen sat forward on her chair her face resting in her hands while her arms rested on her knees and she looked into the ranked and flaming wood-pile. Amato leaned far back in his chair and his eyes were on her alone, their rapt and happy gaze changing at intervals into brief smiles that parted the curved lips and made him more handsome. For never had that extreme physical charm seemed fairer to him than to-night. Helen had only just turned her bewildered glance from his compelling beauty, as if she fled from its overpowering tyranny. Amato was perhaps half conscious of his control, and that brought into his eyes the rising and vanishing smile. But his slavery to the beautiful woman was abject. There may have been just then the sense of a vanquishing power which maintained his freedom and his mastery; but in his heart he knew the relentless passion that desired and yet, through the benignity of his mind’s higher insight, feared.“Helen, can you see the future in those blazing embers? Tea-leaves are better accredited among the sybils.” The voice was half mocking.

Helen laughed a little, and without turning to her companion answered: “Sometimes I wonder whether we can give inanimate things—when they are not altogether stocks and stones—some power born of ourselves to tell us what we wish to know. Surely those burning motes filled with fire, all their parts vibrating with motion, down to their last atoms, might now be ready to respond to the tyrannous demands of our souls.”

“But how will they answer?” the voice now betrayed some interest.

“Why, by pictures. They have no other power. We ourselves are the Seers and we catch in them thrown outwards from our own divinations the images of the things to come.”

“Like the Pythagorean who by looking formed the clay after the image of his thought, or those less recondite magicians who tell your fortune by a crystal sphere.”

“Ah! Rudolph you must not trifle; the pictures are symbols and they are allusions only—not photographs of what you and I will do—” she rose from her stooping position and laid her hand upon his, still regarding the crumbling and reforming mirages of the fire;—“but types, as now a flame, and now a star, and now the—Yes I see it—the Cross—”.“Helen are you one who ‘looks into the seeds of time and tells which grain will grow and which will not?’”

“Why Rudolph, it sometimes seems to me as if our souls left us, were like sensitive glasses feeling the approaching years with what they hold, as the barometer, long before the storm or the fair weather, falls or rises, responding so minutely to the changing pressure around it.”

“O Love could you and I with fate conspire
To grasp this sorry scheme of things entire,
Would not we shatter it to bits—and then
Remould it nearer to the heart’s desire?”

Amato leaned forward as he repeated the smooth lines, with his voice tuned to the depth of his devotion for this woman, leaned so far forward indeed that his cheek brushed against her own, and then his hand was lifted and rested on her shoulder. She turned, he slid forward to his knees on the floor before her. Their faces were opposite each other, their eyes met, and then—almost with violence—Amato leaped to his feet seized her hands, drew her up to himself and crushed her lips with his own. She was plastic in his arms, but there was no return of his frantic pressure.

He slowly relinquished his constraint, and a sudden pain wrote itself in his face—it almost again seemed to him that that invisible partition of the dead man’s memory yet divided them, very slightly perhaps, but Love is so imperious, that the thought stabbed him like a knife and Helen understood.She saw his pain—her words came to his ears like music—such sweet blissful assurance—and yet—;

“No! No! Amato I love you—there is no reservation—” her voice faltered, and tears sprang into her eyes: “We shall be happy—we shall work—the greatness of human destiny will be for us a watchword for endeavor—for all sorts of noble enterprises—”

It was her turn to rouse him—he stood looking at her wistfully, thankfully—but somehow that deep egotism of the true lover craved some greater personal surrender—Why now should designs of Work hide HIM from her—was there not Love—Possession—at least for a little while; let all else be forgotten, and the one personal act of Union reign supreme.

He dropped her hands and laughed bitterly.

“Ah Helen—ever Work! Cannot I count for something?” the laugh vanished—that tender boyish smile, so beguiling and winning came into his eyes and his mouth parted. Helen Lorimer saw it all now—and the revelation, pointing so deeply to the unsatisfied longing, the quenchless thirst for surrender and temporal oblivion, lit up within her a strange wonderful delight. She flung her arms about Amato’s neck, she nestled her head upon his shoulder, she sought his mouth with her lips, and in the flood—the tide running fast and unrestrained—of joy, as if a touch had liberated imprisoned waters, the two lost all reference to the Future—one abounding, mastering, remorselessly ecstatic PRESENT held them wedded in mind, in heart, in soul.Six months later an official ceremony sealed this voiceless pact. It was a glorious wedding. Such a nuptial splendor had never before been seen in New York. Cardinal Fenton intended that it should be made tributary to his own designs; it certainly accentuated again the fact of the new Presence in America—the Pope’s. It was held at dawn. The long procession of carriages from Carrara was resplendently conceived. They were lit with oil torches, and, along the avenues they passed, arches of lights and welcoming discharges of rockets administered a civic public note that was unmistakable. Agreeable to Helen Lorimer’s orders largess, in forms of banquets, gifts and endowments had incorporated the whole great city in this noble function. The Cathedral of St. Patrick’s was encrusted with light, from arch and fret and angle the electric coronas sent their rays, and at its entrance the Pope’s Arms deftly interwoven with the Eagle and the Flag of the States surmounted a flashing arch of lights, and, more ominously to some, above these shone the escutcheon of the City-State.

Within the sacred structure the spectacle was a ravishing picture of color and coruscation. Climbing coils of light terminating in pendent colored globes, lined the central aisle, the altar dressed to its very summit with roses sparkled also with lights; enormous candles burned within the sanctuary, the thrones of the Pope and the Cardinal were opposite each other and each was a hill of lilies. The Pope celebrated the Nuptial Mass and awaited at the altar the slow progress of the bridal procession which was itself a vision of beauty. Priests and acolytes, in swaying groups surrounded the Pope and the Cardinal.Helen had wished to embody again her generous emotions in this great act of her life. One of the lovely and touching features of the bridal train was the presence of fifty little girls from the East Side, and as many boys carrying chains of flowers, chosen protegees from her many foundations of charity. In the centre of this escort Helen, with her twelve bridesmaids, accompanied only by her Mother Julieta, approached. Rudolph awaited her at the steps of the Communion Rail supported by his company of groomsmen, while through the huge temple of worship the music and the chorus of the Lohengrün Wedding March breathed its festive melodies. The curious archaic custom of enclosing the bride and bridegroom in the nuptial cloth was observed, and when at last the rite was consummated the blessing given by the transported Pope, the recessional begun to the accompaniment of a full orchestra playing Mendelssohn’s Spring Song, while three thousand guests watched and wondered—it truly seemed as if the Great City was about to enter itself upon some new chapter of development wherein should be seen again, in closer conflict, the allied strength of Helen Lorimer’s beneficent Democracy of Wealth and the Hierarchy of the Church, against the Lust of Life and the disintegrating agitations of Socialism.

Was it auspicious that as Rudolph assisted Helen, and her Mother, to the equipage, of which he was now an occupant, that the risen Sun of the May morning shone brightly, and that throngs in the streets burst into cheers?
