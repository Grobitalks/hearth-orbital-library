Tom and Ned, the latter encircling the lascive lass who leaned upon him with an embracing arm about his neck, stood at the back of the highest tier of seats, and here Ned poured out the torrent of his explanations.

“You see Tom these quitters come here at night, and they have a banquet just below there on those colored sands and they eat and drink, about as they like, and a good many take the booze pretty straight, and aren’t half more than all there when the gas hits ’em. Others just sit around and listen to the music, and then the fans are slowly turned on—that was a fan that blew your roof off—and the gas flows in, and they get softened like, while the girls do the angel act on that stage, there behind the winders,” and Ned took a more convincing hold of the assenting lady, who presumably had something to do with the “angel act,” though there was little in her generous outlines that comported seriously with the conventional frail figures of the disembodied;“the girls come in there, and swim around with gauzy flippers on their shoulders, and there is a lot of singing, and then colored lights, and the gas keeps on, and the people grow delirious, just as though they had eaten hasheesh, and sing and talk and dance, and then they fall down any where, and they ‘pass over’ just like that,” and Ned snapped his fingers, kissed the girl, and jerked Tom to one side, and hurried him down a central corridor which led through the stone steps of the amphitheatre, and all, just as a heavy and sweetened bell slowly tolled the hour of six.

The girl ran before them and turned into the stage at a side door, which she pushed open, while Ned with gestures of despair hurried Tom away in another direction up a good copy of a ragged defile, leading to the diminutive cliff that sheltered the ineffective grotto, from which issued the rippling waters of the more effective brook. At the top of this alpine ascent, Ned shoved Tom into a cavity which proved to be a kind of trap door leading into a laboratory which was the work shop of the chemist and where the lethal gas was made, whose poisonous vapors inundated the spaces they had both passed through, carrying its occupants away on the waves of a vision-making, sense deadening ecstacy. For Ned explained as he moved between the big bell-jars, and the stills, and the huge beakers, heaped to their brims with green and yellow chemicals, that an obscure man had dreamed long on the problem of manufacturing a gas that would be both fatal to life when inhaled, and intoxicating to the senses. This gloomy plodding man buried in his experiments and living upon almost nothing, often an object of pity to his neighbors, who would force him from his crucibles and furnaces to take nourishment, had at last achieved the grand result.He had formed a new organic vapor which had all these properties, and yielded a pleasant fragrance as well. It soothed as a narcotic, it stimulated as an intoxicant, it killed with unswerving certainty, and as its victims inhaled it it roused a fierce hunger for its subtle influence. “And” concluded Ned, “this hock-shop is not the only one in the city that uses it. It just cost a million of dollars to get it from the old man. Funny. He dropped his chemistry and lives like a lord on Fifth Avenue to-day. He ain’t much to see, but he certainly has the boodle,” and the reflective youth sighed with unconcealed envy.

He was loading the green and yellow chemicals from the beakers into the stills and lighting the bunsen burners beneath them, which slowly heating the mixture, evolved the colorless gas or vapor which was then conducted into large chambers, where at first mixed with air it entered the suicidarium, and was gradually increased in strength by cutting off the air supply, until the unadulterated flood of its flow filled the chamber, and the unfortunates who had become tired of their lives, passed away, drowned in its invisible bath.

Tom asked Ned if the suicidarium was about to be used and Ned, absorbed in his task, nodded his head affirmatively, and pointing to a little recess at the end of the narrow and lengthened room, at length spoke; “Yes it’s off to-night. You can see the whole shindig. You won’t forget it in a hurry either. Come in back here, have a snack, and then you just squeeze up in that corner, and flatten your nose on the window pane and the whole show is yours.”Tom shuddered at the idea of this abhorrent spectacle, but he felt too the fascination of the unique experience, and at the moment nothing would have induced him to relinquish the opportunity.

The snack proved to be a very substantial meal of steak, potatoes, beer and pie, and when Tom and Ned had done full justice to it under the repeated admonitions of the latter, that they would be there some time, and Tom might need a little extra strength for the ordeal, they both returned to the laboratory, where now one additional helper had taken his place, and Tom was put at the small aperture covered with glass, from which at a most advantageous height he commanded the whole Garden of Death.

It was now about eight o’clock, just the time in the upper sun-lit world, when, as Tom thought, the twilight was settling down over the city, and the blaze of the electric arcs were spanning the streets, and the crowds were pressing in eager haste to enjoy the dissipations of the night, while his hotel, the magnificent Kohinoor, rose towering on the sky like a colossal coruscation. As these reminders chased through his mind he noticed the door at which a few hours ago Ned and himself had entered the place, open, and a group of men and women—the latter young and pleasing—came in. The men set about the work of examining the garden, adjusting here and there disturbed spots, repairing breaks, smoothing the scarfs over the benches, testing the lights, opening and shutting the fan doors, until Tom saw them come to the broken almond tree of his amusing surprise.Here there was an obvious astonishment and disgust, and then their movements quickened, and bringing in new material and working with zeal, the reorganized almond tree again swayed life-like before the breeze of the fan, and its pink petals again fell softly on the green turf, or floated upon the ludicrous little brook. Somehow now too a change in relation to the whole trick came over Tom’s mind, and the prettiness of the scene changed into something hollow and horrible, so that he almost was inclined to leave his place and escape if he could. The confined and excavated garden which at first, under the shock of discovery, had filled him with a childish delight, and had actually, so adroitly were its characteristic proportions arranged, given him an impression of size, now became shrunken, almost contemptible in the light of its unnatural uses. It was a scintillating painted harlequinade of deceits, a little theatre of diseased emotions, in which the morbid emaciated victims of self-inflicted despair sought a foolish and wicked relief.

He felt like rushing away, and moved back to ask Ned how he might get out of the horrible hole. But Ned was there at his side. He opened a window and told Tom to look out:

“The gas doesn’t go in yet. First they have a banquet. You can see and hear them from here. Then when they have got through, the dances of the angels begin on that stage, behind the glasses, and then the gas creeps in; they get intoxicated, and slowly as it grows denser and thicker they faint and die. They’re coming in now.”Tom leaned out of the little casement, and as the light glowed again in full power over the garden, from the pink shades, while all the glinting lights within the garden shone, he noticed about ten people passing down the gravel walk among the counterfeit trees and plants towards the petty amphitheatre. They went down the steps of its central corridor, and sat on the benches waiting for the banquet. A table had been placed upon the colored pebbles, in the space before the stage, and it was rapidly being covered with cold meats, fruits, and wines. The decorations and service partook of the theatrical bravado of the rest of the place. There were silver cornucopias, spilling grapes, oranges, figs, and nuts; silver urns filled with wine, silver goblets, platters carrying cold meats, savory pickles, olives, cheeses, jellies and pastes and small tureens of cordials. White bread was heaped upon silver bowls and flowers were thrown over everything, covering even the floor upon which the table stood. The chairs were placed, and the ten strange creatures, who thus prepared their own doom, took them in silence. Tom could have almost screamed in his desperation of horror, but he simply clutched Ned, and the two young men, to whom life seemed so good and desirable, watched the little group in a stupor of attention. There were indeed just ten. Tom noted them very distinctly.First at one end of the table sat a withered sickly old man who was helped to this place by a young woman, on whose face Tom thought he detected a choreal twitching, and a moody stare that betokened madness; next to this young woman sat two masculine wrecks—both men of middle age, their anaemic faces pallid beneath the lights, and their heads turning bald, and their listless hands moving fitfully from their faces to their legs, as they slapped and rubbed the latter, and grimacing between themselves provoked each other to a dreary sort of smiling, by jokes that failed to reach the two young men.

Beyond this precious pair who seemed hardly to belong to the grim company, was a heavy browed, thick haired young man, who kept his eyes down, his head resting on his hands, and who seemed buried in his own somber thoughts. He showed a handsome face to his two attentive watchers, at such times as he lifted his eyes from the table and looked backward over the amphitheatre and the artificial garden. Then he seemed to smile, and he turned to his neighbor at the head of the table, opposite the sickly old man, and spoke, which caused his acquaintance to rise, and scan the spot with a very evident interest. This last guest at the feast was a shorter man with a round head, almost deprived of hair except such persistent wisps as remained over his ears, or formed a fringe above the nape of his neck. He had a curiously soured and grim face, and he squinted and seemed to feel sudden pains in his chest, if the pressure of that part of his body with both hands might be interpreted as a confession of suffering. On the opposite side of the table four more figures completed the assemblage. Of these one was a woman who seemed ludicrously misplaced in such a group.She was a fat, not uncomely person, that under less sinister auspices would have been set down by most people for a prosperous and happy mother of a family, typically bourgeoise in looks, typically commonplace, contented and impersonal. Yet there she was, and Tom shuddered to think of it. Next to her was a rather stout hard faced man who noticed no one but the little glum man at the head of the table and with whom he was evidently exchanging remarks. The glum man was short, the hard faced man tall. The two remaining members of this suicidal gathering were a couple of young people apparently in love with each other, a young man and a young woman, and whose desires to leave a world already beginning to grant them felicity struck Tom as almost more intolerably shocking than anything else in the dreadful picture.

Some of the young women who later would present their celestial evolutions on the stage to these tired souls, simulating, Tom knew not what, of the future states of spirits, were now engaged—arrayed as nymphs and who did not hesitate to accept the kisses of the men they plied with wines—in serving the table. The drinking was more conspicuous than the eating, and some—the young woman and the heavy browed young man—abstained from both. But the rest were insatiable, and even as he felt the chill of repulsion over the approaching tragedy Tom could not repress a shifting smile as he saw the fat comely woman, the glum philosopher and the hard faced hypochondriac eat and drink with a gluttonous avidity, that suggested the grotesque fancy in their minds of feeding well for a long fast. At length the spirits of the company brightened, and songs and loud talking betokened their animated condition. Even the sickly old man waved his arms and his falsetto ululations sounded a forlorn mockery of gayety.The lovers embraced each other with rapturous excitement, the glum man stood up and orated, and his broken adjurations were interrupted by the hard faced man, who smote the table with his fists, and pushed away the decanters, the goblets, and the vessels near him, in a vast effort to expand some notion of science or philosophy. The fat woman sighed, and continued to drink and the two anaemics alternately fell upon each other’s necks, or struggled to catch the flitting coryphaei as they passed about the table, adding new delicacies, or filling the empty wine jars. Only the twitching young woman, and the deep browed youth maintained any decorum, and it was the latter who at last rose, and in a deep musical voice commanded silence. Whether he was known, or whether something stirred the bacchanalian rout to think a moment, before they plunged into the dark uncertainly of Death, or whether it was the relapse of satiety and sleepiness, that caused it, the company became still, and only the voice of the speaker, sounding with a fullness, that gave to every word that escaped his lips, a peculiar resonance, was heard.

“My friends,” he began “do you realize that to-night we shall be together for the last time in this world, and do you care to go out of it without one word to your companions, on the same journey, telling them why you leave the world at all in this suicidal way? With your permission let me at least tell you why I have come here, what motive stronger than life, places me here with you at this wretched mockery of a banquet, waiting for the death gas which we have heard kills while it intoxicates;” the voice became compellingly sweet and urgent, and the crew before him had turned their eyes up to his face, apparently really listening with interest.The speaker continued: “To me at one time nothing seemed so shocking as death, nothing so unnatural, so hideous. To me this minute nothing seems more welcome. I hoped in success, in love, to win the bright things of the earth, as the bright things of the earth go, and I missed all. Then I turned to friendship, and my friends wounded and deserted me. I fled to religion, no voice answered my prayers, the eternal blankness of silence, of disappointment; and nightly around my couch rose only the spectres of want and desire, unsatisfied and unappeasable. The sky was as hard as brass, the earth an iron floor, the air around me without sound, I myself a puzzle or better, I think, a bubble. I could not endure the stupid drag of monotony, the abject meanness of life, its tread-mill duties, its sickening flatness, its vulgarity. I fainted at the thought of a life led in these tiresome and dusty thoroughfares of commonness, with food wrenched from the toil of the repeating days; a chain of identical dumbness from the beginning of the year to the end of it, and then on and on without alleviation, without interest, without meaning. I end it, and I end it here to-night.”

He sat down. His example moved the rest, and one by one as far as they were able or understood their purpose they told their separate stories. The sickly old man at one end of the table staggered to his feet, and by dint of support from the waiters, and incessant catching at the cloth of the table, managed to remain upright while he half screamed what he had to say: “Yes I want to go too. It’s all pain, all pain to me; aches all over me; red hot irons go through me; my feet are blazing with sores; my sides are racked with misery. I can’t walk; I can’t breathe; I can’t think. Pain, pain, pain; nothing but pain.” And he sank down doubled up, and almost dropped to the ground with a shriek.The glum short man jumped to his feet, and stared around him with ominous scowls, that set the two anaemics off, roaring with drunken laughter. But he was not disconcerted; no one so tipsy could have been. He waved his hands, and then folded them, and stiffly leaning over the table, began: “I am a philosopher;” this, brought out with an unmistakable hiccough, gave immense amusement to the two wrecks. He continued: “I was educated to be a philosopher,” more laughter “and I know all there is to know about philosophy; but it’s a farce, it’s a damn lie, the whole of it,” a whack on the table, and a shout from the wrecks, “philosophy is a fraud, it tells nothing, it’s a patch work of guesses. I want to know, and I <i>am</i> going to know, and the only way to know is to go and see. See! See! See!” and the ruffled philosopher fell over on the loving couple beside him, and, hotly rejected by the boy, gathered himself together, and stumbled outward to the gray benches of the amphitheatre.

The turn came next to the hard faced man, who was less enfeebled by his potations, being in that respect a man of some endurance; he proclaimed that he was a scientific man, an explorer, an experimentalist, that he was there in that capacity then, and that he had a commission to find out the fact of another life; “For,” he went on, “I am convinced that there is a conservation of forces in the living matter of the world, just as there is in its energy, and that if the energy of life is not wearing down, we must come back to it to keep up the store of life force. That’s clear, and the chances are that when a man or a woman find out they’re not well placed, the only way to possibly improve, is to go back to the primal energy, and be projected again in some other form or place, on this earth, or anywhere else, where conditions are more acceptable.I have seen that for a long time, and I am permitted now to give it a demonstration. When I come back I announce it, and I shall be the one man—the one man” he was shouting vociferously, “the one man who has carried an experiment outside of this earth, and made a research in the world beyond the grave.”

His excitement communicated itself to the group, and even the heavy browed youth and the twitching woman accorded him a serious attention.

“That’s so, that’s so;” reiterated the anaemics, who roused themselves from a sort of connubial collapse, and standing, sang in quavering trebles a remnant of some song belonging to their most convivial days on the earth.

The effort to sing taxed the nerves and the strength of the two gilded ones, and their raucous wavering tones threw a vulgar expression into the affair which had won a weird or mournful or grotesque interest from the other participants. This contribution made it all wearisomely coarse. Dreadful as its intention was, somehow it took on to Tom a theatrical imbecility as if he were looking at the acting of puppets, who were going through some grewsome mimicry of a bad play. He had hardly thought so, when the woman with the twitching face arose and spoke in a soft voice just audible to Tom, for she turned round facing the cage where Tom and Ned were concealed, and looked at the painted picture behind her.

“You have all known trouble or sadness, but have any of you been told to die? I have. My father, dead these seven years, came to me last week, and told me he wanted me.He came with the big eyes I knew he had, and he came in my sleep, in a dream, and woke me, and as I woke I saw him standing at the foot of my bed, and he said, ‘Come! Come! You will be happier. The Other Side is a blissful sleep a long never-ending joy. Leave the world <i>any way</i>, but COME!’” She stepped forward and moved up the corridor of the amphitheatre into the garden and sat wearily down, on one of the benches not far from the very spot where Tom had lost his hat up the illusive almond bush.

Then there was silence, when the sickly old man poked the comely fat woman and she struggled to her feet, and said: “Vell, I go to my childer. Dot’s all.” The young couple that Tom watched with a frightful feeling that here was some mistake, and that he ought to stop it, were expected to finish this extemporaneous confession and the others stared at them questioningly.

The man stood up and became instantly dramatic. Tom riveted his eyes on him, and could scarcely believe his ears. He waved his arms in gestures, turned around to the company with ease and self-satisfaction, declaimed, postured, and ran through a cheap rhodomontade about the emptiness of life, the folly of prolonging the fight, the ease of dying under the new helps of science, and the necessity of bringing the world to a standstill by leaving it. His wife nodded her head with concurrence to all this astonishing rhapsody. And then while the attendants waited impatiently to begin the final act of this fantastic drama the egotist recited the Plaint of the Captive; it was his own title.The insipid and hollow verses—and Ned whispered to Tom that they were taken out of a circular printed by the management of the Suicidarium—were no sooner over, than the attendants rushed in, seized the table, and removed it, preparatory to the final stage of the sinister business. The occupants of the table walked away, most of them remaining together in the amphitheatre, the heavy browed youth and the spirit-visited woman entering the garden above it alone. Then the lamps paled, music and singing apparently from the side, from a concealed organ began to softly sweep through the dimming spot, and as Ned nudged Tom in their eerie he muttered, “Pull that window, seal it up, the gas comes now,” and disappeared.

Despite the horror of his feelings Tom moved even closer to the little pane peering through to see more plainly. Suddenly the glass fronted stage glowed, and then grew startlingly bright with a purple radiance, and Tom discerned a shifting slowly emerging swarm of ballet girls, with gauzy wings oscillating and hovering from top to bottom of the scene. Among them, he felt quite certain, were a number of dolls or puppets worked in some way into a semblance of soaring angels. The picture made, with the lights thrown upon it, a curious illusion. The motion soft, sinuous, and constant, blended with the music as, in a way, its material or physical embodiment. The swaying bodies, the winding lines, the ordered movements forward and back, the clustering wings, brought into juxtaposition in shimmering canopies, the dissolving figures, sudden crushes to the front of the stage, and then precipitate flights in retreat, single graceful forms gliding in and out, all through the set phrases of a formal evolution yielded an ocular impression, that harmonized with the swelling and pulsating melodies and harmonies.This, <i>as the exhilarating gas began to fill the garden</i>, acted as a delicate and irresistible narcotic, bringing on the drowsy sleep, the haunted sense of coming bliss, the wild delirious, forecasts of eventful happenings, a delectation and revelry of dreams.

Tom watched intently. He could see the people moving, jumping to their feet, extending their arms, then relaxing and apparently falling down; he thought he heard cries and songs, certainly he saw the heavy browed young man, skipping absurdly along one of the gravel paths, while the woman near him flung her arms about in a strange excitement, and then rolled off, as if lifeless, in the little brook, whose petty current was dammed thereby, and the water, rising, flowed over the pasteboard scene, and trickled away to the amphitheatre. Tom’s eyes were not the only ones that observed this accident, for the water soon ceased to run, its very prosaic source in a croton pipe being at some unseen point intercepted.

The others on the amphitheatre were not all so easily observed, but it developed that the scientist—he who went to prove his commission—and the philosopher—he who went to “<i>See</i>”—had embraced each other and fallen, while the sickly old man sat in the comely German woman’s lap, and the young couple had remained seated and in each other’s arms. The lights faded, the gas rushed in with such overpowering force that it penetrated the secret cell where Tom stood, and the last flickering pulses of life in the suicides were brought to a standstill, and the awful act was closed.Ned told Tom that the gas would remain in the garden for an hour or so more. Then the fans were turned on for the rest of the night, so as to thoroughly ventilate the place. Towards morning the bodies were taken away, across the creek, and there delivered to friends—if such had been designated—or cremated or buried according to the direction received beforehand by the suicidarium.

Tom and Ned got back to the upper earth by means of a ladder stretched upward through a narrow shaft. They emerged not far from the Kingsbridge road, and the first flutter of the balmy June air upon their cheeks, the fresh floating odors of the flowers wafted from the gardens of Carrara, the paling stars seen vaguely through that polarized and mystic light that shone from the city, the glad couples laughing with the gayety of the life that surged through all things, the glowing lamps on the shaded slopes of Fort George, the occasional sliding noiseless splendor of an illuminated automobile—at that remote period the automobile becomes a spectacle of garlanded electric lights—with the terraced mansions beyond the river on the east, the smell of the earth, the swishing rattle of the subway trains over their viaduct, all the many moods and signs of glorious vitality, of aspiring and incessant joy, made Tom’s heart leap with thankfulness.

The remembrance of the hidden hollow pageant he had just witnessed, the distorted mental emotionalism and effrontery of its victims, their gluttony, their last convulsions, and the sombre misery of the picture of the dead bodies, distributed over the pink garden, and along the gray benches of the white amphitheatre, in that recess of abominations, nauseated him.He felt a secret distrust and repulsion for his companion; he was silent, and while Ned, with eager garrulity attempted to allay and avert what he felt was a rising antipathy, Tom seemed sullen and brooding, hurrying on with ravenous strides, as if running from a pestilence.

He did indeed hear Ned’s communications, and they were startling enough to his corporeal vision yet hunting for the blossoms of existence, and fully satisfied with the radiant panorama around him. Ned told him of the growing apathy of men to the duties and burdens of life, the rapid spread of a relentless scorn for the promises of religion, and the propaganda of a remorseless flouting cynicism for the realities of society. He made it very clear that the impulses to suicide were increasing, and that over the city the concealed establishments such as Tom had seen were spreading; that it was commonly believed that the escape from pain, sickness, poverty—although in such cases as they had just witnessed the expenses were heavy—was legitimately sought in a violent death; that the marvellous properties of the gas used in these suicidariums—conveying to those inhaling it a sensuous delirium, accompanied, it was said, by an exaltation strange and transporting—had made suicide even a desirable indulgence. Things had gone so far as that the difficulties of the labor markets had been viewed as offering to labor the advantageous opportunity of settling them by wholesale withdrawals, or at least a selected and progressive system of immolation, by which the supply of workers might be kept exactly regulated to their demand, so that good or extreme wages might always be paid.Tom listened to these revelations with an appalled attention, though he still kept his mouth shut, and still covered the ground with violent and angry steps. They had turned from the Kingsbridge road eastward over Dyckman Street, and had ascended the Fort George Hill, whence the whole expanse of Carrara was seen palpitating in the velvet night with a dreamy phosphorescence, that hung over it like an arching tapestry pendent from the electrified air above it. Tom turned back and gazed at it with renewed wonder. It seemed so incredible. He turned to Ned and spoke for the first time. “Where does the electricity come from that fills that air?”

Ned scarcely moved his head to notice the marvellous glory of it, and answered, “That comes from the pits in the earth.”

“Pits?” ejaculated Tom. “Yes, pits,” retorted his pleased companion, “pits bored down in the earth for thousands of feet They draw on the storage batteries of the earth for that, as I understand it. But I can’t explain it to you.”

Tom rumimated for a time. He ran back over those memories of his which were associated with the reading of the wonders of the wizards of the past—Edison, Marconi, Tesla, Ramsay, Lord Kelvin, Perkins—and later names, whose achievements had so often led him on to the small hours of the night, reading in his little room at home, the books, magazines, and papers, that came to his hand, and which had contributed to make him so zestful to reach the outer world of designs and marvels. There was silence between the two men while their footsteps rang out on the pavements, and they approached the dazzling arch of the Washington Bridge.Instinctively they turned by one impulse, to the left and walked out upon the steel span from which the city beyond and below them, and the defile of the Harlem river, formed together a magical and almost bewildering picture.

They leaned over the railing at the side, and then Tom spoke: “Perhaps it’s this way, I mean those pits. There is electricity in the earth, great currents of it, and lesser streams, and it disappears by becoming neutralized with itself, as it were. Now if insulated wires or rods could be sunk for great depths, and these rods and wires spread out in the earth in finer threads, these fine threads would pick up a good many small unneutralized currents of electricity, and they would run together like little streams of water, and they would rise on the insulated rods just as accumulating water rises in a hole or well, when it runs in it. That was done with the electricity of the air. The currents run up to the receivers, and when these are over-charged, they play out in the air. It seems queer that nobody is hurt, or that there are not lightning flashes instead of the quiet glow!”

Ravishingly strange was the scene before them from the bridge. The walls of the Harlem River cañon were surmounted with lofty houses, the edge of the stream was defined with an unbroken path of light, the houses shone with interior lamps, the streets around them were ablaze, and the nebulous crown of light added its ghostly glare. The shadows were intense, and the picture was not unlike some deeply bitten etching, with monstrous opacities in it, skillfully curtained in high lights, that lent it an unearthly exaggeration.The two young men stopped a long time, buried each in his own thoughts, which with Ned had probably slight reference to lofty feelings, but which with Tom, by the indefinable but very real repulsion he experienced for the part he had played at the suicidarium, moved pleasantly forward into deeper hopes for his coming life and sterner resolves to make it worth living.

Finally Ned spoke: “Well, it’s getting late and perhaps we’d both be the better for a good sleep.”

“True enough,” answered Tom, “I’m ready,” and they crossed to the subway on Amsterdam Avenue as to-day, and quickly found themselves at the resplendent porticoes of the Pillar of Light. Tom hastened to his delightful little eerie, and found a letter pushed under his door sill. He tore it open and a card dropped out. It read:

BENTON BRIGGS

Secretary to the Mayor, City Hall.

(and scribbled along the bottom) “Come to the Mayor’s Office to-morrow at 11 A.M.” Tom felt in his bones that it meant occupation for him, and his heart leaped with thankfulness. He would deserve it, indeed he would, and so he fell asleep.<b>CHAPTER V</b>

THE MAYOR

The Mayor’s office had been temporarily removed to the Governor’s room in the City Hall. The lately elected incumbent of the mayorality was seated there, looking out of the window which commanded the view of a restored park area. From this window a fountain spouting its silver drops high in the air could be seen, reviving, with a leap backward of over a century and a half, memories of Old New York, as it was known to pedestrians passing the City Hall Park in 1850. The colossal ugliness of the present post office had been removed, and the curving tip of the park, green and shaded and wet with drifting spray from the resurrected fountain pushed its verdant tongue into the human currents, moving in black throngs along Broadway and Park Place.

And from these currents occasional individuals dropped out, halted, entered the park, and caught the fresh sky over this tiny interval in the clustering cliffs of titanic towers around it. The sky often seemed like a vivid sapphire jewel set between alabaster walls. Everywhere else the soaring babels rose, largely built of marble, shadowing the crevices below them where the compressed tides of business swept and surged almost noiselessly, but intense and continuous.

The Mayor turned from the pleasant picture which lay so completely framed within the limits of the window as to form a unique episode of green trees, flowering beds, the lounging inhabitants of the park, the bristling fountain,bathed in sunshine, and beyond the still and solemn and peaceful walls of Old St. Paul’s Church. He turned to his secretary, standing at the side of the table, with some loose sheets of paper thickly covered with writing, and who seemed to be studying his superior with an interest that could only be detected in his eyes, and in the posture of his head, for his body was slightly turned aside, almost as if he had been stopped in the act of walking away.

“Yes,” the voice had a singular clearness, the articulation of the syllables seemed almost strained, “it is a revolt; it is insurrection. The law is paramount; Sulla repressed the democratic revolution in Rome. And it was necessary. A state must survive. And yet the state is nothing but its members. A state has no generalized expression. It means nothing but a group of people trying to harmonize their ideas into a government, with some hereditary conventions of plans and laws handed down to them as valuable safeguards of society. But experience counts, and experience proves one thing; clearly that chaos, even a chaos that breaks chains and turns slaves free, is practically a worse condition than Order, even if order means tyranny.”

“But, Sir,” it was the voice of the man who met Tom Ratler in Battery Park, “these unbridled excesses cannot be permitted to go unpunished. There is no tyranny in repressing murder, in putting behind lock and key unruly and desperate men, who set no bounds to their savage and incendiary lusts, who would overturn our order and make living impossible. You cannot have sympathy with the growling curs of the street, even if they are hungry when you know their fangs may bring disease and death to the best man that happens to walk past them.”“All right, Briggs,” it was the first voice, and it was the Mayor’s, accompanied with laughter, “have no fear, that I intend to play Cataline or degrade our city by any subserviency to riot and rioters. No! I will meet your expectations on that score fast enough. I will remit nothing. The law takes its course. And yet—” he looked up with a surprisingly youthful countenance into the staring and disturbed face of his secretary; “these poor fellows are misled. They are a sad hearted lot, and life is a stern and empty ordeal, bringing them nothing that you and I call life. And I can see how they get enraged and reckless. What do you make out of a society which lives in the light, the gayest brightest sunlight, at the top, and is held up to that sunlight, by those who never see it; who stand on a pedestal so high that all the world seems theirs, or at least is seen by them, and that pedestal is the thronged column of human crowds who bend their backs, one over the other, immovable in the rigidity of patience, resignation, deprivation, so that the little groups at the top may feel no motion beneath their feet, and may keep their eyes fixed upon the stars, or for that matter,” the voice sank a note or two, “upon themselves, unmolested by any terror beneath them, not even concerned as to why and how they keep their place at all?”

“But, Sir,” the secretary laid the papers he carried down on the Mayor’s desk, he leaned with both hands upon it, and his voice betrayed his impatience; “pardon me, but I take exception to your image. We often deceive ourselves by a false metaphor, and act wrongly, at least in thought, by illusions rising like mirages from a foolish prepossession. Your image is not exact, certainly not in this country.Society is not a pillar with a few disengaged hands at the top, and all the way down to the ground underneath them a hunger-driven, duty-compressed mass. Society is a variously arranged surface, hill and vale, wilderness and cultivated land. People are where they find themselves in one or the other for many reasons—inheritance, accident, self-will, stubbornness, imprudence, stupidity—but they can move sideways, or in any way. They can fill up the valley, drain the swamp, cut down the wilderness. If they start fires and burn out the civilization, the culture, the comfort they envy, will they not only make a devastation more terrible than their previous destitution? Do you recommend plunder, as a remedy for poverty? Do you believe rapine, arson, murder, are the educational means for the elevation of the poor or the ignorant or the vicious? I know well enough Sir, you do not, and you should not raise a finger to help these culprits, convicted by every honest man’s conscience.”

The Mayor’s expression was a study. There could be no doubt that he admired his secretary, but a reserve crept into his eyes, that seemed to say—“very well, very well, my fine Sir, but perhaps if you were looking at the matter from the other end of the social field glass some infirmities of temper that now loom, under the circumstances, to you, as very large, might appear very small;” still he made no motion of vexation. He listened, and when Mr. Briggs stopped, he simply said, leaning back in his chair, and folding his arms; “What were all the circumstances exactly?”

The secretary moved to a map of the city that was laid upon a table, and which he raised in his hands pointing to the spots, which illustrated his narrative, as he referred to them.“It began in Grand Street, in that den and sink of infamy, the office of The Beehive. The whole plot was hatched there. The paper should be suppressed. Longtinsky has made himself an enemy of decency. He is a Jacobite; an anarchist, a ruthless demon, who recognizes no rule, but the same bloody and murderous acts that he thinks can be used to stifle industry.

“The first known about the fire was the flames shooting from the stairway of Helen Lorimer’s Settlement Hall on Pitt Street. A dozen men started out from the basement, and six of them were nabbed; among them Polinck, Longtinsky’s right hand man. He had paper and matches in his pocket and a smell of oil and smoke was in his clothes. The wind was blowing and before the firemen reached the place the building was seething with flames. Then came an explosion. It was that which killed the people in the streets. There was a stampede, and again another explosion sent burning timbers over the streets and started two other fires. Almost a block was destroyed and there were ten deaths. Let these men alone, let them stay here, and they will send their accomplices over the city: they will make outbreaks at a dozen points; and they will make us a laughing stock to the whole world. Immunity will encourage them to believe that they are feared. They will breed a pestilence of disorder that nothing can quell. The electric chair for each of them is mercy.”

Mr. Briggs had, long before this climax, dropped the map, and again stood at the side of the Mayor’s desk. The latter arose, and as he stood there he measured fully the height of his secretary;but he was thin, with a remarkably well shaped and evenly balanced body, and his face, without a distinct claim to beauty, possessed a singular interest, from its expression, its paleness and a winning look, of grace and sympathy in the full blue eyes. His brow was rather too high for the finest facial effectiveness, but this was in a measure corrected by the chestnut hair which richly covered his head and floated downward over the brow; his hands were noticeably long fingered, his mouth wide and too narrow, the ears bent backward to the skull and the nose straight, handsomely formed, but not intellectual. His eyes caught the eye of an observer and held them. They were unaffectedly delightful to see. Many things looked out from them, and not the least inviting was candor and affection whilst in other moods they expressed a keen enjoyment of fun and no inconsiderable delight in living. And then again they became introspective and sad and perhaps their beauty was thus most conspicuous. The face certainly did not produce an impression of force or great mental acumen, but it was a face that men would trust and women might love.

If the attraction it exerted might be safely referred to any one physical feature, it could be ascribed to the delicately pencilled eyebrows, which possessed a coloring slightly more accentuated—deeper—than his hair, and which gave his face an artistic form, the suggestion of aesthetics, or literary or even poetic aptitude.The Mayor had placed his hand on the shoulder of his secretary; “My friend you are too severe, but you are right as to the theory at least of my conduct in this case. There will be no suspense of the judgment, and the men must die. Ah! how hard it is to say that word,” and the man, who had evidently gone through a storm of conflicting feelings sighed, and again turned with grave eyes to the picture seen through the window at his side. At that moment the shivering arrest of an automobile drew the attention of both men to the outside, and it was Briggs who announced, with a breath of vexation in his voice; “Your Honor will now have the supreme pleasure of meeting the superb Helen Lorimer. She approaches with all her charms. If I am not greatly mistaken she comes to plead the cause of these condemned criminals. She has suffered herself in property and in personal abuse, but she belongs to the ‘progressives,’ she belongs to those who think that ‘the quality of mercy is not strained.’ She has an incurable fund of optimism, and some of her aphorisms sound better than they work. May I earnestly,” he had stepped close to the Mayor, and his demeanor showed his extreme anxiety, “beg your Honor not to be too susceptible to her eloquence.”

“You stay here,” replied the Mayor, smiling, “with such a Cerberus of denial and refutation though she brought Apollo’s lute I should be safe. Besides I feel some timidity about entertaining so great a woman. From all accounts she is the Hypatia of our Alexandrian New York.”

The page at the door approached, and handed the Mayor a card. It bore the name Helen Lorimer. The Mayor moved to the door and disappeared. In the next instant he returned, preceded by the woman we have already seen, and who met the apprehensive greeting of Mr. Briggs with a cordial and reassuring friendliness.The Mayor occupied temporarily the great room, formerly called the Governor’s Room, during the period when the city of New York was part of the State of that name. The sacred relic of the Revolutionary days—Washington’s Desk—was opposite his own at the west end of the apartment, and he sat facing the heroic portrait of Washington above the mantel on the west wall; behind him still remained the portrait of George Clinton, to his right hand those of Governors Tompkins and Lewis, and on his left through the unchanged green lambrequins he saw the extended park. The fine distinction of the room, with its east and west chambers as well, the delicate repression and formal simplicity in its ornament, delighted him. Often in his few unoccupied moments he would review the storied walls, standing long before the painting of Hamilton and Jay, lost in reveries over the splendid past, recalling the daring skill, the patriotic fervor, the almost prophetic insight of the great Jamaican. He felt the spur of the renowned example, and in his heart—hardly known to himself—stirred the promptings of a magnanimous envy.

Helen Lorimer entered the room, and her brilliancy gave to the sedate place a moderate radiance, which somehow startled both the Mayor and his secretary. Her beauty was more revealed than usual, from the freshness of color in her cheeks, and the animation and interest in her eyes, while a considerable grace in her approach united with simplicity of dress, made her charming as a mere object of inspection. The knowledge both these men possessed of her qualities of mind and heart evoked in their faces—and for different reasons—a visible response of excitement.The Mayor received his visitor with admirable ease, though a faint tide of color flushing his pale countenance betrayed an interior emotion which did not escape the observation of Mr. Briggs, who subjected the new comer to a scrutiny which suggested his personal reluctance to leave his superior officer alone with her. Mr. Briggs felt too surely the vacillating attitude of his chief, in the affairs of state then pressing for solution to look with complacency upon any influence less uncompromising and severe than his own.

Helen Lorimer extended her hand, which was grasped by the younger man. She looked at the mayor for a moment quite steadily and to his secretary with a noticeable repression of the first smile of friendly salutation. The Mayor motioned her to a chair conveniently placed at the front of his desk, and, as she sat down, was the first to speak,

“I appreciate the honor of your visit, Miss Lorimer, and I beg to make you acquainted with my secretary Mr. Briggs.”

Mr. Briggs bowed a little stiffly, and evidently convinced that his presence was essential, or at least, up to this point, not explicitly discouraged, took a chair somewhat near the side-table carrying the maps he had been examining. The Mayor returned behind his desk, but remained still standing, his gaze fixed with expectancy upon Miss Lorimer, who for a moment turned her eyes to the floor, and then, raising them towards the Mayor, with a resumption of her graciousness and her inviting confidence disclosed her mission.“I presume I should avoid all mere feminine circumlocution and be very prompt and business-like, Mr. Mayor, in telling you the purpose of my visit. It is about the men who have been so quickly convicted of manslaughter, in the affair of the fire and explosion in Pitt Street which destroyed my Settlement Hall. This condemnation to death seems to me harsh, unnecessary, perhaps I should say cruel, and I believe that vindictiveness in this case will breed still greater mischief.”

Mr. Briggs was now decidedly aroused. He turned his eyes upon the lady with the faintest intimation of scorn, and seemed almost unable to repress his desire to speak. But it was the Mayor who answered——and the peculiar clearness of enunciation and the substance of his words as well, evidently impressed his hearer. She leaned towards his desk and looking upward watched him with an eagerness which might have been readily mistaken for personal admiration of the speaker. With alert minds such as hers sympathetic response involves interior subjective harmonies that may be felt, but are not at first understood. Something very personal shone in her eyes, and whether it was art or reason or taste, or goodness in what he said or how he said it, Helen Lorimer perceived, or better, felt, the element of nobility in the young governor of the City of New York before her. Nor was Mr. Briggs less impressed. He congratulated himself upon the result of his assiduous tuition; at least he put it that way to himself.“No! Miss Lorimer there can be no review of that decision. You are moved by an admirable compassion. But you have for once at least forgotten to yield to your better judgment. These men were atrocious criminals. The fact that they have been condemned to death makes them no less so. Punishment is the defense of order. There is no other. Moral suasion has its precautionary value in their case, death or imprisonment removes the agent and the disease. It is a kind of segregation that stops infection. I do not wish to appear cynical, but government on this earth will never escape the requirements of the penal code. The hearts of governors may bleed—mine does—but rulers must act outside of the indulgent motives of sentiment, and have a fixed and even stern eye for the interests of order. The constitution of states may be irreparable blunders: those chosen to execute their commands cannot permit themselves to criticise their operation. This is not tyranny or a stupid conformity, or simply an irrational servility. It is duty.

“Are you invoking a higher ministration? Do you believe that your pity or the State’s commiseration and forgiveness will change these men? Convince yourself that they desire an overthrow of our present social relations that that desire is, with them, conviction, and if you believe in the existing order, what possible alternative of action can you recommend, referable to that order’s continuance, but their suppression?”

“Ah! Yes! your Honor I know all this is appealingly clear—in a way—but is the evidence so clear against the men themselves? And should the State be vengeful? Does not hatred breed hatred and are not the victories of Mercy more secure, more enduring?”Helen Lorimer leaned forward on the Mayor’s desk and her face startled the young executive by its beauty, its beauty of form and of expression, and he sank into his chair, impatiently warring within himself with a strange willingness to surrender to any petition which came in so lovely a guise. Had he been repeating stock phrases? Could anything so tenderly so ravishingly beautiful as this woman be resisted? Was she not more? Her voice was a penetrating music that delighted him. And of praise of her mind was not the City full? He felt a hopeless inability to repel an approach so enthralling. His words had been stately enough, his voice in its best form,—but somehow he felt it was a mask, and his heart, his live heart—not the executive organ encrusted with traditions—rejected his own words, and craved the very thing his fair visitor intimated. Still there was a regnant sense left in him of position, of authority, of responsibility. He remembered Briggs. He cast a fugitive glance at his secretary, who had drawn closer to him, indeed stood directly at his side, as if he understood his ordeal, and would invigorate his strength, with his own uncompromising hostility to any temporising with the ends of justice.

Again the young Mayor spoke, and no trace of indecision, not even the respectful condescension of courtesy sounded in his voice which would have carried, to the critical ear, a note a little too severe, as if uttered under the constraint of a misgiving which, for appearance’s sake, gave it harshness.“Pity is a tender virtue in woman, Miss Lorimer. It has its beneficent uses but it can enter too carelessly into the considerations of policy which determine the existence of society. The State, in which are gathered for safety thousands—in our community millions—of souls, becomes inviolate, as an entity, the moment they engage to live together for mutual security and progress.

“These men would take your pity at a value which degrades it. They would consider it fear. And they would only rage more ferociously against the social state. They are frankly anarchists, they assert the solidarity of chaos, the unity of discord, the prosperity of idleness, the stability of disorder. Their philosophy is a jumble of contradictions. But worse, they are self-elected criminals, or else they are hopelessly mad. They are your personal enemies, Miss Lorimer. Their detestable organ incites the vicious to mischief against yourself.

“I should be recreant to my own oath of office, and I should insult the public intelligence if I changed the sentence of these men. Have you any proof that the sentiment of this city asks for commutation. I have heard something of a petition in their favor, but I have not yet seen it. Miss Lorimer I cannot consider for one instant your intercession. The quickness and rigor of the sentence upon these men is no slight testimony to both the enormity of their crime, and the sureness of their guilt.”“Mr. Mayor,” again the appealing voice, the eloquent motion of importunity as Helen Lorimer moved her hand almost supplicatingly towards the speaker, and above all the flushed beautiful face, “You must know I have no reason to find fault with your position. You are held rather tightly I know within the limitations of your oath of office. But is it quite certain that these men—I mean the very men condemned—were guilty? Are you quite sure they are such red-throated curse-breathing, death-dealing fiends as the papers paint them and the preachers say? Are the mass meetings of the smug and self satisfied and well-to-do oracles of wisdom or sense or even moderation? Let us be very candid indeed. But let us also be careful about our self-respect. Are you willing to send these men to death, convicted as they have been in a two days’ trial not on the evidence, which the State admitted was open to doubt, but convicted by the feverish hostility of a populace, frightened out of its wits?

“Can you forget the devastation that is wrought in the moral sense of a community when it gratifies the gluttony of its rage over those who have half scared it to death? These men were not murderers at heart. They were victims of their own credulity, tools, swinish tools let us say, of cleverer and deeper brains. Kill them, Mr. Mayor, and those cleverer and deeper brains will have new formulas to repeat and in the repetition they will win a perilous plausibility that will give socialism a martyr’s crown, not the ignominy of a felon’s grave.”“But,” the young Mayor had also stretched out his hand and held it for an instant rested upon the back of the gloved fingers of Helen Lorimer, “do you forget the innocent who have suffered, those who have actually been killed, those who have been wounded? Are we coolly to recommend patience to them, while they sit unsatisfied and bitter about the graves of their dead? Are you quite sure that neglect here might not start a reactionary outcry, against the heartlessness of Society, against its laxity, its inefficiency? Are you willing to place us between two fires, the torch of an incendiary anarchy, and the bullets of a Vigilance Committee?”

“I forget nothing, your Honor:” Miss Lorimer arose, and, as if to a gesture of command, the Mayor also arose, “I have seen the families of all the wounded, of all the dead. They need nothing, and their mouths are not filled with threats of vengeance; they speak of the timeliness of clemency. They actually fear the anger of society, as that it may blindly outrage the groveling thousands who will see in it a Juggernaut of selfish fear, not the sword of Justice. They live among these classes and they know. Neither are they convinced that this hunger for punishment is in their behalf. It is the self preservative spasm of respectability.”

Again the Mayor glanced at Mr. Briggs as if he faltered in his thoughts, but a sudden surge of blood mantled in his cheeks. Even to Helen Lorimer he became handsome, and it was this strange new beauty, in a man, that brought for an instant, the color in her own face, and challenged her heart with a question of incredulity.“Odd, Miss Lorimer,” he said, “that I should place myself in opposition to yourself as the guardian of your own class. I can understand your feelings, their magnanimity; indeed I see more than that. Your purpose might be termed an esoteric patriotism.

“Apparently condoning a wrong, apparently forgiving an outrage you intend to turn into legitimate channels the angry tides of unrest which you think mount higher and more dangerously as they were resisted or overborne. You mean to win back to allegiance by kindnesses the unruly spirits that may grow, as you think, more turbulent by repression. I understand, of course, but is it wise?”

The Mayor’s question was addressed to two listeners, for, as he asked it, he turned half away from the absorbed, lovely figure before him—more beautiful than ever—to his inward sense for the nobility of the soul that dwelt in it—to his rigid and observant secretary.

The invitation to speak was accepted, almost precipitately by that individual, who directed every word to the person of Miss Lorimer. He seemed to have forgotten his superior, although his first word answered the latter’s query.

“It is not wise; it is not sane; it is frivolous and it is cruel. Cruel because it will lead to greater disasters, to more loss of life and property. I know these people too, the men who read, who believe the creed of The Beehive. They are maniacs of unrest. They are demented with illusions; the distortion of their minds distorts their feelings, and they are social outlaws. They have become ravenous with impious hopes.In them the jackal and the hyena are united; the lust of their hearts is murder and rapine, though they do not always care to put their own hands to the job. They have ambition. It is formless, inconsiderate, of colossal selfishness and preposterous egotism. But they are without true courage. The leaders at heart are a pack of besotted braggadocios, empty of all daring, furiously vindictive, meanly cunning, and skulkers behind the bodies of braver, more robust men. They can be terrified into inaction. They can be prostrated by one overwhelming act of condign punishment. They will shrink into nothingness before the retribution of summary annihilation. The paralysis of terror will kill their will and their energies and they will vanish.

“Parley with them and they will devour you, for they are as ruthless as wolves, and, judging others by their own degraded instincts, the first intimation from your lips of clemency will form a new incentive for their insulting malice to propose more preposterous concessions. They are a class engendered by vicious exaggerations of the ills of society. They were originally formed in the prison houses, the miasmatic swamps, the pest houses of European tyrannies. They exult in the supremacy of their shameless dreams. If there are exaltations of speech or even of ideas among them, they are baseless lunacies built up on tricky words and on ignorance of their supine victims. No, Sir,” turning to the Mayor, “Society as we understand it is incompatible with these people, except as under the coercion of fear they remain inert and helpless.”The Mayor had not taken his eyes from the face of Helen Lorimer, and the gleam of a flitting smile crossed his lips as he noted the concentrated attention she gave his secretary. But her attention changed visibly to anger. The liquid depth of her eyes grew firm with a light of repudiation and denial; her lips straightened just a little with disdain and impatience her posture was more erect as if courting the blow that seemed aimed at her mistaken benevolence, and then all this changed again, into a sad look which gave her—in the Mayor’s mind—an even loftier loveliness as of a seraph mourning over some carnal contumacy that he could not help or bless.

That quickly passed, and Miss Lorimer gave at last a wondering glance at the Mayor, as Mr. Briggs concluded his fiery exhortation, and then withdrew a little, almost as if meditating an abrupt exit. But she, in this way, put herself just perceptibly in opposition, she secured a physical separation which adroitly emphasized her complete dissent. Both the anger and the momentary graveness had left her face. She was smiling. It was a smile of deprecation, an unspoken reproach in which pity dwelt. She spoke with a hurried earnestness that betrayed her suspense.

“You wrong these people, Sir, indeed you do. I have not been slow to learn a great deal about them from their own lips. I have not busied myself solely in a lecturing propaganda. I have indeed striven to make less hostile their resentment against the rich, to overcome their ingrained sense of separation and repudiation, but I have also been a listener and a pupil. I know some of the very men condemned. It is not possible that they are murderers at heart.Their families have fallen at my feet in weeping agonies of protest that they were misled, whipped into a blind rage by artifice, by drink, by bribes, by threats. If you thrust these men into Eternity you are doing a bitterly cruel thing and making a grave mistake. I ask you, Sir,” she came forward and addressed the Mayor, “only the slight favor of a delay—let it be that—let me present new evidence to the courts to show that these men—some of them—are the victims of perjury, that they were absolutely free of guilt—that the bombs were put in the building by other hands—miscreants who fled and left them—and then forced or persuaded these unfortunates to enter the building. I have a curious suspicion that my own class are not innocent in a big plot—favored, for money by the creatures of The Beehive—to bring about an ignominious triumph over the enemies of their order. There is something to be learned on that score.

“You speak of Society and its indignation. I too run risks and encounter forfeits. But an injustice will more grievously wreck your career than the transient rage of ignorance. Prove rather the greatness of your office by defending these speechless victims of a popular fury. More, Sir, I will accept the challenge of the people, I will establish their innocence by proof—the innocence of some. It can be done. My lawyers have been successful in gathering fresh evidence at many points. I can prove perjury. There have been paid witnesses commissioned to destroy these lives. If the State trembles at the thought of an assault upon its life, how much more deeply it will grieve, if, in its own defense, it has struck down innocent men. What will it say to the assassins of its honor? Be at least rational. It is common talk; the precipitate action of the State; the apparently prejudiced and frightened decision of the judge; the hasty verdict.Shall the blot of an equivocal judgment send six men to death, five of whom I believe can be proven innocent, and can either a State or an individual survive such a miscarriage of justice? Listen to me, Sir:” the Mayor was indeed listening, he hung upon her words, he had pushed past his secretary, he bent himself forward, keenly searching the beautiful face before him, which itself by some prompting had come closer and closer to his own, and which, in the hurrying current of words made its very breath fan the parted lips of the young governor.

The Mayor spoke. Mr. Briggs would have stopped him, he even touched his arm in an admonition more strenuous than respectful—but the Mayor paid no attention; he shook his arm free of the detention, his voice rose somewhat, and he held out his hand to Miss Lorimer who took it, and even pressed her disengaged hand upon it, and thus in the chancery of the touch of a woman, who made his heart beat strangely, the Mayor forget the rhetoric of his office and spake frankly.

“You <i>shall</i> be heard, Miss Lorimer, you <i>shall</i> have the reprieve. Let me see your evidence, let the courts see it. Time and place shall be allotted to you to put this all in order; but why have you withheld it, why urge abstraction when you had the concrete facts to support your claim. God forbid that this State or I, shall commit judicial murder. An order goes from this office now, and by the hands of my secretary,” he turned with a slight almost indifferent gesture towards Mr. Briggs, who stood flushed and angry behind him, “to delay the execution, and, on the showing of new evidence and the suspected treachery of witnesses, to recommend the re-trial of the whole matter.”Miss Lorimer withdrew her hands and said; “It may seem to you peculiar that I did not come to the point of the new proof I have, to establish the innocence of some of these men, at the outset. But, Sir, I did indeed wish to withhold it at this moment, unless less convincing arguments failed. And they did. You may have been right in forcing my hand; I do not regret it, except that, so much known it may too quickly reach the ears of the prosecutors, and help them to overcome or avoid it. I hope, Sir, that this confession, may, for the present, be considered sacred. From the bottom of my heart I thank you. You will have no regrets.”

She bowed and, extending her hand again, shook heartily the Mayor’s; she stepped past him, and advanced to the recalcitrant Briggs. That non-plussed and utterly disgusted person was not to be outdone, and he received the lady’s proffered advance with a discretion and civility that was entirely courteous but also significantly formal.

The Mayor passed out of the room with Miss Lorimer, and, as they moved down the serpentine stairway of the rotunda, he told her that the reprieve would be officially published, the lawyers she retained should then apply for a new trial to the courts, and that she would personally grant him a favor, essential for his own defence, if she would permit him to know more definitely the nature and the details of her action and case.

Miss Lorimer assented to everything gladly, was profuse in her thanks, and, as they descended the outside steps of the City Hall, intimated the honor and the enjoyment it would give herself, her mother,and her uncle to have the distinction of a visit from his Honor. Almost exultantly the Mayor accepted this invitation—he felt within himself nothing had ever seemed so desirable, so inviting, so valued—and only a little hesitatingly stipulated that she would allow him to make the appointment. And that favor was politely granted. Miss Lorimer entered her motor car and the Mayor watched her depart, and was himself invested with other watchers, attracted to the spot by so unusual occurrence; but among the many eyes rivetted upon the distinguished couple, two only, from the room they had both but an instant vacated, bestowed upon them an inspection, a hard reflective scrutiny, that meant neither satisfaction nor approval.

As the Mayor returned up the steps of the Hall he passed a young man whose fine proportions and handsome face, at the very moment when another face filled his thoughts with a singular tenacity, caught his eye and extorted from him an involuntary notice of admiration. It was our young friend Tom Ratler. Mr. Briggs had also seen him from the Governor’s room, and was moved to an ejaculation thereby, that opened his lips, but was not embodied in any articulate sounds.

Tom felt that he was following the Mayor, and he turned backwards a few steps to delay his entrance in the Hall from a modest and becoming sense of the propriety of respect. He looked at the disappearing automobile with its precious freight of beauty, and a pleasant smile—that smile that always accompanies the subjective sense in any one of having discovered a love affair—lit up his face, and it was with its lingering grace still in his young eyes, that he entered the Mayor’s office.Mr. Briggs had expected him, and left the room as the Mayor returned by another door; he met Tom and ushered him in. He was introduced to the Mayor as the “young man Mr. Briggs had suggested for his chauffeur, and whom his Honor may have remembered he (Mr. Briggs) had told him of, as having defended himself with so much spirit at the Battery Park.”

The Mayor was delighted. He very soon satisfied himself that Tom understood the nature of the avocation he intended him for, engaged him, and sent him to his home, in one of the most charming sections of the city, the revived, renovated, restored area of the Ninth Ward, in the old precincts of Greenwich Village. Tom was provided with a letter of endorsement and identification and with an elation that invigorated him, started for his new place. He left the Mayor and his secretary standing in the embrasure of a window, and heard their voices distinctly rising in a discussion that showed—if he was not greatly mistaken—that both were angry.

The changes of the centuries have their compensations. They seem to old fashioned folk the more memorable and pleasing when they accomplish graceful restorations of the past. Such a wonderful thing had happened in the old section of the city which one hundred or more years before this happy revival, had formed the Ninth Ward of New York.It had boasted of the sweetness of its past, but at that time (1900, or thereabouts) found itself slowly slipping into the squalor of a miscellaneous and mongrel existence with increasing tenements, “flats,” and slowly multiplying miseries of dirt and dilapidation, where once had been (away back in 1850) lovely homes, thrifty, American, and enticing, shaded with beauty of trees, and linked with a still more remote and romantic period, when the village of Greenwich caught the reflected rays of the setting suns from the broad waters of the Hudson, and its lanes ran down to the water’s edge.

A marvellous revival had again covered this district, (anywhere from Canal Street northward to Fourteenth Street and between Broadway and the Hudson) inserting a finer phrase of expression and feeling than it had ever known, actually transfigured into a joyful ostentation of gardened homes and spacious houses, enclosing streets of cleanliness and all knit together into a texture of deliberate and calculated comfort. Here was the Mayor’s home.

The movement which brought so pleasantly and improvingly this ancient reserve of the city into prominence was the creation of a Governor’s House within the city limits, and the selection of this region, by the fortunate suggestion of an adroitly reminiscent artist. The city had taken six houses along the present St. Luke’s Place, just north of the little renaissance park, and made there—moving some back from the sidewalk, and taking others away, and adjusting garden spaces around them—a central house, in white and red, with creamy white shutters, an iron fence about it all, and a few white mulberry trees in front.It formed such a pretty picture, and revived so keenly an old time veneration for the historic site of the ancient village, that others copied the city’s example, and from point to point, houses were bought, torn down, or moved, and changed; and slowly, but with a well sanctioned enthusiasm, the blocks, now so dull and unresponsive, blossomed and grew warm, with sunny and shadowed homes. The extent and outlines of this urban restoration, just mellow enough, just delicate and grave enough, embraced the spaces from St. Luke’s Place to Christopher Street, with meandering threads of extension up Greenwich Avenue to Seventh Avenue through Hudson Street, across to Washington Square, in Waverley Place and Gay Street, even at last undauntedly attacking the mercenary interests of Bleecker and Bedford Streets, until a polite and literary or artistic fever thrilled the city, and gathered together groups of such models, filling place after place, till the oasis became charming and entertaining and restful. Like a green and spreading plant that pushes its way over ash heaps, and up dry and ugly walls, so did the stupidity of vulgarized and threadbare neighborhoods become decent, and pure, and lovely, before the tasteful invasion.

This alteration meant a goodly expense. In some instances it meant the appropriation of whole blocks which became under the treatment, delicious gardens, blending delightful affectations of antiquity—with box rows and oleander bushes—with smart contrivances of the day in horticulture. These inter-urban ruralities sent meandering fingers into Seventh Avenue and stretched along Hudson Street as far as the present St. Luke’s while most curiously an outlyer was created in Varick Street near the rescued St. John’s Church where its altheas bloomed against the background of the railroad freight depot.The extension of the movement was then—in the year 2000—well afoot and pressing for more extension.

Tom reached the Mayor’s house in St. Luke’s Place, and presented the Mayor’s card. He was received by a quiet faced old lady who sweetly explained to him she was the mother of the Mayor. He was led through the broad corridors of the house to a back yard, where he found a sizeable garage, and a well equipped vehicle. He was told his own room would be above the ground floor. He felt some curiosity about <i>that</i>, and went up the stairs for its inspection. Perhaps it was not so bristlingly pretty as the room in the Kohinoor, but it suited him much better. It was large, and a book-case on one side filled with the overflows from the house gave it a home like air. The little yard lay under his windows and he just heard the occasional murmur of the city life.

Tom thanked his stars and set about an overhauling of the Mayor’s handsome motor car, in which, that evening, he was to bring the Mayor home from the City Hall.

The day had waned, but for an hour Helen Lorimer had sat dreamily resting her head in her hands, as her arms leaned up on the sill of the broad window, looking straight to the still talus-encumbered slope of the Palisades. It was a strange idleness. She had loosened her hair until it flowed down tumultuously over her shoulders, and she had gazed at her own reflection and even approached the mirror with a smile of satisfaction.As she mused, or planned, or slept,—it was a trifle hard to say what she was doing—at the window,—the door opened softly, and her mother, the dark Julieta entered, and came almost unheard to her side. There was a start, and as the uplifted face met her Mother’s, Helen rose, and said; “the Mayor is coming to see us. I like him immensely.” She kissed Julieta and the two women—both so beautiful and so contrasted—stood for an instant, silent, together. What impulse suddenly raised Helen’s arms and made her fling them about her mother’s neck, and kiss her again with an ineffable tenderness?

Tom had brought the ruminating Mayor to his home, and that alert official had bounded up the front steps pushed open the door, sprang in the drawing room, where his mother sat knitting, and had also put his arms around her with sudden eagerness, and kissed her. It was apparently an unprecedented performance, at least in that way and at that time, for the old lady pushed him back and asked with candid wonder, “Why Marston! what’s the matter?”

THE BEEHIVE

In the year Two Thousand the tides of human travel through Grand Street had grown beyond all measure. They surged from morn to night in swaying multitudes in which the admixture of all the races of the great city were seen and heard. The babel of sound recalled the thoroughfares of a dozen cities of the world. The East Side, problem and despair of the twentieth century, remained the enigma of the twenty-first. Its congestion, at least, had met an approximate solution. Enormous high tenements had been built there, whose clustering stories became microcosms of national amalgamation, wherein were born diverse and blended groups who were endowed with remarkable pugnacity and a sort of fierce ability to engage in political argument and metaphysical civics.

They were not devoid of business sense and an inordinate thirst for money. On the whole they had made a most reputable element in the public life of the city, but they were prone to excitement, and they were not American. They responded with Celtic avidity to rhetoric and poetry, but they were not Irish. They were composed of a mixture that had outrun the mere boundaries of religious prescription, and Catholics and Jews in the close companionship of the Russians and the Italians, Hungarians and Turks had intermarried. These unions had grown more common, and there was a corresponding drift away from the orthodox formulas of each church. Socialism as a theory had taken a remarkable hold upon their imaginations but, though they were poor, there was no immediate justification for discontent in their lives. Pauperism was uncommon.To the resident populations of the East Side, at this point (Grand Street), and north and south, there were commingled the steady streams of commerce and industry that crossed the Williamsburgh bridge at Delancey Street. These converging currents had produced a vortex of business along Grand Street, and so huge department stores, a few big and second class theatres, and tailor or clothing shops with the adventitious association of gregarious restaurants had grown into a solid wall of many hued occupations. At night the streets, especially in holiday time, and on the hot nights of summer swarmed and seethed with the jocund, good natured, and somewhat highly dressed throngs. The little parkway of the bridge’s approach on Delancey Street was set with tables and here gathered companies of friends and fun makers, who were supplied with wine and lager from the restaurants, while at the entrance of the smaller shops or stores similar tables had been instituted. The street then assumed the complexion and the purpose of a great and lengthened caravansary, or, on such nights when the stores were open, and the side walks accommodated the warring peddlers, an open air bazaar, or a Kalmuk fair grounds. The street then blazed with electric lights, and in front of the theatres and the department stores, the restaurants, and indeed at every hand, coronas, arches, and surcingles of lamps, contributed a chaotic confusion of haloes, and reflections and illuminations, that dispersed the darkness beyond the highest buildings, and eclipsed the discomforted and neglected moon.It was in the midst of this multivarious human maelstrom and complex, that Longtinsky, the Russian, had made his abode, and established his paper, The Beehive, with which he furthered his insidious agitations and furnished a not unskillful compound of threats and grievances with the truth half dressed in fiction, and fiction wearing, a little stupidly, the mask of truth. The man had ability, and being unscrupulous, with a tenacious memory and an almost morbid alacrity in hunting out the peccadilloes of the rich (none of them worse than his own and his companions) he had gained a clientele, his paper a sort of subterranean circulation, and himself a modest fortune, though quite incommensurate with his ambition. Some fancied insults to him by the organ of the better classes had rankled in his mind, and he had exceeded propriety and prudence by recent utterances which were seditious and revolutionary; much more so than usual.

He had been goaded into a blind rage also by the unexpected and most unwelcome invasion of the district that he thought was peculiarly fitted for the ripening of his theories of social reform by the successful propaganda of Helen Lorimer. This had been phenomenally effective. Its courage fascinated a spirited people, and Miss Lorimer’s address and personal power, with a well established and practical commission of mercy was making havoc with Longtinsky’s reputation and prestige.It drove him to madness, and the fact that some of his young men-reporters, hired to dog and spy upon the less wary and more undeserving of the class he hated and plotted against, had, in their nefarious work encountered the beneficent agencies set in operation by Helen Lorimer, and having also met that engaging lady at her own settlement, had been flattered or actually been enslaved into a sort of devotion, which made of them furious mutineers against their soi-disant master loosened the last restraint of decency and caution.

Longtinsky understood well enough his danger. He did not propose to hang himself for any crime, but he had no hesitation in getting others into a noose that would have better become his own neck. He had become depraved to the point of murder, and his selfish and inflated egotism, provoked beyond endurance into resentment, slowly degenerated and assumed the worse complexion of brooding, sullen, hatred, the precursor of the worser incentives to atrocity and revenge.

The office of The Beehive was behind a clothier’s store not far from the corner of Pitt Street and Grand. It was entered by a narrow alley which debouched into Grand Street by a covered shed, and might have been easily overlooked by any one not accustomed to use it. The alley threaded, a door, entrenched in the brick wall of a back extension opened upon a small irregular room which served as the editorial sanctum, and the council chamber of Longtinsky, and his sympathizers. Behind this again a small projection shielded the archives and the printing press of the incendiary sheet.It was the day after Miss Lorimer had entered her plea for the reconsideration of the sentence of the convicted Socialists, and which the Mayor had deferred to with such unction, that a rough and shaggy looking man, with a slouched hat, half covering features that might have reasonably craved some such concealment, walked into the open alley that led to the penetralia of the Longtinsky establishment. The man showed no hesitation in making his way, and a shrewd suspicion, that he had been there often before, would have occurred to any observer who noticed his nonchalant ease, the half dissimulated scowl with which he turned towards the street, and scrutinized the passing and oblivious crowds, his slow ingress through the crooked corridor, and the questioning and companionable rap that he gave to the inner door which the next instant he pushed in, and within which he disappeared.

In the furtherance of the ends of this tale we must follow this figure. The room that the man thus invaded was dark, the few windows that were intended to help out its interior illumination being extremely dirty, and in a measure curtained by ragged curtains, that rivalled in their disarray the neglected panes they partially covered. A few gas burners had been frequently used to dissipate the gloom, and the effects of their imperfect combustion were discernible above them, in sooty patches on the low ceiling. At this particular moment, although it was almost mid summer, the jets were alight, and by their helpful aid the visitor, if the matter interested him, could recognize the two pictures which decorated—or were expected to serve such a purpose—the walls. One stared him in the face, as he entered the room.It was a colored chromo of the great Russian novelist and humanitarian—Tolstoy—depicted in his peasant costume, amid his ancestral acres, his hat in his hand, and his crumpled and benign face looking out upon the world he repudiated and failed to save. The other possessed a less perceptible congruity with the room and the purposes of its usual occupants. It represented a most commonplace scene of rurality and domestic peace, and was called Rest at Home. This pleasing pictorial note in an atmosphere that quite generally was filled with threats against all such chimerical fancies, portrayed a farmer’s family, gathered around a hospitably covered board, in which the artist by a series of conventional imitations showed the products of the farm, with the ostensible father of the prosperous and numerous group, standing at one end of the table, his face upraised, and his hand, in the posture of a blessing, stretched out over the abundant food; while his sons and daughters in attitudes of respect or reverence, formed before him a most irreprehensible assemblage of duty and devotion. The alert mind might have thought that this unexpected illustration was intended, as an eloquent reminder, to those assembled below it, of the very thing and things they were commissioned to upset and prevent.

The remainder of the room’s furnishings requires but a few words. There was a long narrow table—so narrow as to comfortably allow those who sat at it to attack their opponents or critics across it—in the centre of the room, a few chairs, an additional oil lamp on the table, and in one corner a wooden box of the sturdiest construction, armed at its corners and along its sides with iron. The door leading to the editorial precincts beyond was open, and the few operatives there, could be seen leaning over their tasks or engaged in conversation which seemed curiously low and timid.The man whom we thus persistently have followed, overturned a chair at his entrance, and then striking the table with his fist, proceeded to reveal his lineaments more completely, by removing his hat which he threw on the table. He was a short thin man of a Sicilian type, with black hair which would have abundantly screened his small head, if it had not been so suspiciously shaven into a crop of bristling points. His deep set eyes, rather high cheek bones, prominent nose, regular mouth, shaded by a restrained moustache, a handsomely chiselled chin, united into a facial whole of properly noticeable contour and expression. Craft was marked in his face, and a physiognomist might have been readily led to prognosticate a sum of evil possibilities in the Italian visage, which again in less questionable environment might have been mistaken for that of a star of the opera. So easily do the reflexions of our surroundings lend a deleterious, or responsible air to our physical personalities!

He wore the clothes of a workingman, somewhat refined by a little more careful preservation, and by more expensive texture. One surprising and noticeable feature of his dress was the small shoes which clothed his feet, made from patent leather, and a scarf and pin, the former of a brilliant scarlet, and the latter of emerald, expensive items which shone all the more conspicuously in their plain and discordant associations. As he rapped the table he flung himself into a chair, and waited for the response which apparently he fully understood would quickly follow.He was not disappointed. A heavy movement of chairs in the neighboring apartment announced the approach of his host, and the next instant the broad squat figure of a man filled the doorway, between the rooms, and the visitor looked lazily up and accosted his friend.

“Well, Tinsky, have you heard the latest? The Old Man has given a reprieve and the thing’s to be done over again, and I don’t feel sure that the nippers won’t get a twist on your wrists this time. There are some squealers about, and perhaps they are as little anxious to get the ‘tickler’ as you.”

A curious smile covered the speaker’s face, and at length he raised his eyes, previously dropped to the floor, and gazed at the editor of The Beehive with a queer suppressed grin of malice, that was distinctly irritating.

The man thus accosted was a teuton in type, blond haired, thickly whiskered, with blue eyes, inconspicuous eyebrows, a short and heavy frame and a disproportionately developed mouth, and—his most striking feature—immense ears that protruded from the side of his head with an unquestionably porcine saliency. For an instant this aberrant arrangement caught the eye of his observers before they fully scanned his face. The face itself had a mean look, and this arose in part from the lowness of the brow and the smallness of the eyes, but particularly from a misshapen nose, full and drooping cheeks, and from the formless gap across his face, which served as a mouth. Had any one unexpectedly encountered him under circumstances of possible danger they would have instinctively shunned his company and suspected his intentions. His eyes squinted slightly, and this added to the sinister and depraved expression of his face.In some lights, and under the influences of conviviality the editor of The Beehive might have appeared more reassuring. At this moment pricked into a momentary aggravation by his remorseless friends, his looks were disappointing. His eyebrows drew together, his little eyes shone vindictively, his great mouth twitched with anger, and his malformed nose, set between them like a red proboscis, displayed its cavernous nostrils. Longtinsky was distinctively unpleasant to see at that moment.

“Oh, the jig is not up yet, Pony. I am not afraid of the tyrants. My arm can wither at my side before I’ll stop the crusade of justice and liberation I have begun. What about squealers? Do you think I have anything to fear? Can you or anybody else put any crime at my door. I don’t want to hear any nonsense and I won’t listen to any threats. Am I the man to be chased to death by a mad rotten pack of smooth faced, slick tongued, wine-fed vampires of wealth, men who do the bidding of that besotted purse-proud boasting wench, Helen Lorimer? The speaker had risen in his imprecatory rage, and his body, not visibly afflicted with any signs of want, pressed against the table, while the voluble spirit that possessed it glared through the shrunken blue eyes at the stranger.That person quite imperturbably waited for the exhaustion of the Socialist leader, and even ventured to raise an impatient hand as if to check his speech, while at the few moments when he condescended to look at him at all, something like a masked grin covered his features. “Say!” it was Pony who was talking, “what do you think I am. Perhaps you had better keep your mouth shut and the tongue in your head tucked a little closer to your lamb-chops. The effect of those adjurations and reproaches upon Tinsky was remarkable. It only offered another tribute to the force of truthful words, forcibly uttered. The editor had lost some of his color, some of which was temper and much of which was beer. His hands nervously plucked his beard, and he looked at his stormy interlocutor with real dismay. His bravado had suddenly vanished, and in its place a sullen discomforting sense of danger overwhelmed him, but it was resisted by the man’s refractory egotism, and his stolid apathy to advice. Flight meant surrender, impotence, loss of prestige, it might mean detection, conviction, and punishment. He could not reconcile himself to an abject retreat. The danger pictured by his picturesque and implacable friend was a real one, and yet he shrank from an absolute disclosure of ineffectiveness and alarm. Under the smarting sense of guilt his mind, not unfamiliar with labyrinthine devices and coarse subterfuge, worked fast.

There emerged before him a desperate project, which if successful would establish his fame, might help him to rid himself of his embarrassing assistants—such for instance as the man before him—and, blackened as it was with the aims of murder, might finally light those fires of insurrection which he fondly thought were latently glowing in the hearts of the people, would clear the way for his assumption of power,and—so vividly did the picture work itself out—lift him into a position of eminence. Once there he might hold the balance of power and dictate the terms of his own acceptance and the shape of a future socialist plebiscite. The outlines of this plan—if plan it could be called which involved many elements of crime—had been vaguely worked out before in his audacious and credulous mind, but now at the storming point, when the enemy was forcing him to bay, when the incredible play of chance seemed to stretch out to his waiting hand the implement to strike the blow which would bring him immunity and place, they grew more distinct.

The cerebral convolutions behind that low forehead were intricate; thought of some inventive force hurried through them with an easy flow, and designs, pictures, dreams, rose before him with imaginative poignancy. His fear of danger—a danger which seemed literally heralded by its messenger before him—his hatred of Helen Lorimer, the stupid vindictiveness of his nature, which urged him on upon a course of rapine and disorder, his thirst for wealth, the sudden inner consciousness that he had been playing false to his own people—though bribes which he had taken from the “Aristocracy of Wealth” had only been accepted with hypocrisy and rage—his powerlessness hitherto before the inertia of the masses, who had failed to raise any standard of revolt, already broken hearted as they were over the conviction and condemnation of their companions, his perception that he had played into the hands of his enemies in doing as he had done, the residual force of a conviction that he was really working out, besides his own fortune, a greater destiny for society; all these considerations drove him to an ultimate step which he often vaguely realized might be useful.It now became to his mind a necessity. It was desperate, and like some tempting demon, it rose before his eyes with beckoning gesture, though in his hands it carried the weapons of destruction, and its face was darkened with the shadow of assassination. Longtinsky was not averse to involving in his plan some steps looking towards the forcible removal of the unwelcomely candid man who had just entered his retreat.

Longtinsky had been thinking fast. As his visitor paused Longtinsky got up, shut the door leading to the inner room more carefully, and locked it. That character, hitherto quite indifferent and evidently influenced by very contemptuous feelings for his friend, whose nature he knew, as well as some of his subterranean contrivance and plots, had become by this time aroused into curiosity. He looked at Longtinsky a moment with a very disagreeable leer, and then he laughed, picked up his chair and lurched around the table, and put himself at Longtinsky’s side;“See here, Whiskers,” Pony’s voice sank to a guttural croak, “what’s your game now. “Come Pony,” said Longtinsky, with jocular familiarity, “put it away. There’s no harm here for you. You’ve known me a long day and there never was a time when I haven’t been a good friend to you. Put it away. Jessie might come in here too. It would do you no good with her to have that iron out.”

The effect of this apostrophe was remarkable. The man grabbed his revolver, shoved it back in the holster at his hip beneath his coat, a smile entered his cynical face, as a beam of light might illumine into seemly grace a patch of darkness; he looked at Longtinsky with almost a beseeching motion, as he laid his hand caressingly on his arm:

“Tinsky, is Jessie here? Ah! Tinsky, you’re the father of that girl, and I <i>do</i> love her! Blast the folly of it, but I’m a child in her fingers; I’m putty with her. Longtinsky laid his hand on Pony’s shoulders, shrugged his own lightly, and half closing his little eyes, watching shrewdly his confidant, spoke slowly, in a low voice, revealing bluntly his nefarious scheme.

“Pony, I’m not going to admit or deny what you’ve said. “But we’ll drop that. There is danger for us all in this new move. That arch contriver Helen Lorimer intends to undermine us by the hypocrisy of clemency. Longtinsky smiled faintly: he knew he must go on. This announcement veritably startled his hearer. “Good Tinsky—you’re a handsome guy to be plotting murder to save your own skin, and get a poor devil’s neck, like mine, kinked in a noose. Work a little quicker, old cut-throat. You know I like corpses, but just now I’d hate to get into a cold grave myself.”

Encouraged, Longtinsky continued. “Pony,” he began again, “I’ll make a clean breast of it. I blew up the Settlement House. It was a plot; that witch Helen Lorimer wanted her House blown up. She meant it to bring ruin on us. I was fooled. The janitor came to me and promised to open the place if I wanted him to. He left, the house open, and went away—and I dropped into the trap. I meant no harm to the men. Some one had to do it—for the effect—don’t you see—<i>the effect</i>. I thought the East Side would rise,—but the police got wind of it too soon.The men might have got away, and none the wiser but some one peached, and that very night when the house burned, and I had started the revolution—” Longtinsky for an instant looked self-deceived, as if his aspirations for a New Dispensation, with himself as its protagonist was a sacred hope, “—that night every reserve in the city had been doubled, and—you know the rest—six of my men were nabbed with Polinck; the uprising nipped; locust clubs killed the new republic.”

By this time Pony was fully interested; he no longer shrank from the portentous confession. His indolent attention had been replaced by a very alert ear and eye, and not a word was missed, not a reflexion of the utterance, in the face of the narrator, escaped him. He had suspected much of this, some of it he almost could have sworn to. He felt repugnance for the perfidy of the man who could thus coolly rehearse all of this with a callous and untouched heart. But it stimulated his own thoughts, to run rebelliously along a chain of images, in which he saw Longtinsky dead, and himself with Jessie in his arms fleeing the country, the demolition of The Beehive, and his own comfortable existence elsewhere, with a sack of plunder torn from its vindictive and over credulous editor. The dream pleased him, and Longtinsky for once shone to him with an interior and native light of reasonableness, that made him—Pony—urbane and receptive, a transformation not concealed from Longtinsky, whose perception of it only made him more communicative and less cautions.“You see,” continued Longtinsky, “the reprieve will act as a stay to the execution, of course, and the new trial will not come off until the fall. That is sure. There is time for me to undo the mischief we have suffered. I’ll fill The Beehive with storming articles. Careful you know Pony. I’ll rouse the men we need and I will be bold too; I’ll let out at that woman again, and she can come back at me. I can tell her plans to a dot. She will be down on the East Side again; she will put up some shack and start in with her talks. I’ll bait her all right, and get her in some sort of an argument and then—with our men ready—armed, Pony—we do it. <i>The bomb, Pony, that blows her to perdition will win you Jessie.</i>”

The speaker paused, watching with a flicker of madness in his eyes, as he fed by anticipation upon the ruthless sweetness of his vengeance, and the intoxicating prizes of success, the ardent countenance of Pony, to whom the offer glittered with all the radiance of happiness, complete and inexhaustible.

There was a moment’s silence between the men; Longtinsky never lifted his eyes from the face before him, which responded to an inner elation, and grew good and wholesome under the gentle influence of the exultant and comforting thoughts that Jessie’s name aroused. Longtinsky himself felt the benign touch somehow of his daughter’s name, and there poured into his lowering glance the restful smile of a father’s love. Surely Love—thinking of the abused word in the loftiest sense, as the guerdon of unselfish sacrifice, and devotion—can work, and it alone, the miracle of Redemption!Pony extended his hand, and Longtinsky was not slow to grasp it. “Done. I’m game for the throw, hit or miss. But a bargain’s a bargain, and <i>you</i> Longtinsky either stick to it, or QUIT BREATHING.” Again the baleful lights in the eyes of both, and the evanescent ray of Heaven’s sunshine on their faces faded away with the harvest of suspicions springing anew in their plotting brains.

There was a light tap on the inner door, which led to the printing and composing room of the paper, and Longtinsky recognized it; “Here is Jessie now,” he said, and before he could extricate himself from the table and chair in whose legs his own had become wound, Pony leaped to his feet, and opened the door.

How strange are the contrasts of Nature’s works; with what necromantic art she weaves the dreadful and the harmless, the lovely, together! How perfectly she contrives—though there is no contrivance, only the mastery of metempsychosis—to mix her materials, the same material it would appear, and form from them now a demon, and now an angel. Or is it in this wise; that a little, Oh! so very little of some transforming juice of virtue can sway the ferment of a mass of rougher ruder stuff, making its effect at last beautiful, choice, and even more exquisite, because forsooth it has risen from its very opposites. Chemistry in our human arts does such things. Heredity is a fact, and yet Jessie was Longtinsky’s daughter. There she stood like some wild and winsome flower, flourishing in the midst of harshness and commonplaceness and reptilian motives. And so fresh and lovely! The arch play of her smile like a golden beam falling on ragged walls and blanched careworn faces, fell on the two men and their lifted heads bathed in it as if in a benediction.Jessie Longtinsky—her mother was dead—was Longtinsky’s only child, and he lavished on her an unselfish care. She had learnt the most excellent rudiments of an English, German and French education; she spoke all these tongues; she had musical talent, and she developed taste and talent. But best of all the graces of an unsullied nature, a nature so vital and womanly, so confiding, earnest, and hopeful, so searchingly sincere, so buoyant, and yet very brave, dressed, God knows how, in the nobility of goodness.

Such graces were hers, and Jessie became them. They became her; it was an ideality of character and beauty, more piquant and entrancing because unexpected at first sight. It might have seemed almost impossible.

Jessie Longtinsky was fair, her hair literally golden, her features regular and perfectly harmonized, each formed so that it, by the most delightful collusion with the rest, added to the total witchery and charm. If hunting for some particular culprit among them all, that made her win from every one the meed of praise, it might best be attributed to her eyes, as indeed why not, seeing how joyous and soulful eyes can be. Jessie’s eyes were indeed most pretty, and they were not blue, as became convention, in the matter of eyes, with a fair skin, and the story teller’s golden hair. They were the faintest green, though that color craved no prominence in them, and often surrendered its claim, to be considered there at all, to a soft pearly grayness. And they were such honest eyes! Soft and reminiscent sometimes they were, and filling unbiddenly with tears, and then the home of mirth, but perhaps more usually they were tender and grave.Longtinsky had had great luck in keeping his workmen, because they were young, and because they could frequently feast their amazed eyes on Jessie. Some had been ambitious in her regard but none had been successful, though Jessie managed to make the wound endurable, being calm and sensible and considerate. Among the habituées of the Longtinsky establishment—and they were many—Pony, or exactly Poniatowsky, had proved to be the most formidable suitor. Perhaps Jessie did not care much for him, but she humored him, she may have been a little afraid of him, and then her father favored him, <i>or seemed to</i>, and Jessie was a dutiful daughter. Longtinsky, as men will, even of the roughest, or the worst types, had guarded Jessie from contact with his own world of men; he did not converse about the social aims he had, he even kept his own paper out of his house, not because it was dirty or vile—which it was not—but because it was violent, sanguinary, maledictory and abusive. Longtinsky knew that, but these ways were his weapons. Mrs. Longtinsky probably was an amiable sweet woman, Jessie may have been like her, and so her father wanted to keep her, feeling too some masculine aversion to a femininity becoming raucous with debate and policies and doctrines.

As the door opened, Jessie glided past Pony who failed to catch her, and stood apart from the men turning from one to the other as if questioning their purpose to be locked up there. Then she looked around the room, which she seldom entered, in which no trace of that clean housewifery she practised in the story above the press room, and where Longtinsky dwelt, was visible. Her glance was furtive and shrinking, the place was distasteful; its staleness and meanness oppressed her, there lurked in it an unavoidable meaning of crime. She somehow felt that.“Father, do let me change all this. I can. You won’t know it. With clean windows and curtains, fresh paper, lamps, a new ceiling, scrubbed floor, and that pretty old furniture in the attic; why it will be so different, so pleasant!”

“Now Jessie that wouldn’t do at all, not down here. If you made everything spick and span, we couldn’t think as well. It is hard enough sometimes to say what we want to say in the style we want, but because we don’t have to think where we are, and what’s about us, we do better. Come my girl, leave us. Pony and I have some things to talk about, which you aren’t interested in. Go up stairs.”

The voice of the speaker had lost its querulous murmur, and was soothing and even affectionate, with a plausible earnestness that discovered to the listener a father’s heart. And indeed there flickered up in Longtinsky, when Jessie was near him, a tenderness that would have saved him from many unruly and wicked impulses. It was the psychic radiance of her own excellence that brightened the dusky souls that came near her, and warmed their sensibilities, such as they had, into a responsive vigor. Jessie sat down, and a cloud of some sort of sadness passed over her face. Pony had been intently watching her, with now and then a perturbed glance at his rumpled clothes, which were not the habiliments he usually wore when anxious to move Jessie’s reluctant heart.

Pony considered himself the recognized swain of Jessie, though it would have been regarded as a mere piece of self flattery, a quite unsupported assumption, by any one permitted to study Jessie’s attitude towards him.She frankly did not like him, though Pony was at his best with her, and really loved her, and because he did, and was quite willing to go any lengths in supporting his claims, against all rivals, he, by a rustic and aboriginal force of argument, concluded she belonged to him, a conclusion not always agreeable to Longtinsky, and certainly not approved by Jessie herself.

Pony started towards her, with a smile of pleasure in his face, that intimated the probable intention of an osculatory salute, when Jessie moved quickly away to the further end of the table. A frown troubled Pony’s features, and threatened to break out in a stormy expostulation, when Jessie unexpectedly fixed the attention of the two men by a raised warning finger.

“Father, there are men outside in the yard watching the house. They came in through the alley a minute ago. I think they may be there yet. They may be your enemies!” Jessie of course knew her father was engaged in a propaganda of a civic nature, and the best sides of a humanitarian socialism appealed to her quite naturally. These she heard, and was honestly led to feel that the rich were the enemies of her class, of her father, of progress. She was cognizant of the higher types of socialism, as she had become acquainted with it in the books of Hilquitt, Marx, and others.

The recent riot, deaths, and the arrest of the conspirators, had been viewed by her with terror, and yet with an exalted conviction of its necessity. Through the sacrifice of the martyrs, she had been told, Liberty came into the world, and always would so come.She saw the sufferings of the very poor, and she exerted a beneficent influence among them, working with an eager sympathy for their good and improvement. She had in this wise done much to strengthen her father’s power, and the affection bestowed on the daughter was an effectual aid in assisting the projects of the father. But lately Jessie, unsuspected by Longtinsky, had come under the spell of Miss Lorimer, and the result threatened to bring about a little alienation for his schemes. However her love for him was true, and when that day, looking from the upper windows of the rear tenement, in which they lived, she saw the usually empty yard invaded by the strangers, and noticed their equivocal actions, she became alarmed, and had, for this reason, interrupted the unhealthy machinations of the two friends.

The two listeners to this startling news, jumped to their feet, and tiptoed to the window, on the yard side, and cautiously raising the corner of the shade, peered into the little yard which had been made into a grass plot, worn irregularly into bare patches, by the feet of those who frequented it, when, on summer nights, Longtinsky gathered there his sympathizers and co-workers, to smoke, and drink, and reform the crooked and nodding earth.

The view obtained under the lifted curtain was a very imperfect and partial one, but there indeed were the intruders walking towards the door of the room, where the watchers crouched. Neither Pony nor Longtinsky stirred, and Jessie behind them, trying to reassure herself over their shoulders, was quite still.Their common apprehension that the men would next instant present themselves for admission, at the door, was relieved when, with a last glance above them, and a review of the few features of the yard, the new comers turned into the alley and vanished. Pony stole to the door, opened it, and creeping outward, saw them disappear into the street, and the next instant an automobile puffed, shirred, tooted, and moved away through the throbbing thoroughfare, and in it sat—our former acquaintances—Mr. Benton Briggs, the mayor’s secretary, and Tom Ratler.

Now it happened, naturally that Jessie had not detailed the circumstances of her discovery, but that incident had consequences that later developed into pleasing importance. When Jessie discovered Mr. Briggs and his companion in the yard, she was at an open window engaged in watering some of her flower charges, and her particular bent attitude or position, had shot a tide of color into her face, so that when she raised herself and gazed down on the men, the eyes of Tom Ratler met a vision of beauty, which the rapidly working appreciation of that susceptible youth, approved as being the sweetest thing they had ever seen. And—happily let us say—this was not unreciprocated for, in the nascent powers of her own healthy observation, Jessie felt aware that the young man underneath her windows was particularly good-looking.

The immediate consequences of an encounter, fraught with such mutual pleasure, vary greatly, according to circumstances and temperament, but with Tom Ratler it produced a strong hope—not shared by Mr. Briggs—that he might have confided to him further work in the capture of the Longtinsky household.It seemed to him that his intervention was necessary, for the security of the defenceless and innocent maiden connected as he discovered,—from Mr. Briggs—by a filial relation to the obnoxious Longtinsky. As to the effect of the same encounter with Jessie herself, it deepened her repugnance for Pony, and made a fluttering sensation discernible in the neighborhood of her heart, that, with more sophisticated girls, would have been indubitably assigned to recondite and tender sympathies.

There was some natural confusion of mind, a little trepidation perhaps, caused by this interruption, and when Pony had returned, Longtinsky and he exchanged irresolute glances which finally adjusted themselves into a settled stare of wonder, while through all this mute interchange of ocular interrogation Jessie stood apart, with her own eyes questioning each of the conspirators. At length Pony broke out;

“I tell you Longtinsky: it’s cut for you. That young chap is the Mayor’s shover, and the old dog is his secretary. They’re on a spying trip; looking the ground over, and before you can say Jack Robinson they’ll have the service men down here, and the whole outfit under lock and key.”

While he was speaking Longtinsky’s head was, metaphorically, whirring with the soundless revolutions of all its interior clockwork, wheels, cogs, and pins. He saw too, that there was a suggestion, in this sudden visit of inspection on the part of the Mayor’s forces of just such a predicament and complication as would allow him to reanimate and reestablish his prestige.He could now, if the attack was made, the suppression of his paper attempted, raise an outcry against the tyranny of such suppression, the danger of Star Chamber proceedings against the freedom of the press and, sounding the tocsin of alarm over the encroachments of government upon the Palladium of liberty. He might add recruits to his cause, drawn from a class usually out of sympathy with him, while his own followers could be roused into an acute resentment. This latter fanned into a blaze, might create the kind of conflagration he needed for his own escape from censure, and for the completion and execution of his felonious designs. These possibilities rapidly formed themselves in his mind into a logical chain of events, which expeditiously utilized, would immensely further his plans.

He turned sharply to his daughter and said: “Jessie go up stairs, and leave us. You were right to tell us about these spies, and now Pony and I must talk over the matter. Do go!” He approached his daughter whom he truly loved, and putting his hand around her neck kissed her with an ardor that reacted dangerously on Pony’s self-control. That envious gentleman hurried also to Jessie’s side, and was in the act of throwing both his arms around her, when she sprang sideways, escaped him and fled.

Pony’s pursuit was unsuccessful, and he fell over a chair which sent him sprawling against the wall. He was up in an instant, and furious with disgust and disappointment. He would have renewed the chase, had not Longtinsky shut the door past which Jessie had fled, through the press room to her own quarters, and placed himself in the way of the infuriated lover.Pony stopped; it was not Longtinsky’s formidable frame that caused him to pause; a sudden thought of the handsome Tom Ratler, whom he had seen before, connected itself with the flight and reluctance of Jessie to submit to his embraces. The idea suffocated him. It made him fall back into a chair with a groan and a curse and then his hands clutched the table, he shook it, as if the desperation of his jealousy would reek itself upon this insensate object.

“Damn him,” he cried, “let him come between me and Jessie, and I’ll tear his liver out of his black body. No I’ll do better than that. I’ll mark him for life; I’ll slash his pretty face into strips, and break his legs. A monkey on crutches. Ha! Ha! A fine figure he’ll cut then,” and the fiend gloating over his imaginary revenge drew his knife and struck the table before him with repeated blows.

Longtinsky watched this outbreak with some satisfaction. His acrobatic mind saw that through the incentives of this jealousy on the part of Pony, he could engage that desperate man to do his bidding. He could involve all his enemies—the antagonists of his propaganda—in this man’s hate, and when the moment arrived and the blow was to be struck he could inflame his exasperation to the point where Helen Lorimer—Longtinsky’s rage against this woman partook of the nature of a mania—would become confused or identified with his personal rival, and, relying on his furious love for his daughter, through Pony’s arm, he might strike her down. At present he must decamp, but so arrange things that the police should seize the place—which he would make an empty trophy by its evacuation—and then he would elsewhere publish his grievances and ring the changes upon an outraged liberty.“Listen to me, Pony,” said Longtinsky to the glowering lover, “all that in time, but now we must get out of this, and save the plant. To-night you must help me to move. There’s a house up Pitt Street that I can get into and there we will go, Jessie and all. In spite of them all we’ll print The Beehive, and we’ll make it hotter than ever. Understand! Come! there’s no time to lose. Remember you stay with us and—” it was his decisive stake “—play the game right, and Jessie is yours.”

Pony leaped to his feet and seized Longtinsky’s hand and the two men disappeared into the inner sanctum.

That night and for some succeeding nights there was much smothered activity in the region of the office of The Beehive. Pony and Longtinsky now banded together in a confirmed partnership wherein the motives of the second outstripped the merely egotistic designs of the first, were busy with the removal of the various archives of the socialistic paper, and engaged too in a wholesale destruction of the more compromising issues of the past year. They worked efficiently and Pony derived a gratification from the occasional help of Jessie who had learned from her father of the impending removal. The old office was left intact with its signs and furniture but the presses were taken to a new retreat in Pitt Street, not far from the site of the ruined Settlement House, and the preparations made for the continuous printing of the paper, but, under the fear of some impending blow, their distribution was secretly arranged, and at the same time the bitterness of their editorials increased.They instilled insidiously a poisonous venom against society, and they craftily wove into all their allusions the name of Helen Lorimer as the embodiment of the wrongs the deluded readers were convinced they suffered. Yet all this was not unopposed; the contingent which Miss Lorimer had gathered around her at the Settlement was very faithful, and Longtinsky regarded with alarm the preparations for rebuilding the Settlement House. The moment must be soon improvised for his blow, wherein the purpose of outright murder steadily and grimly deepened.<b>CHAPTER VII</b>

THE MAYOR’S VISIT

The Mayor had been very punctual in keeping his engagement with Miss Lorimer. He was standing in the wide parlor of the Lorimer mansion into which he had been ushered by a servant apparently already apprized of his expected visit, so felicitously suave was that person’s reception of him, and so unhesitatingly was he conducted over the hallway to the great parlors.

The room or succession of rooms, in which he found himself was a superb example of architecture and decoration. The effect of a kind of compromise between colonial spaciousness and simplicity in the proportions of the room, and a Louis Quinze elaboration in its furniture, was not as disquieting as might have been anticipated, seeing too that the villa in many aspects was an Italian creation. The ceilings were high, the windows broad, and almost destitute of the framed arrangement of heavy mouldings; the cornice of the room was scarcely emphasized, the windows descended to the floor.

The decorative features were a treatment in gold and pink with the chairs and sofas upholstered in Du Barry satin, garlands of painted roses delicately imprinted the flat ceiling, high mirrors framed in gilt surmounted marble mantels overhanging generous hearths, and the august dignity reached an intellectual note and emphasis in the few large and many smaller paintings which were, with one or two exceptions, the works of American artists, and were, for the most part, landscapes.The three exceptions were religious subjects, being carefully executed copies of The Assumption of Murillo, The Flight by Correggio, and a Holy Family by Domenichino.

Great rugs were spread over the marqueterie floor, Du Barry pink satin curtains, overspreading lace portieres, hung at the windows, a grand piano was accommodated in a swelling niche on one side, and the chandeliers intricately composed, and thrown into a sort of bizarre relief by white enamel threads upon them, supported a galaxy of opalescent globes lit with electric lamps.

It was evening and in summer. The Mayor entered the brilliant and glowing room, walked admiringly towards an Inness which hung almost opposite the entrance of the parlors, and then sat down in a convenient chair, his eyes eagerly inspecting the delicate opulence about him. Near him was a remarkable table—a creation in ivory and ebony—and he noticed a few book-cases holding books bound in white, a few marble sculptures were revealed, and a snowy owl, upon an ivory bracket loomed upon him as at least, if not tasteless, an indiscreet note in the beautiful rooms.

A moment later and his eyes encountered, approaching him from the distant end of the room two female figures, one of whom he quickly recognized, though now Miss Lorimer came to him in a sort of aureole of summer emanations—her dark hair unadorned—and wearing a faintly blue ashen gown, the sleeves caught thrice about her arms with black velvet loops.
