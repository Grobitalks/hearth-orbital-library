<b>CHAPTER I</b>

TOM RATLER

It was a picture of wonderful beauty, with elements strangely transporting. The sky was clear, with vaporous vestiges of a thunder storm, whose dark draperies of lightning stricken depths lay effectively on the southern horizon; the moon was at its full, and its unimpeded rays burnished the waters of the harbor with a silver splendor. A group of men and women on the upper and forward deck of an incoming steamer were the silent spectators of this scene as the steamer lay anchored towards the New Jersey shore of the harbor of New York in the coming year of grace 2000, and as the lingering spring, with an already lost freshness, was passing into the warmer flushes of the summer. The margins of that great bay which once at such a time had been dark with the clustering volumes of leaves upon the arms of encircling forests, was now girdled with a zone of artificial light. It did not encroach upon the domain of the moon’s regnancy, but it seemed to throb nebulous and soft upon all those shores like a titanic wheel of fire. It represented the last triumph of electrical science. It was an electrification of the air above the cities, and that air was palpitating with shooting wreaths of color that gave, for each changing instant, a new festal brilliancy. The gazers were shocked into an admiring astonishment, which only had an audible expression in murmurs of delight, or took the form of appreciative exclamations.The particular glory of this crown of illumination over the cities about the harbor was its irregular surface or outline. Above the island of New York it rose far up into the outer spaces, burning with an exquisite rosiness; above Staten Island, where it was carried still higher, the color was a strangely mutable blue, and over the lower regions of Long Island and New Jersey it lay in auroral sheets, that every now and then, with a billowy exhalation, shot up phosphorescent projections that seemed actually to detach themselves, and float away like luminous clouds. It gave a spectral splendor to everything and on the deck of the steamer, and at the feet of the mute on-lookers its reflections for an instant shimmered and vanished. But the moon, unchecked by this terrestrial rival, shone on, and its limpid refulgence on the faintly rippling waters lost none of its charm, in contrast with the schemes of artifice around it. In the penumbral glory dark lines appeared, which may have been unilluminated and cavernous streets, while its changing lustres revealed many a towering turret, especially over Staten Island and New York island, and terraces of light marked the arched bridges and their precipitous piers, over the East and North rivers.

The steamer, whose occupants thought they had arrived at some celestial fairy land, was a coast wise “tramp,” and had steamed from Portland, Maine, with a cargo of stone and lumber. Among its passengers was a sturdy youth from the lumber lands of that state—lumber lands which yet, with decreasing resources, strove to supply the markets—who had stolen away from home, and determined to try the tempestuous and stirring arena of the huge metropolis, that now, at the dawn of the twenty-first century, expressing the awfully expanded powers of man over material things, expressed too his illimitable luxury,and held within its area an unfathomable possibility of strife and devastating conflict; a city that had become the centre of the world of thought, confining the seething tides of a radicalism that pushed forward a militant atheism against the still struggling standards of the Church; wherein too lay the ripe and bursting seeds of an ambitious artistry that would scale the heavens and reincarnate the flesh of man in an immortal youth, while it lit all the torches of a brazen defiance of the moral law. Tom Ratler had heard of this tremendous city, and his blood boiled to meet its intoxications and his mind delighted in the dream that he might scale its dazzling heights, and linger long beneath the canopies of its indulgences. He possessed those things which go to make the conquering man, pride, courage, an instinctive readiness in danger, a supple mind, a compelling passion dressed in all the allurements of a strong and noble form, his voice had chime and music in it, and a certain spirited demeanor held attention and enforced respect, and all this not unreasonably yoked with judgment and no inconsiderable self-control.

Tom had been born in the Aroostook country of his native state, and had lived the fresh healthful life of the fields. He had availed himself of the advantages of a high school, had read, and, belonging to a farmer family of excellent reputation and commercial prestige, wore the garb of good manners, and listened to the incentives of good taste. His mother was a woman of exceptional purity of heart, and his father a rugged Ajax skilled in extemporaneous prayer, and not unskillful in hammering an opponent to the ground. Tom was a young giant perfectly moulded. His size did not surpass natural limits of comeliness, but it filled with caution its unlucky antagonist.His face was fair, and the rounded bullet-head not strikingly expressive of scholarship—was sheathed in crisply curled hair like a Grecian Apollo, his mouth broad and ingratiating held an armory of ivory teeth—the charm of all mouths—and his eyes of chestnut, were sweet and tempting, by the side of a straight nose and beneath a just obviously receding forehead which however was a little narrow, while at the union of the nose and forehead, a depression gathered a few lines of tension around these formative kernels of flesh and bone, which, to the phrenologist are the signals of observation.

None among those on the deck of the Barrymore had been more astonished with the miracle of science and loveliness before them than Tom, and he thirsted eagerly to reach this prospect of delectation, and plunge into its weaving web of life confusion and intrigue. As he leaned over the rail gloatingly feeding his eyes on this vision of delight, and somewhat apart from the others, he heard, and then he noticed a movement at the stern of the boat, and on the side, which was followed by the swish of oars and the emergent outlines of a row boat. He ran down the rail and whistled softly. The next instant the row boat was alongside revealed from stem to stern in the moonlight, with one figure in it, a Whitehall fisherman or “dockrat,” who was looking for such chance opportunities of profit as a newly arrived ship afforded in passengers, freight, errands, or—less meritoriously—in “smuggle.” His keen ear had caught the sibilant invitation of Tom, and he quickly brought his little boat immediately beneath that interested person’s observation.Tom whispered to him to “wait,” and then hastily disappeared, returning in a few moments with his modest dress suit case, which vanished as quickly as it had come over the side of the ship, and was unceremoniously succeeded by its owner, the latter making use of a loose rope fastened to the taffrail of the steamer. The row boat shot out into clear water, and Tom then became the object of expostulation and entreaty on the part of his former companions, who no less eagerly than himself, wished to reach the planetary wonder before them. Tom paid slight attention to these remonstrances, and urged his guide to renewed efforts, while he seized an unused pair of oars, and added an impetuous speed to the rocking and arrowy motion.

But Tom’s participation did not last longer than was necessary to bring him closer to the docks of the city. Then he drew in his oars, retired to the stern of the boat, and looked around him. And need he had for he was within the scope of the opalescent marvel, and its stupendous loveliness alternately crushed him with amazement, or roused him to enthusiasm. His position may be understood from a few contemporaneous land-marks.

The dancing row boat lay in that criss-crossed and vexed acre near Governor’s Island, where the quarreling tides of the East and Hudson rivers form a patch of troubled waters. On Tom’s right hand was the island, now kept as a military reservation, but one hundred years later forested and mantled with flowers, marked with paths, and surmounted—in the old Fort Hancock—by a conservatory, while the ancient keep of Castle William still remained, embraced in climbing vines, with its roof converted into a garden of roses.On his left the flaming pharos on Bedlow’s Island still burned, but in the hand of the colossal figure there was no longer an electric star, but she held a torch of flaming gas, whose long, wayward threads of fire the winds now hurried upward, now stretched in horizontal films. Near this dramatic station the immigration buildings on Ellis Island were seen framed in light, and thence northward in all directions, along the great rivers and in the median zone of Manhattan, tall structures climbed upward, now dark by reason of the desertion of their human households, but bathed over their summits with the descending rays of that incredible aurora that filled the air above them. It was yet early evening, and immediately in the foreground of this nocturnal spectacle, was the old Battery park twinkling with lamps, in the intervals of a renewed forestation which made it a dark spot on the water’s edge, yet crisply dipt in a silver bath, that came from the moon, mingled with a penumbral halo that came from the electric arc far above it. Tom could now see the reason for the irregular elevation of the luminous ceiling over the city. It was due to the varying heights of the tall buildings that seemed to climb into the skies.

The refulgence was also not everywhere; he could see that over broad spaces of the peopled shores in Brooklyn and New York black lacunae occurred where the shining curtain was absent. The boat had pushed its way through the strife of waters and was nearing the little Whitehall basin which then as now, afforded a retreat and harbor to the battery boatman. Tom overwhelmed his pilot with a generous fare, and jumped quickly to the landing, hurried up the stone stairs, and was in New York, ready for adventure, not disinclined for mischief, and fully committed to the wayward guidance of his fancies and his fortune.His heart glowed with delight, and his mind leaped forward in anticipations of forming a part in that shifting scene, which had drawn to it the eyes of the world, for here again, as in ancient Rome a tumult of social collisions had arisen, mingling with miscellaneous and picturesque confusion with the last expressions of mechanical and architectural daring, with the fevered promotions of every luxurious taste, with erratic dissipations, and with a mental ferment of scientific and literary and philosophic speculation.

Strangely there reverted to Tom’s mind from out of all his desultory but avid reading the picture of Apollonius of Tyana arriving in Rome in the cruel days of Nero. The book of Mr. Tredwell’s he had picked up among a nondescript collection of odds and ends at a country-side library to which gathered the tailings of private scholarship, the chance offerings of missionary enterprises, an occasional presentation copy from an enterprising publisher, and the discarded extras of a newspaper office. Tom in a restless and skipping manner had scoured these books for diversion, for instruction and as youth will, for intimations, palatable but unhealthy, of the currents of life in the channels where life moves most boisterously and least restrained. Apollonius of Tyana quite caught his fancy. That wandering figure, well furnished with the goods of the world, appropriately screened from danger by a half supernatural grace, wearing the garb of a preacher, and well drilled in local customs, gentle with the bravado of spiritual superiority, and not averse to finding out what the world contained, suited his own dreams of action, though the companionable guardians of Tom’s fate in their seclusion among the stars, might have smiled and shed tears over the hazardous attempt to put Tom Ratler and Apollonius of Tyana in the same category.Tom was not Apollonius, nor was New York in the year 2000, the Rome of the year 50, though filled to the brim with seditious, dissolute, serious and aggressively progressive elements. Tom however—in his own thought—was seeing the world, or about to see it. As he gazed upward that on-coming evening at the dark bulwarks of the shrouded city, intermittently bathed on their summits and then down their cliff-like sides by the aerial and diaphanous glows in the skies, Tom felt the thrill of the unknown advancing towards him, and all his eager senses reached out to receive its message and its gifts.

Nor was Tom a Nigel Olifaunt, the young Lord of Glenvarloch whose adventures in London town at the beginning of the seventeenth century Scott so humorously sets before his readers. He had come upon no errand of restitution; and while his handy hand and emulous spirits might engage him in some unlucky business, and his spirited resentment bring him within the limits of a metropolitan Alsatia he was not likely to become the victim of so unscrupulous a knave as Lord Delgarno, nor run any risk of being tried for treason to the state. If any comparison will help the reader to put Tom Ratler in his proper phase of action let him recall Kenelm Chillingly’s justification of his abrupt departure from his father’s home; “I go in quest of adventure like Amadis of Gaul, like Don Quixote, like Gil Blas, like Roderick Random; like, in short, the only people seeking real life, the people who never existed except in books. I go on foot; I go alone. I have provided myself with a larger amount of money than I ought to spend, because every man must buy experience, and the first fees are heavy.”Tom certainly bore little resemblance to that accomplished cynic, and would have heartily derided—if he understood them—most of his paradoxical conclusions, but like Kenelm he had left home to see the world, and see it, where at the beginning of the twentieth century it presented the most contrasted garniture of forms and sentiments.

Tom noticed that while many persons were walking along the sea wall of the Battery Park, and the settees were occupied by many couples deeply interested in their mutual felicities, and a few families were engaged in animated discussion of their household affairs, prospects, and designs, the larger numbers were gathered around a central point, whence the strains of very enlivening music proceeded. By reason of its elevation, the band—evidently of large proportions—succeeded in conveying these strains to the outskirts of its acoustic domain, where Tom found himself. This concentration permitted him to walk unimpeded around the wall, against the beautiful and shining bay, looking towards monstrous beacons of light on the hills of Staten Island, the shimmering walls of the Narrows and the nimbus of rays that played over the populous shores of Bay Ridge, no longer retained as a semi rural contour on the edge of the Harbor.

Scarcely conscious of any definite intentions, where all around him invited him to continue a delighted and aimless ramble, Tom reached the walls of the Aquarium, now occupying the old foundation of Castle Garden, and, at the distant period to which we transport the reader, yet retained, but immensely heightened and most inordinately ornamented.Tom could not clearly see the chase of zoological detail over the glistening terra-cotta sides, though salient representations of whales and porpoises, dolphins, tunas, tarpons, sword fish, and sharks were descried by our traveller, whose astonished eyes however were not always well enough informed to report their correct identity. His eyes, bright and eager as they were, quite failed to discern the wealth of marine naturalism, in sea urchins, coral groups, medusas, hydroids, sea-cucumbers, molluscs, which encumbered the ornamentation, mingled with the larger motifs, and luxuriantly clustered at every interval between the swelling and pompous immensity of the cetaceans, and the giant fish.

It was at this spot that Tom had reason to recall with gratitude the agricultural training of his muscles, and to be roughly reminded that the splendid city to which he had come was not unqualified in its apparent possession of peace and order. In going round the circular outskirt of this immense pavilion Tom reached its northern walls approaching the confines of a great basin for the accommodation of motor boats and naphtha launches, small pleasure yachts, and a miscellaneous assortment of less definite water vehicles. Here the shadow of the building was less successfully dispelled by the park lamps, and as Tom turned to leave the angle, prompted to do so by the deficiency of light, three figures seemed to spring from the pavements, and with a consecutive movement too rapid to be easily discerned, to fly at his back and front aiming fist blows at his head and neck, and vicious kicks at his shins.The most listless admirer of self defense, or the least intrepid of its critics, would have rejoiced to have witnessed the lightning-like metamorphosis in our whilome inert wanderer. His valise dropped from his hand, and the motionless arms—so long and powerfully braced with muscle, multiplied themselves with incredible rapidity in a Briarean thicket of appendages before whose centrifugal violence his assailants quickly vanished. The scuffle attracted some notice not divorced from a desire, on the part of many, to see the stranger worsted, and this sentiment was reinforced by the sudden approach of a policeman entrusted with the wardenship of this quarter. A sullen group of men and boys, vaguely referable to the Syrian section of Washington Street, followed Tom, and their inquisition of his appearance grew less and leas favorable, while a few epithets, accompanied by a chance and unsavory missile in the shape of rotten fruit acquainted him with his unpopularity.

Tom experienced a faint sense of disappointment, but his own warlike instincts, readily aroused, permitted no movement of retreat, and his disdain of his cowardly critics finally prompted him to turn and walk with threatening alacrity towards them. This movement served to disperse them, and as Tom remarked their skulking shapes, he was joined by a much older man who had watched with interest and sympathy the shifting relations of Tom and his tormentors. The stranger spoke, and his measured and pleasant tones fell refreshingly on Tom’s ears, to whom, overwhelmed by the immensity of his new surroundings, the incident had seemed painful and unlucky.“I see you are quite able to take care of yourself. I was ready to take a hand myself against this bunch of loafers. I guess that you are a new-comer, but don’t misjudge us from this little adventure. The city is fairly orderly, as much so as most large towns, and you can safely ramble over it at any hour of the day or night, without much danger of molestation, though”—here the speaker drew a cigar case from his pocket and handed its open end to Tom who with a slight hesitation engendered by unfamiliarity with smoking, took one of the proffered Wizards, as his new friend continued;—“though in some ways we are dangerously near a domestic conflict much more serious than street brawls or the incivility of hoodlums and nocturnal poachers. Come over to the sea wall and sit down. I don’t wish to presume upon the acquaintance my admiration of your strength has given me, but as an older man and one not indifferently acquainted with the conditions and the opportunities of this city, you may welcome some friendly directions and advice.”

“Indeed, sir” answered Tom now thoroughly interested, if not quite assured as to the honorable intention of his interlocutor, “I should feel grateful for that. I am not a baby, and I believe that I can pull through easily enough, but a few pointers at the outset may save one from dangerous or expensive blunders. What is learned without cost can be used without loss, is an old saying with us in Maine.”As the two men moved towards the edge of the reservation it was easily seen that Tom surpassed his companion in height, and if this were not a sufficient assurance, any incipient suspicion of danger would have been dispelled by the striking visage of Tom’s acquaintance. He was a man of middle life hardly showing any of the usual symptoms of increasing years. His hair, the weakest defence against time’s encroachments, was yet untouched with gray, his face was unmarred by a single wrinkle, his eyes of a peculiar brilliancy, betokened intelligence and frankness, and the broad mouth, firm and rather narrow lips, the chin perhaps a little too massive, confirmed the unmistakable impression that our young adventurer had fallen in with an unusual man—a man of observation and experience and convictions.

Tom had himself quickly noticed these details and his confidence increased, and even a sudden impulse of affection sprang up in his heart as the momentary sense of his extreme isolation deepened into a half reminiscent sadness. They had come up to one of the many seats facing the bay, and the older man motioning to a bench, cut up into separate seats by iron straps, sat down, while the end of his cigar shone vividly as he puffed away, apparently abstracted, and making no further overtures at conversation. Tom himself had become thoughtful and silent, though he fully expected that the dialogue would be resumed without his encouragement, and his mind dreamingly fell into that mixed revery and consciousness, which overcomes us at such restful moments. The waters before them, dark and viscid in their unctuous flow, with scolloped concavities glazed with the moonlight, lapped softly against the stones of the wall, and the band of music, recoiling from some tempestuous climax, was breathing over them an enticing melody.It was Tom who first broke the silence between them.

“I suppose I can expect good employment in New York, sir,” he said, yet unwilling to expressly declare his purpose. There was no answer; and Tom looked more inquisitively at his neighbor, fearing that his favorable deductions might have been premature. Again he spoke; “I had a fancy that in this city of rich people I might get a good job as motorist; I understand machinery, have used a steamer myself at home, (as indeed he had), and reading of fair pay given capable men in New York came on for employment.”

His companion woke up, or at least alighted from his soaring meditations, turned a little towards Tom, and without the slightest reference to Tom’s inquiry and declaration, began a deliberate sketch of the social conditions of New York, its political dilemmas, and its industrial power, almost as if, in response to some unheard interrogation, he took advantage of the occasion to deliver himself of a carefully prepared disquisition, though he conferred upon Tom the honor of being the subject of that inaudible question.

“Yes, my friend,” he began, “New York has become the microcosm of the whole world. Here are gathered together the nuclei of every school of thought, every phase of social state is here; the abject poor, the fabulously rich, and the industriously competent. Our population is over sixteen millions. We are spreading to the furthest limits of our territorial domain. Every art is practised here, and here each art secures its highest rewards. Architecture has here risen to its most stupendous and impossible tasks. Music finds here its greatest audiences, and its exhibitions take place in the grandest temples.Painting has here displaced the former superiority of France; factories making every article that human ingenuity or human appetite can invent for its satisfaction or its profit, are here in operation.

“We have become the cynosure of the world, and one million of visitors a year has reversed the tide of curiosity from the old world to the new; because our enormous wealth permits us to build marvels and realize dreams. We have become a state, and this city now possesses its own legislature, and frames laws for its own government. Enormous universities furnish us with learning, and under the impetus of an outrageous intellectualism large classes of our population discard old habits of life, old creeds, old morals, struggling with an impossible ardor to compass the meaning of life in the surfeit of enjoyment and the rhapsodies of speculation. The Catholic Church almost alone upholds the dogmas of the past; Protestantism has deteriorated in Agnosticism, and Agnosticism falls a prey to a devouring melancholy, which councils a happy death, whenever life forgets its pleasures, or Age robs the senses of their uses. Euthanasia, although a forbidden crime as the enemy of the state, is secretly practised and from day to day sumptuous Suicidariums are discovered and destroyed by the police.

“Science makes contributions, to our convenience, but is still dumb on the questions of our purpose and origin. The transmutation of metals is said to be almost discovered, and when gold can be made from lead the tocsin of industrial activity will be sounded, and the social machine, no longer able to maintain financial differences, will run down, and, clotted with a metallic lubricant—no longer serving its just ends,—will stop working,and our social state resolve itself into a primordial protoplasm, whence the savage tribe with chief and slaves, will slowly emerge, and the earth begin over again its tiresome political evolution.

“But just now in this city the questions of policy grow daily more complicated and dangerous. We have a gloriously rich class enjoying the highest culture and withal benevolent, but living in undreamed of splendor, and we have an educated class of operatives and middle men with a proletariat of the very poor, who, often by their own misdirection or proclivities, live stunted and desperate lives. These classes form a vast body proclaiming a radical socialism and their organization grows stronger and stronger with increasing population, for family life, except within stringent limits, is discouraged by the rich, and is despised by the <i>emancipated</i>.

“The balance of power will soon fall to the socialist, who lives for breeding, and breeds by living. You have just come to our city (and here the speaker for the first time turned directly to Tom, and involved him directly in his allocution), at the close of a general election for Mayor—the Mayor being the governor of our city-state. It was a hot rancorous contest, and the successful candidate was a compromise, a fusion brought about by the rich classes, the moderately well off, and the Catholic labor vote. The new Mayor’s position is a perilous one. He has hardly a bare majority in the city Legislature.He himself is a dreamer, and over balanced with metaphysical guesses, well trained in psychology, not inexperienced in business, but at best a compromise, chosen to be the tool of the rich, yet constitutionally a believer in some creed about the Brotherhood of Man, and so without absolute direction, and without force; and so also a farce, and a failure. Well—” (here the singular monologue ended, and the speaker, who more and more astounded his absorbed listener, stood up) “Yes—there are good chances for a chauffeur of the right stamp in this city, where over ten per cent. of the accidental deaths occur through motor accidents. Have you any money?”

Tom no longer doubtful as to the integrity of his friend—his rhetorical tirade convinced him that he was some one with reservations of conscience—answered, as he also rose to his feet:

“I have three hundred dollars. Enough?”

The stranger had resumed his musings, looking away in the direction of the Liberty Light whose flaring chords of flame now rose straight into the sky like a contorted and tapering pinnacle of fire;—but he heard; and turning again to Tom he said; “Very good. Go to the Hotel Kohinoor near the Times Square. Get well lodged; Look well; Dress well, and put an advertisement in the New York Times for a position as a chauffeur. Give the hotel address. You will have answers to choose from; mark me! Take my card! I will see you at any time.”

He handed Tom a card, and walked away after cordially shaking his hand, though as he did it, he looked less at Tom, than at the dispersing crowds who,no longer kept together by the band, were dissolving in streams through the Battery grounds.

Tom moved to a lamp and read:

      BENTON BRIGGS
(Secretary to the Mayor)
        CITY HALL

Tom of course was duly impressed, and thanked his stars that the assault on him had been made. He placed the card carefully in his pocket book, and proceeded forthwith to find out the location of the Hotel Kohinoor. He found the policeman who had aided in interrupting the machinations of his enemies, and from him was directed to the hotel which his informant assured him; “was the all-firedest most expensive house in town.” Tom perhaps quailed a moment, but he partially divined the philosophy of Mr. Briggs’ advice; he wanted a rich employer, his own qualifications were, he knew, unimpeachable, a little spurious distinction given to him by his handsome quarters would not form a prejudice against him; on the other hand it was intended by his adviser to form a direct appeal to the fastidious personal tastes of the rich employer he was after. He intended to be careful about his advertisement, and he had quite enough reliance upon himself and his good looks to improve a favorable impression.

Ruminating over these things Tom found his way to the Subway which in the year 2000 maintained its supremacy as an artery of travel, and was flashed through its blazing conduit, to Forty-Second Street, where he alighted.The hotel Kohinoor occupied the present combined sites of the Metropole and the Saranac and represented the most munificent expenditure of skill and fancy known to the publican world.

It was the marvel of two continents. The most daring mechanical device had been united with the strangest and most defiant artistic conceptions, and a result roundly denounced by the better taste of the day, was also blazoned and paraded as the consummate expression of the New School, a hackneyed phrase devoted at all periods of art, to give distinction to oddity, if not to the vulgarity of empty display.

It was a monstrous pagoda of stone and stucco, ornamented with extraordinary grotesques of great size, and involved a curious use of colored decoration with surprisingly copious development of electrical illumination. At night the tapering structure, prolonged upward to almost two hundred feet became a shimmering and splendid spire of light by reason of chains of lamps swung from tip to tip of its projecting antennae, each chain from top to bottom being swung, as it were, by a mounting line of grinning goblins and laughing nymphs, rendered in terra-cotta and affixed to the ends of the antennae, which, on each story numbered twelve, making a sort of angulation of the whole building, and these polygonal faces were constructed to form as many façades, and a different treatment was allotted to each in ornamentation and color.This facetted and rather brilliant composition had suggested the name Kohinoor and, as a term descriptive of scintillation and gem-like glory, it applied well enough to the chromatic and photal encrustation of this singular architecture, whose effrontery of design drew to its doors and rooms the richest and the most eccentric patrons.

The interior arrangement was not less striking nor less effective, limiting that term to the range of ideas expressed in surprise and disordered novelty. It consisted, in outline, of a central air space, or well, reaching to the apical skylight—itself a fantastic medley of colored glasses—conical in form. From the contracting areas of the successive floors, encircled at each floor or story by a gallery, whose edge upon the conical well was protected by a <i>rococo</i> railing of brass, hung aprons of English ivy. The ascending floors were designed as radial spaces with diverging corridors producing rhomboidal rooms, and the corridors opened at their outside limit upon a broad balcony, cut up into stalls, for the occupants of the rooms.

The ground floor, entered from the street by ascending steps, was a labyrinth of columns, arranged in stars, and holding between them groined ceilings luxuriously painted with multitudes of enlacing figures, borrowed from all the stages of life, the personages of history, contemporaneous character, the idols of the theatre the legends of poetry, figures of mythology; an intemperate extravaganza of portraiture, in whose chaotic investiture and train the impudence of the artist had placed politicians in compromising union with goddesses, and mingled the decorum of the pulpit with the bacchanalian antics of the chorus hall.Momentarily it was denounced as a scandal and later it was welcomed as a joke, and at all times it crowded the place with sightseers and gathered to it a spendthrift retinue.

Lounging chairs and sofas of a deep cherry red, imitating coral stems and growths, upholstered in a sea green tapestry, filled the space at convenient intervals, and every hour was signalled by soft utterances of music from a mammoth orchestrion. It was all in bad taste relieved by extreme skill, but from top to bottom of the building, and throughout its details the loosened revelry of libidinous fancy was given full play. For want of a method it chased over the known world for the exotic, the bizarre, the beautiful, and brought them together in all phases of contact, producing a colossal maze of features that could only, with trained artists, react in a monstrous and terrifying weariness. It was principally a marvel of decorative ingenuity, picking from the resources of industry every thing rare and costly. What might have been styled a system of house gardening prevailed within, and patches of flowers were installed in ample vases and bowls, themselves richly conceived and executed, kept alive by artifice, and stimulated by electric shades.

Into this unusual hostelry Tom Ratler came with no inconsiderable trepidation, provoked, and naturally enough, by some apprehensions as to the sufficiency of his pocket book to meet financial requirements which might conform to so marvellous a place.He found that however, in spite of its splendor, it could afford him in its skyward limits a small room at, if not exactly a small price, no ruinous toll. So he was whisked upward in the elevator tube that, as a steel tube in open work, represented a towering and stupendous palm, extended through the central air-space, with bridges at each floor for the escape of its travellers.

The room was tiny but deliciously neat and, to Tom’s eyes, ravishingly beautiful. It was in enamelled white, with a wall paper that began at the bottom with a flush of white blossoms on a dainty pink ground, and ended at the top in pendent boughs bearing yellow—golden yellow—oranges. The furniture was made from a delicately stained green wood, designed in patterns of rustic simplicity; the windows were shaded by crisp chintz-like curtains, whose figures represented various idylls of the country, from chestnutting to May-day frolics. A soft downy red mat or rug covered the floor, and water colors of charming freshness, delineating the undressed pleasure of the seashore, decorated the walls. A little bath room opened off this fascinating boudoir, and Tom standing in the centre of the bewildering beauty and comfort, dropped his hand valise, whistled softly, and for an instant thought of his own plain bed-room at home, in which, were the reader able to follow our friend’s dream, a well stored book-case, rude but serviceable, occupying an entire wall, brimmingly filled with books of all sizes and on all subjects, would have been revealed.

Tom at once attacked the task of preparing his advertisement. The writing escritoire was supplied with pen, paper, and ink, and a few moments only were necessary for the rough outlining of his “ad” which changed a few times finally took this form;WANTED—A position as a chauffeur by an experienced driver of automobile cars. References furnished. Address Tom Ratler, Hotel Kohinoor.

Tom again descended the elevator shaft; was directed to the Times newspaper office opposite to the hotel itself, entered his application, and, unwilling to yet trust himself to the throbbing and blazing streets, retired to his exquisite aerie to think of his new surroundings, and to gaze over that carpet of light punctuated with brilliant orbs or crossed by sombre shafts of stone, that like an undulous and phosphorescent scarf floated over and through the Capital of the World.

And to Tom’s musing eyes there seemed to be at times speeding meteors in trained or panoramic succession passing in the sky. And there was. For to all other marvels of the growing years Science had added the partial conquest of the air.<b>CHAPTER II</b>

HELEN LORIMER

On that long and high ridge known to-day as Inwood that extends from about 155th Street and Lafayette Boulevard to the northern edge of Manhattan Island where the rocks with moulded and spherical outline dip into the peaceful currents of the Spuyten Duyvil Creek, in the year 2000, wealth and art had created an encrustation of the most wonderfully beautiful architecture, harmonious in expression and imposingly ornate. It was called—the region—Carrara, a term signalizing the almost unbroken use of marble or limestone in the construction of the palaces that dominated this rocky and precipitous comb of vertical schists, tumbling picturesquely into the waters of the Hudson.

Looked at from the eastward elevations at Fort George, itself at the beginning of the twentieth century a public park, extricated from the present serpentine horrors of scenic railways this Inwood slope presented a splendid façade. It borrowed features wrought in dreams and pictures, it contained delicate and noble effects incorporated in stone, blended together with unerring mastery, and linked with each other throughout its entire length, as if in a tracery of architectural wonders. Imagination, controlled by scholarship, played here its part with subtlety and composure. It dealt with creations, and it dealt with them as the Creator has dealt with the world, through the imposition of Law.A little of that bizarrerie and witchcraft, rampart elsewhere in the stupendous City, had faintly, yet noticeably touched the august and serene construction, but had touched it as the coruscations of golden edges touch the towering clouds at sunset.

It became almost roseate under the spell of some transfiguring sunsets, dark and effective beneath scowling skies, and again shone like alabaster in the zenith sweeping sun. Conceive a long heaped hill of chateaux, built entirely of marble, except at a few telling points relieved with colored stones, their principal pediments crowning the summit of the hill, and their approaches—that on the Kingsbridge road—an ascending terrace of steps, with broad platforms hedged with green thickets, plentifully sprinkled with wide and capacious vases, opulent in flowers and trailing vines—the trumpet creepers sometimes lit up the snowy walls with orange carmine coronas—here and there a noble tree, usually a pine or fir, fitted with artistic precision, cut out, cameo-like, against balustraded loggias. Conceive the crest of this hill, a sky-line of towers, minarets, angulated gables, flat roofs—on which again by a somewhat indecorous fancy flowers were strewn—and clotted ambuscades of spires. Pass beyond the eastern exposures to the westward, and imagine a contrasted aspect over the steep escarpment of the Lafayette Boulevard; see how, limited by the precipitous declivities there, the multiform expansions on the east have changed generally into flat walls, graced indeed by niches and balconies, overhanging extensions, but rising with perpendicular austerity to considerable heights. It was as though a growing and lovely crystal had flattened against an impregnable partition, and, revealing its color, concealed its form.The topography of the region compelled this curtailment, since the villas could not well be built over the ragged and vertical edges of the cliffs, though by an extreme audacity, at points, with great expense, the ornate or diversified architecture had been pushed out over the beetling rocks, by means of iron piers estopped in the gneiss beds, whereon, upon a platform of iron also, the structures had enlarged symmetrically to their formal type.

This partial abbreviation was not necessary in all cases, as where the platform at the summit of the ridge was wide enough to allow the development of the building. The hill side on the east, thus constructed in related and harmonious architectural contours, summoned an enthusiastic admiration. It represented the aristocratic isolation of extreme wealth, in a class born to indulgences, fostered by education, and not devoid of intellectual distinction.

And Carrara represented a wholesome unity of purpose; in an artistic sense its design was consolidated into an expression of the renaissance, and except at a few spots, where the prevalent taste for irregularities and the conceited thirst for strangeness—a strangeness based on kaleidoscopic conglomerations—had impertinently entered its purer and poetic thought, like a hectic weed among chaste flowers, the aggregation was a lovely structural symposium.

Northward, where now the protecting spire of the House of Mercy pierces the ambuscade of trees, towers small and round, large and square, many affixed in corbelled tiers to angled mansions, and some, campanile-like, aspiring upward with tenuous and diminishing lines, filled the sky.Further south on the declining slopes that bend to the Dyckman road an interfering group of flat topped Norman keeps, their walls broached by wide embrasures, with brick chimneys on their sides, picturesquely soaring above the interlaced and somewhat compactly combined trees, brought the eyes of the reviewer to the more stately Italian villas, that decorously encumbered the southern section of this long Inwood elevation.

The harder rugged contours northward, with their architectural austerity, here met an expanded and luxurious invention. Extreme taste and the insinuation of transatlantic memories were apparent. Park-like expanses along the Kings Bridge road, traversed by paths, ascended to the marble stairways whose stately and interrupted ranges led to the marvellous villa palaces irregularly delineated on the crown of the ridge.

Among these was one which by some idiosyncrasy of ornament, some bewitching adjustment of parts, and predominantly by a rare invocation of the graces of plant decoration drew the attention most quickly, and held it by a richness of detail, controlled by refinement and discrimination. It was throughout coherent in style, with such exterior embellishment as to attract notice, without going so far as to disfigure its technical virtue. Its architect would have suited Mrs. Rennselaer’s requirements of being “content with conforming his structure to its function, and modelling the masses thus arrived at, so as to heighten their inherent expression.” Yet he had taken his renaissance quite sincerely, and absorbed with devotion the mainsprings of artistic design it furnished.The mansion was Italian, and by reason of its scholastic fidelity freed itself from the aberration of the treatments which had become rampant in the City elsewhere, and which, to revert to the same authoress’s denunciation of an earlier forerunner of this boisterous school assumed “rather the character of an explosion, than of an evolution.”

The villa was not however a stone fortress, such as guarded the treasures of the Florentine nobles, nor precisely those less simple constructions of Rome. Deprived of the foil and the accompaniment of the water, it yet simulated the style of the Venetian palace, and rose, a tetragonal block of incised and decorated masonry, its stories separated by ribbons of ornamentation in balustrades, their windows arched and spandrelled, their pilasters grouped and salient, with the corniced roof dropping in pendant spindles of cut stones, while still higher rose a low parapet pierced by circular apertures. Sculpture and chiselled delicacy of the brocaded stone surrounded some of the basement windows. These latter continued to the floor, became doors, and these opened upon the highest terrace, itself fenced with columns separated from each other by a low intricately cut wall, while each carried on the flat top of its capital an electric lamp within an opalescent shade.

The obvious incongruity of this pile on the table top of the ridge was successfully masked by the addition of a grove of trees on either side, and by the spreading terraces, that with a medial convexity and deployed edges, melted into lateral coppices of bushes.The terrace formed by this peristyle was the entrance courtyard of the mansion, and the distant observer gazed upon the facade of the house, through a wide interval between the columns themselves, which could be closed when desired, by two richly designed iron gates.

This composition possessed the rational elements of beauty. An intellectual grasp gathered its not over-charged detail into a unit of purposeful expression. We are to enter that beautiful home and within it again the evidences of culture, and the outlays of fortune, would fascinate us, but they will pay tribute, in our minds, to that woman, who, like Hypatia in Alexandria sways the heart no less by her loveliness, than by the strength and depth of her nature, Helen Lorimer. This challenging spirit, clad in feminine allurements, had been called in the socialist paper of the day—The Beehive—“the demoniacal Syren of Injustice.” This abode of cunningly devised beauty is hers.

The few words dropped by the Secretary of the Mayor to Tom Rattler in his fortuitous encounter with that young man, and which may have sounded enigmatical, and certainly ominous to the reader, may now, at this stage of our history, be usefully enlarged upon, as it will bring this unusual woman into intelligible relations with the coming events to be here described.

Movements throughout the twentieth century had reflected that changing molecularity in the social stage, which presaged new and perhaps perilous rearrangements of its political constitution.Their potency was more conspicuous in America, than in Europe, for in America the mobility of the social fluid was more established, and the solvent of opinion worked more rapidly and with less opposition; the organs of publication and propagandism plied their trade utterly unfettered, and not without admirable aplomb and sincerity. Similar movements of course had made themselves felt even more obviously in Europe, and had provoked repression, that was violent, but successful, for in Europe so deeply rooted and inexpugnable are the tradition and the fact of class, that power had only to appeal to the military support to still maintain ancient forms. In fact it had long been felt that the armies of Europe were less necessary to maintain existing states than to curb and extirpate the brooding forces of insurrection and discontent, less necessary to defend frontiers than to establish garrisons as a network of protection against internal foes. Throughout large portions of Europe a certain sloth of mind, the inertia of custom, the niggardliness of choice, and a certain artistry in the common people with an undeveloped culture, attached them to the livery of caste, and created a residue of resistance to radical change.

Still in Europe there had been trouble, and the arguments of socialists and other more unrestrained and dangerous, illicit and quarrelling cults, had kept the social cauldron boiling, and the ci-devant crust, formed over hitherto quiescent and hidden contents, in many places was broken into fragments, rotating and pitching in a turbulent commotion. But that, at the exact moment of this story, had subsided. In America prosperity and education, which had reached deeply down to the lower areas of society, had resulted in contrasted conditions, but in a no less active and indeed more energized demand for some sort of renovated and metamorphosed state.And for this reason. The Trust system had enveloped everything; and quite acceptably to the majority of the people. Established in the nineteenth century, its first status had considerably changed, since, by reason of the regulative legislation inaugurated by Theodore Roosevelt, it had instituted enormous popularizing movements throughout the nation, whereby its stocks of all sorts became distributed, so that separated and numerically large communities were its beneficiaries. The dangers predicted one hundred years before, of its subversive power over justice, and its high handed and conscienceless demolition of competition, seemed to have disappeared, as in the first place the trusts were forcibly regulated from Washington, and in the second their power over trade had practically exterminated the individual merchant, or left him a tributary to the Trust’s needs.

Their avenues of emolument were unimpeded and they were numerous. But however much they had absorbed the savings of the laborers and middle men of all sorts, of course, immense fortunes, rolling up into colossal sums, had accumulated in the hands of a rather vast group of rich men and women, and the tireless development of all the utilities of the state, and the resources of the soil, with an enormous average prosperity, had augmented swollen thrift, and created fortunes which were the marvel and the gossip of two continents.

By the mere fact of their almost superhuman possibilities of indulgences, these rich classes, at several points in the nation’s domain had gathered into brilliant and luxurious colonies, and in New York they had concentrated into a nucleus of really dazzling splendor.Now contrasts work the ruin of states, if interested agitators and the exorbitant envy of malicious men, or even the plausible volubility of self-deceived reformers attack them. Physical conditions in New York were not bad at the beginning of the twenty-first century. Science and a most munificent and extravagant government had given to the populace almost everything they might reasonably expect, but there were poor and poverty, a great deal of it, indeed, and amongst the dreams of the socialistic philosopher was the abolition of both.

New York city had become a state with its governor (the mayor) and a legislature, and it had incorporated the elements of socialism quite momentously, in so far as the street cars and all public utilities were controlled and operated by the city government, the ferries, telephone, telegraph systems within the city’s limits, and it was, at the era we are now studying, contemplating exercising some sort of discretionary power over the disposition of wealth beyond a certain amount in the interest of public improvements, public hospitals, and public entertainments. It had advanced the ideas of public and free education beyond all previous conceptions of convenience or propriety, by maintaining a free theatre, and a free opera, whose primary institution had in view the presentation of classic art in the drama and in music, but which, under a lax administration and the encroaching influences of a deleterious gayety, had declined somewhat from these ideals.For it must be remembered that socialism had at this time assumed the features of a most frank and to our mind, most repellent naturalism. Without suffering from need and lacking the incentives of acute deprivation, the poorer classes had attached themselves to leaders, some of whom as to-day were rich men whose political ideas involved the utopia of universal competence, individual equality, radically extended to every function and faculty of man, and they mingled with this irrational aim a cloud of violent vagaries, in which age disappeared, sickness, the family, traditional religion; and humanity, by the preposterous hocus-pocus of their blandishments of words, became something different, ineluctable, and divine.

As a matter of fact, in spite of all this vaporing the visitor to New York in the year 2000, from this present year of grace, would have found a prevalent social resemblance to the civism of to-day. The theory of the suffrage remained the same, also its use, courts of law tried cases, the police repressed crime, the streams of people in the streets presented sharp and informing contrasts, papers reported the news and, with art and circumspection, discussed their meanings, occasional correspondents ventilated their nostrums or their wit, the book stores and the publishers still fed the eye and mind with elegant books and sumptuous illustrations; in Wall Street the same fevered haste and nervous throw of guesses; elsewhere the marts of trade plied unremittingly their business of getting money, and dissipation and amusement claimed their votaries, and claimed them with a freshened splendor of apparatus and intoxicants.He would indeed see churches, cathedrals, though his distinguishing observation would have noted the vast predominance of the catholic houses of worship. One astonishment might be expected for him; it was the larger size of the men and the robust beauty of the women, together with the appearance of a type of face especially pleasing, in which an artistic or poetic fire dwelt, a suggestion that would have been provoked in his mind by a certain depth of coloring, by a suavity of demeanor, a graciousness of address with a predisposition to expressive smiles, a luxuriance of hair, and essentially by dark and luminous eyes.

This type had been projected, through intermarriage and domiciliation, from the Italians, and somewhat, by a remarkable refinement of face, produced in Hebrews who had married Italians. The Italians had developed in America an extraordinary and consuming patriotism, with an incessant and almost belligerent skill in making themselves felt in its art, and its politics. They had eventuated into an important fibre in the national issue, and markedly so in New York. As a rule this type were smaller than the average in height and size, and again this larger type belonged to the Germans, Americans, <i>in stricto sensu</i>, and the English, and this macropodia, as the faculty designated it, had resulted from health and exercise.

But our anticipatory visitor would have quickly noted the physical magnificence of the great city, its inordinate ambitions in building, in design, in contrasts, together with the evidence of a positive mania to erect statues, itself a most illuminative proof that the age had undergone some mental or emotional changes, since that period, one hundred years before,when the claims of art in the streets had either been neglected or awkwardly remembered by limping executions. This change again had been effected by the Italians, in conjunction with the strong American school of art. And among all the marvels of the metropolis, Carrara, in its marble glory would have most acutely raised his marvelling admiration.

This unique precinct, with its amazing glory of architecture blending into an expression of consummate taste and boundless wealth, the reader has already been asked to notice. A more difficult task for the writer is encountered in imposing upon his imagination the character and the temperament and mind of Helen Lorimer whose force and fascination swayed so effectively the people who built and inhabited this region of delight. Here again a retrospective study is necessary, and the reader must inspect the phases of this woman’s ancestry. Helen Lorimer had not been the originator, though she was, at the moment, the most influential and eloquent exponent of a remarkable movement which had elevated the rich into an intellectual distinction, which had made of them a class by themselves, not from the possession of wealth, but from an indisputable eminence of aspiration and achievement. All the rich were not certainly in this class, but the processes of selection and sympathy had separated into a phenomenal isolation and a real prominence those, who, conjoined by a religion of practical ideals, met the requirements of a very exigent and noble standard, wherein also grew a lofty and not altogether prepossessing pride.As early as the end of the nineteenth century a few rich men had begun a process of self-appraisement and conciliation. Rockefeller, Carnegie, Stanford, Widener, Mills, Morgan, Jesup, and others had signalized their oneness with the great majority, and their personal sense of responsibility, by large gifts to the common weal, both in benevolent and in educational foundations. They established a certain nobility of conduct, as essential to wealth. Such an impulse, elaborated and added to, in the prerogatives of superiority, had developed, in the closing years of the twentieth era, notably by the powers and talent of a group of the rich, who had achieved eminence. <i>Noblesse oblige</i> in them had assumed an expression of high motives, painstaking industry, and deliberate culture. They established a most curious system of self inspection. They insisted upon an aristocracy of talent, and scholarship, and prowess as well. A social congress among them settled the possible career of the younger members of their class, whether in art or literature, or public service, and this settlement was quite rigorously recognized and obeyed. They aimed at a splendid publicity before the people and they won it. At every point—such was their programme—they intended to make their class felt as powerful and as illustrious.

And it was the doctrine and the policy of self protection. The more astute had felt the dangerous—in their eyes—movements of the day and they knew that a high bred and insertively practical and ornamental prestige before the world, would protect and establish their union with the state.The indication of talent among the young of their class was noted with rapture, and the young possessors were pushed along prescribed paths of prescribed duties; so that this wealthy aristocracy were influential at all points in the civic circle, and shone with an enviable brightness before the country. Relentlessly pursuing this plan they even went outside of their class to ally to themselves new blood to engraft upon their own stem the strength of its promise and gifts, wherever it was discovered; and these new recruits, chosen—but with circumspection—anywhere, were caught up into the heavens of culture, and their talent augmented by education and opportunity, while they themselves, body and soul, were incorporated in the caste that became their sponsor and host. It was a really singular phenomenon, the inward growth of a realizing conviction that a rich society should knit its life with the nation’s in a meritorious service, where, ever and anon, the fire of genius lit a resplendent flame.

This movement, at the beginning of the twenty-first century, was at its zenith, and among the most noble, and intellectual of its exponents and actors, was the beautiful woman, Helen Lorimer. It was said of her that she combined the brain of a Harriet Martineau, the soul of a Florence Nightingale, and the bodily glory of the Fornarina. In her conjunction of circumstances and the generosity of nature had united to form a very perfect being. The blood of Italy was in her veins bearing its passion, and the blood of the New World bearing its sense. She was a dreamer but she lived in the field of action. She painted her horizons with pictures, but she fashioned the instruments of her designs with judgment, and made those pictures part of the solid earth.Phraseology with her became a portent of new plans, and the plans vividly recalled the language that depicted them, without losing much of that fidelity, when they became facts.

Miss Helen Lorimer presented to the world the fusion of traits and powers, which, like some admirably contrived fruit in nature, was the result of lineage. Not that her descent involved any reference to distinguished forebears, but that a union of characters had developed in her which heredity had secured from a cross in stocks, whereby, to use a Mendelian phrase, the dominant traits were all good and remained, and the recessive were all bad and had disappeared. Her grandparents had been Italians, who in a love match had come to America, and had quickly won success, so that their children, one of whom was the mother of Miss Lorimer, secured all the advantages of education, with those of travel.

Her grandfather belonged to a good family of Florence and her grandmother was a fashionable belle in the city of Genoa. Their elopement provided romantic incidents, in so far as the parents on either side, united in a frantic effort to separate or intercept the runaways, as they had completely disarranged some prepared alliance, commercially more valuable. The chase ended with the sea-side, and Antonio Ferrara and Francesca Espozito enjoyed their married bliss uninterrupted, and surrounded themselves, in their new home, with a family of five children, the third of whom was a girl of physical perfection, possessing musical talent and a voice of melting contralto richness.This popular addition to her native charm, and the excellent social and business position of her father made the young Julieta a widely popular <i>debutante</i>, and, at some public Ball, she had ensnared the affections of Robert Lorimer a scion of one of the richest of the New York circle of millionaires and a devotee of art, naturally not inattentive to the enchanting sweetness of the young Italo-American girl.

Young Lorimer with the studied gracelessness of artistic and emotional youth failed to inform his family of his desires and designs, and, realizing at rather a late moment, the value of the family recognition of his matrimonial choice, contrived to introduce his fiancée to his family’s notice under the following promising circumstances. He inaugurated a series of impromptu concerts which were given at houses on the Carrara. In one of these Robert arranged a picturesque situation depicting himself as a wandering artist, ravished with the beauty of an Andalusian orange grove, and less impersonally seduced by the maiden, who, lingering behind the other gatherers, at the close of the day, turns on him her inexorable eyes and just as a sentimental and well regulated moon sheds a mellow radiance upon an abundance of Florida Grape fruit heaped up in a New York market basket.

The lingering orange picker of course was the irresistible Julieta of whom the wandering and transfixed artist asks a number of irrelevant questions, and on expressing a surmise that she probably could sing is answered by the sudden outpouring of a melodious Spanish love song, in which Julieta, herself hopelessly entangled in the same silken chords with the handsome Robert Lorimer, exerts her skill to immoderately confess her own predicament.The trick was a complete success, and the young woman rapidly won a technical position, whence her introduction as the daughter-in-law of Algernon Lorimer, the head of the Lorimer establishment, though somewhat disparaged by the timidity and decorous hesitation of the mistress of the same institution, was definitely accomplished.

Robert Lorimer inherited his father’s brain and much of his mother’s consistent delicacy of feeling, and developed what was quite undiscoverable in either, a deep artistic creativeness, less signalized in broad conceptions than in subtlety of composition, and the mysticism of shadows, half lights, and spectral shapes. Julieta magnificently completed her conquest of music. Her vogue was immense, and she and her husband engaged with enthusiasm in that regulation and elevation of the rich classes which we have pointed out which became a consecration and mission in their daughter Helen.

Helen was their only child, and as upon her would develop the trying responsibilities of the Lorimer millions, and as also she revealed precocious gifts of mind, with a startling beauty, Robert and Julieta lavished upon her what might be called a bountiful care. In other words, they took every imaginable means to enrich her mind, to mature her nature, to refine her feelings, without losing sight of the immense opportunism nature had granted them, because of her beauty, to make her glad and buoyant. And the subject of all this care responded with precision.She grew daily in graciousness not unmixed with roguery; she engaged her time with scrupulous regard to every demand, and fulfilled the promises of her parents in music and art. Her taste became more massive than her father’s and her music more German than was the flavor of her mother’s luscious Italian melodies. She had advanced upon her parent’s standpoints, and they admiringly noted the firmness of her grasp in scholarship, the speculative earnestness of her reflections, and the unexpected depth of her religious feeling. In a sense and way they felt a curious awe of her, as if her thoughtfulness betokened some dreadful reaction of weariness and collapse, and they watched her at times with sincere anxiety, and with whispered comments of mutual confession.

But Helen ripened with absolute normality. She loved friends, she entered the customary diversions of society with a vigorous thirst for all its reasonable pleasures, and she brought to her feet the young men who found her delightful in discussing a regatta, or a lawn tennis tournament, or in the tripping coquetry of winning raillery, and who left her enthralled, without dreaming (except as common talk gave her reputation) that she could narrate the history of the popes, or give them a review of the early systems of American philosophy, or even with less effort, set before them the triumphs of modern music. In one aspect of human learning Helen Lorimer was apathetic. She spurned science; not indeed with self-satisfaction and disdain, but by a quiet repulsion of silence when its vaunted triumphs were rehearsed, and its importance was mentioned.Something in the hard and fast outlines which science gives its topics, something especially in the sodden matter-of-fact dreariness of its professors, and yet more relevantly, in her case, something repellent in its unmistakable rejection of the unproven and the imaginative. Its war with religion jarred her, and she withdrew from it as from a monstrous and fatal thing which would wreck the beauty of the world of poetry and art. At the age of twenty Robert and Julieta went with her to Europe, to its capitals, its galleries, its historic centres, completing an exhilarating survey with a prolonged visit to the city of Rome. The impressions left by Rome upon Helen were critical. They influenced her whole life, and made of her instantly the instrument of accomplishing the most momentous religious change that the modern world has seen. It was at Rome under the seraphic touch of inspired painting, under the thrill and the mystical appeal of antiquity, under the occult fascinations of symbolism in its ritual, that Helen imbibed faith in the Catholic church, and threw herself at once into its arms, without misgivings and with a trembling joy.

This was not wholly unexpected by Robert, who in many conversations with his daughter had been struck with her reactionary tendencies in thought, and her many museful inquiries into the power of the Roman church, while in a natural way the habit of Julieta, brought up a Catholic, reasserted itself with the ardor of resurrection in the younger woman. The matter at first gave her parents some concern, but in so cosmopolitan a temperament and so loving and soothing a nature as her father’s, there could not be long a permanent objection, and in the case of her mother there was just a secret satisfaction.And so Helen Lorimer became a Catholic, and her profoundest affections turned—at first lightly—to a strange project, one barely to be mentioned aloud, one only to be dreamed over in the darkest hours of the night, or brooded over in the uttermost seclusion of revery and anticipation; <i>Helen Lorimer yearned to bring the Pope to America</i>.

It was a mad thought perhaps, some Catholics might have deemed it iniquitous, and most would have found in such a plan the dissolution of the church from all its temporal and historic verities. But Helen was not thinking of Rome and its associations, perhaps not clearly concerned was she at first with the Catholic church itself.

She was intensely patriotic, she felt a divination of the vast political tendencies of the age, and she saw around her in the states a disintegrating Protestantism, or thought she saw it; with too, a very militant Catholicism, she believed the restitution of religion to the country she so ardently loved could only come through a momentous application of sacramental grace, and that, she also believed, would be brought by the Pope’s removal to America. She had indeed become entirely familiar with the savage dissatisfaction of a large portion of the Italian capital’s population with the Pope, and she knew that the body of Leo XIII yet remained unburied in its selected sepulchre at the St. John Lateran, the beautiful basilica outside the immediate limits of the City, because of this dissatisfaction.She indulged too, in other fancies, as that the Pope, taken away from Rome, and placed in America, would mean a renovation of the church itself, an infusion of the fresh blood of her country, with its unselfish attachment to ideals, in the parched and worn channels of circulation in the church, so that its altars might more worthily carry the weight of that supreme sacrifice which daily fed its votaries. The emotional tides of her being, stirring now with restrained and yet compelling force, swept her on into pageants of speculation, where her susceptibilities received the stimulus of dazzling and painted pictures, pictures of the church’s triumph.

When Robert and Julieta returned to America with their wonderful daughter in whom they began to see the promises of a powerful and creative personality, they encountered the secularistic movement among the rich which we have partially hinted at. Nothing could have better suited the lofty temperament of Helen Lorimer, with its aristocratic isolation as also its sane industrial zeal to add to the beauty and usefulness of things, than just this attempt to give a new and special significance to wealth, building up a group, a class, a caste, eminent by talent, by accomplishments, by public devotion, and so enshrining their heritages of mere money within the protective virtues of character and mentality.

Helen turned to this new thought with all the energy of her temperament, and wrote, talked, missioned, gathered around her coteries both of men and women, who received power at her hands to accomplish the desirable result, and her home—fully surrendered to her for her own best purposes by her parents—became the headquarters of the secularists.Older heads than Helen’s, also saw the usefulness of this. They knew the widening strides of, to them, an unaccountable socialism; they heard its threats; and while nowhere else in the world was there such remarkably distributed competence and household happiness as in these United States of America, at this one point of New York socialism raised an ominous head, showed a spiteful tongue, and at the last election—that referred to by the Mayor’s secretary with Tom Ratler—manifested a most unwelcome and alarming numerical power.

It was well and necessary that socialism should be robbed of its influence, cut loose from the moorings of discontent by the utility, the generous attitude, the fine quality in its acts, the nobility of its sentiments, the individual strength of its members, of the rich. Helen had advanced all previous schedules of action in this particular. She had given public lectures, had entered the enemy’s country, had translated the lessons of history to her popular audiences, and had immensely swayed her listeners by her eloquence, and no less by her conquering beauty, a beauty which came to them dressed in the habiliments of an opulent taste, for Helen Lorimer instinctively rejected that shallow condescension which would palliate differences by false pretences, and the masquerade of imitation.

Her strength had indeed disconcerted the editorial corps of The Beehive, whose younger members became as much reconciled to her views by her face and form, as they might have been by her syllogisms had they taken the time to carefully rehearse them. Helen reiterated the philosophy of regime, and dwelt on the analogies of nature. She contended frankly for the gradations of society, and urged it as a biological law or as a principle of physics.She popularized Hobbs and she disseminated Montesquieu. She christianized Hume and lent a new flavor to the amenities of Talleyrand. She was a success, and The Beehive had retorted by epithet and innuendo.

In time the windows of life darkened to Robert, her father, and he died almost in her arms, a deeply loved parent. He had watched his daughter with a gracious pride, and an almost pathetic obedience to her slightest wish introduced him to her intimacies of thought, and made him the confidant—more than her mother—of her unusual hopes. On her father’s demise the Lorimer family consisted of Robert’s older brother Theodore, her mother Julieta, and Helen herself. Theodore was a childless widower, and although somewhat more severe and practical in type—being a good business man—than his brother, fell too under the fascination of the gifted woman, who now represented the residuary heir to the immense wealth of Algernon Lorimer, her grandfather, a Napoleon of finance with more enduring qualities than this misused appellation usually connoted.

In many ways perhaps Helen Lorimer recalled the image one might form of Margaret Fuller, but it could not be said of her, as Miss Martineau had said of her distinguished contemporary, that she lived and moved in an ideal world, “talking in private and discoursing in public about the most fanciful and shallow conceits which the transcendentalists of Boston took for philosophy.” Helen Lorimer doubtless moved and lived in an ideal world for herself, but she was practical, and this aspect of her nature gave her that humanness that made her delightful to those immensely removed from her intellectual eminence.And she had the saving grace of humour, wholesome, natural, indisputable fun. She denounced indeed the widespread flippancy of the day which represented the levity of a world which had seriously lost its faith, and restlessly sought a sensuous satisfaction, drinking, to the death of all ennobling feeling, the ready made potations cleverly concocted of mere pleasure; but she knew, none better, that the world was wonderfully ingenious, wonderfully smart, apt, and in the works of construction and convenience wonderfully helpful. Only she felt that the world revelled in too much delight, she felt that it would forget any outward and ulterior destiny in its passionate absorption in its own triumphs, its incessant excitements. Death and old age and infirmity had had sobering and realizing shocks to apply to carelessness in former times, but at the beginning of the twenty-first century, science had marvellously dissipated even these, in conjunction, let it be admitted, with a kind of psychic medicament whereby thought helped to banish worry and ailments.

Miss Lorimer recognized that the world moved, but she would have substituted other motives. She often said that the world must have a motor power and it would always be either pleasure or religion, as the thirst for knowledge actuated too small a numerical number to become a virtual force in its economies or plans, and of these motors she preferred religion. To the poor it had always been the great consolation.And rich as she was, Helen Lorimer felt that the arbitrament of life’s worth rested at last with those who most sorely tasted its disappointments and yet, with so developed a sympathy with suffering, she clung tenaciously to the hierarchical conception of society’s classification, reverting with a kind of mediaeval intensity to past social states.

About this woman Romance spun its silken and iridescent webs. Youth bowed to her smiles and lost its heart in her favoring glances. Perhaps her cloud of chevaliers was made up of a tested selection of admirers, or those confident and brave enough to match their acquirements with one so loftily endowed, but if so, it was not because of any pedantry in her. A delightful geniality made her always companionable, always winning, and put at ease any <i>soi-disant</i> swain, who indeed might have been thereby captivated with his own eligibility. The blood of Julieta Espozito was in her veins, and nature disdained to release her from the impulses of all her sons and daughters. A DINNER IN CARRARA

The <i>entresol</i> of the Lorimer mansion was occupied by the dining hall, a room of magnificent proportions and superbly conceived in decoration, the elegance of its detail being dominated by the size and breadth of a few features.

One wall was breached by a wide entrance, from which descended a series of steps to a lower hall; opposite this entrance the high wall contained an admirable reproduction of the painting of Alma Tadema of the Hunt of Diana, between two high windows, issuing upon balconies overlooking the Hudson; either end of the room held two enormous mirrors framed in Japanese bronze designed in flowers, fruits, and trailing creatures of the field; under these astonishing mirrors were two fire places equipped in monumental brass; on the sides of the entrance the walls were partially concealed by two large Madonnas, one by Perugino, the other by Tintoretto, and under these pictures two sideboards in carved teak wood held a rich assortment of blue India ware grouped around a few high silver vases, whose surfaces in repoussé reviewed the spoils of the hunter; the corners of the room received four large groups of statuary, which separately symbolized the seasons; the ceiling was a massy plane of gold unrelieved by any designs, and from it depended three chandeliers of cut glass in blue aqua-marine like Brazilian stones; the walls were treated with a pale veronica blue, the chairs were of twisted Honduras bay wood, and yellow silken curtains masked the light that poured in at the casements, whose outlook faced the Palisades, and caught the purple banks of the New Jersey hills.The floor of this apartment was in Pompeian mosaic, and at its centre rose the singular dining table, which was literally nothing else than a huge block of stucco of a pale terra-cotta ivory color, cut out underneath into arches, with a frieze at the edges of the table top representing oak leaves and acorns. This rigid and somewhat unaccommodating erection had a very beautiful effect indeed, and added an entablature to the room that made it more stately and impressive. Heavy rugs invested the floor, and banished its stony coldness, which effort on their part was successfully reinforced by the flickering and homelike light of the candles in the quaint majolica candelabra over the mantels at both ends of the room. It was that liquid season of the year as the spring retires from the presence of summer, leaving at the latter’s feet the garlands of her flowers, sweeping away the last fragrant curtains of April’s showers, and inviting the auroras of the sun to rise higher in the skies, while, as she fades away on the white blossoms of May, she already touches the petals of a thousand waking roses, and summons the tides of life, upon whose floods June approaches, to renew the world. It was indeed a little before the opening of June 2000, that, as the shades of night fell, or rather as its shadows grew accentuated beneath the orbed splendor of the moon, a group of six persons were seated around the dining table of the Lorimers, whose physiognomies and demeanors were sufficiently contrasted, though harmonized by a common culture and deportment. The table—the stucco block we have mentioned—was itself a picture of bounty and refined effects.A large golden <i>epergne</i> shaped as a cornucopia borne in the arms of tritons on the back of a silver wave, stood at its centre, holding a tumultuous wealth of roses, the table itself was covered with a delicate sheeny diaper of silver threads, a <i>chef d’oeuvre</i> of eastern workmanship, found by Robert Lorimer in a bazaar on the east side of New York a tantalizing and wonderful mixture of linen and metallic filaments, teazed into a low relief, on which figured peacocks and weeping willows by sweeping streams; and the service was—at the moment when we are called upon to observe it—made up of faintly golden chased Bavarian glass, with old Sevres china.

We have become cosmic, and in our land the final clash of the conflict of opinions approaches, unless indeed this fagged out earth is doomed to an eternal struggle with the battle grounds changed from continent to continent. Science and Religion offer their clues to the central mysteries. The measure of their values is determined <i>here</i>. I think Miss Lorimer’s plea should be respected. Let the Pope—the Head of the Church—come to close quarters with his antagonist, the Apollyon of material Lust, and of intellectual denial.”

“Thank you, Cardinal,” it was again the voice of the first speaker, “I almost thought that my audacious fancy, bold as it was, had been covered up, and thereby just a little abashed, by reflections that were intended to keep us from considering it at all. Every one was jumping the traces, and running a field. Yes! shall we bring the Pope to America?”

“Quevstion! quevstion!” murmured the Count, like the impatient voters at a primary, while the cardinal with an exorbitant curiosity painting itself upon his expressive countenance, looked around the table, the goal of his scrutiny evidently being the Professor, and the unrebuked silence continued, until, as if answering the voiceless implication that it was he who should break it,the Professor said; “Yes! the challenge has been often given; Let it be accepted, where the swords of both duellists are near enough to each other to not only strike fire, but to give the <i>coup de grace</i>.”

This truculence was perhaps a little harsh for the occasion, and Professor Dale felt its awkwardness; he turned blushing to his neighbor; “Pardon, Miss Lorimer, but the Cardinal is an uncompromising foe, and I suppose,” leaning to the Cardinal, “the Church would prefer death to sufferance?”

“Hardly,” answered the prelate, “the Church has often prospered upon little more than toleration, but on the other hand if it came to Science, asking for sufferance, it would be told to close its doors.”

“But vy! vy!” and the Count’s voice rose with a compromising seriousness, “dere is room for every ting, dis and dis, and dat and dat, and so ve grow a beeg, beeg, rug, vith a tousand colors and tousand threads, and on it the centuries come and go, and eet nefer, nefer wear out,” the ardent Hungarian had an inspired look, and his wife who apparently compensated him for her snubs, by an unimpaired adulation whenever he opened his mouth, became visibly grave, and dispensed a glance around the table, as if in admonition of his captivating simile.

“Well! Speaking of rugs,” said the Cardinal dryly “they are associated in my mind with Turks, and Turks are not the most desirable citizens; they certainly are not members of the Church, and a national rug of a thousand threads and colors is only another metaphor for chaos.”“You mean Cardinal,” said the Professor, “that the Count’s promiscuous carpet, would never harmonize in color or design with the tapestries of the Church?”

“Perhaps,” gaily rejoined Miss Lorimer, “I can twist the Count’s rug into an image that suits the Cardinal and myself better. It shall have a thousand colors and a thousand threads—ten thousand for that matter—but they will not make a miscellaneous and inconsequent woof; they will form a beautiful and gracious figure,” and the lady smiled as if there then floated before her mind’s eye something that fled from the profanation of a name.

The company rose with probably a different conclusion as to the value and meaning of Miss Lorimer’s disclosure, resting in the mind of each. But it dwelt with a singular poignancy of interest with two, and one of these, at least, lavished upon it a wealth of improving comment.

Scarcely had the group with gliding step reached the entrance of the grand chamber, and the attendant had drawn aside the curtains which revealed as they parted the noble stairway descending to a lower plane, constituted by a wide hall now enveloped in a golden glow, that descended from an electric corona, almost two stories above it; from which again suites of rooms extended to the right and to the left,—scarcely, had this moment arrived, when a resounding rap smote the front door of the hall, and, as a servant darted towards this entrance, the door itself was pushed from the outside, and the tall figure of a handsome elderly man entered. The face of the stranger was disturbed, and he flung down his walking stick with a gesture of despair.The next moment he raised his eyes to the arrested guests, and repressing his impatience moved to the foot of the stairway and spoke;

“Ah! my friends you must pardon my absence. It was not intentional. I see too well what pleasures I have missed. Of course Helen explained to you that I was called away down town to attend a meeting of directors of the bank, who are negotiating with the city for the latter’s accommodation of a loan. The meeting was interrupted by an alarm, that proved only too true, that the socialists had started a tremendous riot on the East side, and that fire, incendiary in origin, and attributed to them, had broken out in several quarters. Your lecture hall, Helen, where you have attempted your propaganda of the Divine Mission of Wealth, is burned down, and those scoundrels of The Beehive have doubtless had a hand in it. It was a compliment gratuitously offered to yourself, but I think it has in it the darker meaning of a menace. Come to the porch and see the signs of the Future.”

It was Theodore Lorimer, Helen’s uncle, who spoke, and the company, quickly assenting to this invitation hastened down the steps, and moved out upon the front terrace, lit by the electric globes upon their pillars. From this point an actual fire could not be descried, but the skies south and east beyond the intervening ridges were plainly illuminated, the light rising and falling, and at its highest intensity, as it scored the low lying banks of clouds with reflected flashes, it revealed the summits of high buildings that momentarily spanned the firelight beyond them with black columns.Theodore Lorimer led the company to the railing of the enclosure, and it so happened that the Professor found himself standing beside Miss Lorimer, between two of the marble pillars. Her face was discernible and it bore the marks of a kind of subdued anguish. She looked at him and pointed to the horizon growing more and more lurid; “Are these lights indeed signs of the Future as my uncle has said?”

Something intensely tender burned in the Professor’s eyes as he answered softly; “Miss Lorimer I do not know—but when the Pope comes we shall see whether they go out or whether they burn higher.”

“Ah!” answered Miss Lorimer, “You mock us,” and she came closer to him with her hands clasped.

“Mockery!” he rejoined, “Mockery! Surely mockery ill suits one who may become a supplicant, perhaps a penitant,” and the professor’s voice had a rueful accent, and his face looked at hers with ever so slight a glance of passion, from which she turned away with the faintest possible sigh of disappointment.<b>CHAPTER IV</b>

THE SUICIDARIUM

Tom Ratler had been almost a week at the Kohinoor, and as yet no intimation had come to him that he was wanted in the vast gregarious city, that grew to him more and more monstrous, and rapidly put on the form and functions of a maelstrom; a maelstrom where life, property, reputation, were engulfed, again momentarily to reappear, as if the regurgitation of the whirlpool shot its human victims back to the surface, to enjoy a new glimpse of respectability before they finally were sucked under, forever carried away in underground sluices to the merciful and impersonal ocean of oblivion.

Or else at other instants the city seemed to him a kaleidoscope with its human occupants, its myriad fancies of design and architecture, its dissipations, its gluttonies, even its piety and all the agencies of its philanthropic life thrown round in a spinning circle, confusedly glimmering and changing color, from which too arose an accompanying roar of voices, not unfrequently punctured with cries of pain, not always without passages of music.

Tom had made friends cautiously, from a sense of danger, and with some agitated memories of what he had read in books. At first the number of social advances to him were suspiciously numerous, and he somewhat indiscriminately classed them as a concerted conspiracy upon his pocket book.That indispensable support had already considerably shrunken, vanquished into a sort of querulous obedience to the gestures of necessity in the glittering Kohinoor and to the no less unavoidable gestures of its dazzling occupants. He later recognized that his own personality, with a good face and the evidences of a trained and equipped body, had much to do with these social advances. Among the friends or acquaintances whom he, by a companionable sense of mere liking, had chosen as more exactly intimates was a young man, who confided to him at a moment of especial expansion, that he was connected, in a business way, with the most extraordinary of the many features of the multivarious city. It was nothing less than a <i>suicidarium</i> of which Tom had heard something from the Mayor’s secretary, and which he knew was one of the forbidden extravagances in New York. The information given to him by his young friend, was to the effect, that enough people who wished to die for diverse reasons, connected with all sorts of experiences could be found in the City to assure the maintenance of this grewsome institution. The young man was himself engaged in the work of making a peculiar heavy and lethal gas or vapor, which was used in accomplishing the appalling end. Tom naturally recoiled from the horrible idea of ending life, and could hardly believe the story of his friend that these outfits variously designed, and of varying degrees of elegance and size, had proven to be profitable enterprises, because of the exorbitant prices paid for so abhorrent and hideous a purpose. It required considerable persuasion on the man’s part to bring Tom to an acceptance of an invitation to visit the scene of this unnatural conduct.He went with some misgivings, but he went at last actually fascinated by the incredible weirdness of thought, so unspeakable and strange. His friend—Edward Links, or Ned—took him by the subway far up to the end of the Island of Manhattan, at the old Kingsbridge, where the place, disguised as a shabby tenement on the outside, was situated. And as Tom approached it, somewhat separated from the other houses around it, and on the edge of the Spuyten Duyvil creek—offering, as Ned explained, a convenient way for the conveyance of the dead to their sepulture, or to the cremator—on the opposite bank—it unmistakably presented the familiar aspects of a dilapidated and almost abandoned tenement. It was a little too long, and some of its dilapidation looked made up for the occasion; displaced clapboards, perforations and disfiguring splashes of mud applied to its sides, the forcibly wrenched doors, the broken windows, broken beyond any possible reconcilement with their human uses, all gave it a rather contrived appearance. This might not have been obtruded upon the casual passer-by, though men and women were moving in and out of it, but it was detected by Tom quite quickly, and he asked his friend if people couldn’t see it was a “blind,” especially the trained and unsympathetic optics of the police.

“Well yes and no! You see it’s this way. There is a good deal of swagger in the business, and joining the majority this way means a clean spill of a thousand, or anyhow five hundred spielers. Now you wouldn’t believe it, but the applications are thicker than they can accommodate, sometimes. It all depends. Epidemics, failures, panics, love-matters, all work in spells, and when they happen to come together why they just run over each other to get the quietus they talk about in the playhouse. Not here. Oh no. The game is on the Q.T., and the proposals and assignments come in at the office down town. Well as I was saying it pays, and it pays the police to have a kind of blind eye for the hock shop; that’s the name for the outfit here, though it goes among the squad as The Oblivion, a pretty name, but nothing to the point.

“I don’t so much care for the job, but it pays good, and I don’t have to see things, but just switch on gas and wait. It’s risky. Every mother’s son of us would be pinched and run to jail, if they nabbed us. What helps the whole affair is a kind of talk that goes on now in certain papers and journals about the emptiness of life, and a funny fancy that they all come back and get better places. It isn’t a bit bad how they do it. You’ll see, and I guess that some kinds of men and women, who have just about squeezed all they know how to get out of life, get a sort of faint heartedness and sickness, and kinder fetch up here with a jerk, bunch it together, and let ’er go. Horrible ain’t it? But it’s slick, slicker than a wet rat. You just see. It’s a kind of a dream.”

They came to the piazza that extended along the front of the slovenly domicile, its floor rent and gaping, and some of the pillars supporting its roof weakened by decay, while the windows and blinds broken, torn, and dismantled completed the rather overwrought imitation of poverty and disorder. As Ned Links approached the place, his companion noticed his nervous inspection of the neighborhood, and when with a whistle he turned to the tenement, and stepped within one of its open hallways, Tom saw to his astonishment a slight unevenness in its floor move upward, exposing a trap door, and beneath it descending steps.Tom felt indeed he had embarked upon an adventure appropriately theatrical and strange, enough so to satisfy his romantic fancies, and even the mingled odors reaching his nostrils from the aperture, in which a kind of fragrance was not unnoticed, added a peculiar zest of danger.

Ned caught the door of the opening and held it up as he motioned to Tom to descend. Tom, with no misgivings of danger, but with a slightly sickened heart obeyed, and observed a female figure rather carelessly dressed, or less equivocally, rather immodestly undressed, going before him. It was her hands that had lifted the door for their entrance. The female did not turn her head but she held above it a small glass lamp, which vouchsafed sufficient light to enable her visitors to follow, and it was not too dim for Tom’s observation, that the woman before him was comely and unabashed. Her occasional backward glances were lighted by very becoming smiles, and Tom, under their ravishment, failed to notice that they were treading a very narrow and damp passage, and that it was leading downward upon a rather steep grade. The opportunity for such observations was suddenly checked by their reaching the end of the defile, and stopping abruptly at a door. The door opened, and Tom, with Ned upon his heels, and with some unmannerly pressure upon the giggling maiden, whose dishevelment became more and more pronounced, shot into a room or an enclosure before whose strange features at first dimly descried, but in an instant later revealed in a dazzlement of light, as the bacchante touched a button which sent an electric thrill into thousands of little lamps, Tom fairly gasped in astonishment.He seemed to have walked into a bower of shrubs, flowers, and low trees, under whose protecting shade couches were carelessly distributed, while everywhere a rosy light cast its softening illumination, enchanting the eye with tinted fantasies as where he saw a stream of water lisping among rocks, just perceptibly stained with the pink sheen, or where a marble grotto, opening at the end of a gravel walk, bordered with tulips, caught on its flaring white lips the same delicate blush. Ned and the girl withdrew a little, while they watched the bewildered stranger, turning with new wonder to the points of this sunken garden, and Ned was not loth to intimate his irrepressible satisfaction by facetious antics behind Tom’s back, some of which could only be described as evidences of his personal infatuation for his companion.

But Tom was not long intimidated by his strange surroundings, and had already begun to walk forward over a yellow gravelled path that wound through a sward magically gleaming with a light that appeared to radiate from its innumerable blades of grass. He had hardly gone four steps forward, when a blast of air struck him, and sent his hat whirling off among the branches of a flowering almond tree, while his discomfiture was hailed with hearty approval by the shouting Ned and the indulgent and favoring maiden. Tom received another surprise. He sprang to the flowering almond shrub that looked gracefully real, and on whose crowded flowers the rosy light fell, imparting a fairy glamour to its petals. His hat was caught in the slender limbs, and Tom raised his hand to recover it, clasping the tree trunk for that purpose.The tree trunk under the pressure of his body swayed forward and broke, while Tom rolled over in the confusion of boughs, and the tumbling counterfeit entrapped him in wire, strips of painted linen, paper corollas, wax leaves, in short the paraphernalia of a clever imitation, put up consummately, and only one part of the wondrous mimicry about him. This last incident was greeted with more boisterous shouts by Ned and the young woman, who not loth to increase the stranger’s dismay pelted him with theatrical pebbles picked up from the banks of the theatrical rivulet where indeed the sibilant water belonged to nature, but where everything else was an artful disguise of paper, plaster, and pigment.

Tom extricated himself and in great good humour rejoined his friends. He realized now that he had entered a subterranean theatre which as he gazed around him assumed the proportions of a beautiful picture. Its deceptive nature disappeared in the sickly radiance, while the artifice of invention had been exhausted in completing a fascinating illusion. And the picture had been skillfully composed. It represented a blending of features whose mixture, unified under the strange purpose of arresting life, lulled the spirit, through the senses, into a dreamy languor. By means of the hushed and crowded visions thus summoned or inwardly created, the surrender of the will was gently accomplished. The opiate of a luxurious feeling, swayed ever so discretely into an anticipation of delights just dawning in the Life to Come, dwelt with increasing pressure on the swooning and dying clients of this strange hostelry. For the means and the methods were ruthlessly explained to Tom by his new friend, and Tom responding intuitively to the more poetic suggestions around him, almost resented Ned’s commonplace coarseness, and rude matter-of-fact indifference.To Ned the Suicidarium was a business episode, and the awful ghastliness of its work, a detail of commercial manipulation, so hardened had he become to its tragic consequences. To Tom’s less sodden nature, while he shrank with healthy repulsion from its meaning, yet as the scene grew into a congruous whole—artificial as it was—the mystery of its work somehow reflected a wonderfulness upon it all, as the exhalation of a perfume sometimes makes a poisoned air salubrious.

The deep pit lay in the dolomitic limestone of the north end of Manhattan Island, at a point not far removed from the splendid encrustation of Carrara upon the Inwood Ridge. It had been deeply dug, and its lateral extensions stretched far beyond the limits of the sordid tenement that, like a rag upon a jewelled shawl, covered its entrance from the upper streets. It combined the features of a Japanese garden and a Grecian theatre, and it was enclosed by the rising walls of stone, up which its numerous lamps sent streaks and fluttering bands of light, lost at last somewhere above in a canopying sky, wherein the implanted and clustering spots of light simulated a waiting Heaven. The Japanese garden, with bridges, dwarfed cedars, clambering wistarias, painted hedges, yellow walks, and white buddhas perched upon iron trellises, was carefully developed. Stone seats under overhanging peach and plum trees were draped with a green gauze that invited the rueful guests to await there their sepulchral summons, closing their eyes delightedly upon a picture of shimmering loveliness.
