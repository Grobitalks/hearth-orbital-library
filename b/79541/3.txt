“Father” said Miss Lorimer “it has been <i>my</i> thought and I am proud of it. There is a spot that can be made into a beautiful home for the Pope. It is the summit of Staten Island. “Yes it can be made supremely fit. The world’s clamor cannot come there and yet the world’s voice can come. It can all be secured, for there are few houses on it as yet, it being an old estate or reservation.It has been called <i>Todt</i> Hill meaning the Dead Hill, from some far off legend of their having been a massacre there of the early Dutch, by the first Indians. We shall make it the Hill of Life. And my idea would be to keep it very simple and unostentatious, full of trees with the wide glimpses below, and a simple serviceable home, some jewel of a church on the highest point, and the home of the Pope, also very simple. I would drop all the abundant pomp of Rome. That would be pernicious here. The utmost severity of living, a kind of homely husbandry. Nothing to tempt the criticism of our democracy. Just the furnishment of the Pope’s court and that bare as it can be made. What else is necessary? Nothing I think. Let the sublimity be moral rather than physical; a few well selected treasures, the impress of culture but all the larger corporeal grandeur cut off.”

The Cardinal smiled approvingly, and then Amato spoke. “Why Father should you not go. I would be a poor embassador to Europe. I have no authority. It is true I brought these letters, and I have seen the fathers of the Church, and Father, perhaps, in that greater matter that concerned me—well—I think that I must pause a while.”

The Cardinal started, and a shadow of wonder passed over his face, the young man reddened to his fine white temples, and Helen Lorimer felt the slight shock of some misunderstanding between the two men; somehow she felt that she was its cause.“I am sorry my son,” said the Cardinal, “we can talk of that another time. I do not think that I could do as well as you in showing just what is expected here, and what are the chances here, and for my own part—well—there are several reasons that would make the mission, in my hands, impertinent.” He looked earnestly at Amato, and his serious countenance took on the expression of pained abstraction, almost as if he had forgotten his visitors and his own intentions, while he rested those scrutinizing eyes of his upon the young man with an increasing severity.

Miss Lorimer felt the unseen obstruction, and rose.

“One moment,” began the Cardinal who also stood up, “one moment Miss Lorimer. Just a few words will show you how far we have got along in this difficult scheme. Nothing very definite has as yet been effected abroad, but I am sending out communications to all the bishops, over the names of a council of American bishops who you know have discussed this subject. This council has told you of its gratitude for your proffered help. It has been fairly unanimous in considering the many aspects of the plan—and I had hoped that Amato would assist us in reaching, in a personal way, certain influential members of the papal household, before we came more out in the open——as it were—in placing the whole subject upon a public basis of discussion. But—” here a glance of mute reproach at the beautiful youth, who with crimsoned cheeks, and an evidently rising temper, stood near Miss Lorimer, “perhaps something more will develop shortly, and it will be my pleasure to let you know everything. I agree with your views exactly as to the delicacy of the innovation. Nothing must jar or irritate the spirit of our countrymen.It involves for the Church a new procedure, a new vista, a new apprehension of its tasks. It is pregnant with the strangest possibilities of good. It is likely to land us too in new vicissitudes. It is a challenge to the age in one sense, and again IT IS RETREAT. Perhaps a clerical habit of thought makes me dull, but I foresee a new warfare, and the Church Militant deserving its name in a fiercer struggle. For the Pope to leave Rome and confound the traditions of over two thousand years, to reestablish his theological supremacy in this newer world, means a violation of prestige whose consequences we can scarcely measure.”

Miss Lorimer turned to the young and yet silent man at her side, and invited him to visit her, and handed him her card, and then to the Cardinal, yet watching with an alert suspicion, she gave her hand, bade him farewell, and protesting that they had not seen him lately left the room, and, escorted by an attendant, the residence.

The two men were facing each other in the room that, to the younger, had suddenly grown distasteful, after the glorious woman that had been near him there, had left it.

“Rudolph,” it was the Cardinal speaking, “you have been entranced, perverted by beauty. You renounce a mission you were anxious to undertake. But more grievously you will deny the Church your consecrated service. Is it so?”

“Father” answered the Amato, but without a tremor of hesitation, “I do. I never will be a priest. You say I am perverted by beauty.It is no perversion. I love that woman who left us. I care nothing for promise, advowson, nor expectation. I have not been sure that I was right to engage myself so closely in your plans. <i>I do love life.</i> My visit in Europe stirred the fires of my emotion. It suddenly painted the skies and put flowers on the earth. The hidden, separated, cloistered, existence grew hideous to my eyes—impossible. I am not traitorous to my ideals, to my service to the Church. No, no, they remain, but all the beckoning truthful loveliness of life overwhelms me now. It seems embodied in that superb woman, who has just left us. Call it what you will, revile me, despise me, but I break with the past. It has now no meaning; it was a mistake, a mad design made thoughtlessly, in obedience to one strong feeling, which now turns to weakness before a stronger—Oh—an irresistible compelling impulse.”

There was good reason for the Cardinal to trust his ears, though he was angry enough to be compelled to; to his eyes the Passion in Rudolph Amato’s face was palpable enough. The Mayor would have felt some distrust of his fancied security in Helen Lorimer’s opening affections—and perhaps Helen Lorimer herself—susceptible to beauty, carrying in her breast the yet but half awakened power of love, would have bowed before a storm that came so suddenly, a storm like some onrushing freshet of the spring, bearing all before it in its tumultuous rending strength.The Cardinal gazed at the young man with interest. He was breathing heavily, his arched lips parted, his eyes became intensely dark and brilliant, and, through some impatient movement of his head, his raven black hair, hanging almost artfully, shading the pale brow and brushing the burning cheeks; and the Cardinal’s interest turned to admiration.

Cardinal Fenton was a connoisseur, and he responded intuitively to every glimpse and form of beauty; he was indeed a <i>bon vivant</i>, and a man of subtlety, faculties conjoined in him with intellectualism, administrative resources, and an almost inerrant judgment, within which too ran the current of an optimistic policy. But he knew Cleopatra’s maxim, “wishers were ever fools,” and, one expedient dashed, it was not lamented, but itself trodden upon by a quick successor.

He understood Rudolph Amato, and he knew that instant that he was lost as a priest to the Holy Roman Catholic Church, but his flashing instinct told him he was yet useful, a tool rejected in one service can be brought to an infinite test of usefulness in another.

And so this bundle of unfixed desires must not be too loosely pardoned, too carelessly freed from his own sense of obligation. He stood yet in the place of service, of friendship, of fealty—much value remained. The quickly coining Cardinal knew that, and just now when his own ambition—for Cardinal Fenton was ambitious—had launched a tremendous undertaking, an instrument so skillful—he knew Amato’s talent—must be kept husbanded, not rejected, because wandering from its first design.The Cardinal kept his appreciation of Amato’s beauty to himself, and when he answered, “wait,” he motioned the young man to his seat, sat down himself, rang a bell, and told the servant, who attended, to bring them wine and cordials and cigars—the latter for Amato—the Cardinal never smoked, and, though he relished the qualities of good wines, he was abstemious: he yielded to a perverse appetite for cordials. When these were brought, and the two had regained a composure mutually lost for an instant, the Cardinal without at once raising his eyes from the liqueur glass before him—a delicate affair of cut glass and gold—spoke;

“Rudolph Amato you must remember some things. Your dying Mother entrusted you to me when her husband deserted her—and, when your Father was brought home dead, killed in some brawl in Constantinople, I secured for you his wealth, guarded it, increased it. You must realize that your education—certainly better than is usually allotted to young plutocrats—since you were protected, by me against the indulgences your fortune made possible—was under my charge;” there was a motion of expostulation from Rudolph—“Yes; I remember too some things—I recall them with sorrow now. I did indeed hope you would enter holy orders; I recognized your poetic power of language, your unadulterated idealistic fancies, your spiritual grace; I knew the needs of the Church; I hoped—alas; how vainly—that you would become a priest, and I was not placing too great a sanction in my faith in your powers and virtue to promise these eyes the pleasure of seeing you exalted to the bishopric, and hearing your voice raised in aid, in the desperate approaching crises of the Church. Nothing else I can recall, ever disappointed these expectations until this moment.You were obedient, grateful, indefatigable and devout, apparently contented with this prescription, even accepting it with enthusiasm. Are you quite sure you do not owe me a greater effort at self-control than you have shown; is it fair to me; is it fair to the Church?”

Again there was a short silence. Amato—so quickly swayed by any sympathetic appeal—had walked around the table and was kneeling before the Cardinal; his upturned face told his contrition—if it were contrition—or at the worst his honest regret for the Cardinal’s disappointment; but it told nothing of a changed mind. The Cardinal placed his hand on the handsome youth—he really loved him as a son—and the severity melted from his face, though he yet withheld his pardoning smile, reserving a little the new purposes he intended Amato to undertake.

Amato regained his feet apparently satisfied with the relenting gesture of the Cardinal. He spoke less impetuously, but the Cardinal noted—and he was a keen listener—only a deeper settled determination to banish the past, uproot its incentives, and its aims, to—forget it, to

_Wipe away all trivial fond records,
All saws of books, all forms, all pressures past,

Here were two great talents, two great fortunes; they should be forged into a great implement of protection against the adversities of the Church.

“There could be no greater injustice to you Sir, nor to the Church, if I covered myself with the confusion of a bitter renunciation, which I rebelled against. I should have made myself into a distracted fool, perhaps worse—a fool and an apostate;

“What lay undiscovered has been, as with a lightning’s flash, revealed. This moment has been providential. If, as you once taught me, God stirs the wind, that stirs the leaf, that drops its tiny pellet of dew upon the thirsting flower, at the foot of the tree, that becomes the adornment of the altar whereon the Sacrament shall lie, then I feel that I have been saved by the same interposition from a blunder, and you from a disgrace.

“If service is wanted I am still yours to use, but a fire now bums so brightly in my soul, that all the waters of abnegation could not put it out.”

The Cardinal was now looking very earnestly at the speaker, who returned his gaze unabashed, and strangely enough there dawned on both faces a smile.On the elder man it appeared as an imperceptible admission of defeat, on the younger as the harbinger of happiness. Perhaps in neither case was it justified, for the issues for either of them were yet undecided.

“Well,” said the Cardinal, “I must still remain your confessor. I confess if I must withdraw those hopes, that you, to this day, have consistently strengthened, the unions of your name with that of Helen Lorimer would also give me pleasure, and—let us believe it is God’s better way;” he paused, turned again in his hand the fragile glass, and then continued, “Miss Lorimer is a beautiful woman indeed; she is a remarkable one too, and much admired, and her hand has been sought by distinguished suitors—some of them those hungry bankrupts from Europe. I know she has been dismayed and angry with them; she is a year or two older than you my son—but—Ah, well, she is indeed very beautiful, and—perhaps—you may succeed—but, if you fail; how then, how then; have you thought of that ambitious boy?”

“I shall not fail,” was the unexpected answer, an answer that made the Cardinal turn quickly. His glance assured him that the ardent youth felt no misgivings, and as he saw him there, knowing too the trained and gentle power of the boy, his intensity of tastes, and his winning spirit, raised now by this manly daring into something knightly, the Cardinal silently accorded him his acquiescence.

A few minutes later Rudolph Amato passed down the steps of the episcopal residence—then as now upon Madison Avenue—and walked to Fifth Avenue, and again he went on into the famous thoroughfare, which still resisted,above that point, the ingression of the sky climbing stores. Had the Cardinal been able to have followed his protégée he would have felt some astonishment, for this <i>ci-devant</i> postulant for the priesthood, walked briskly, with no little assertive grace of motion, to Forty-Second Street, and, stopping for an instant to watch the boiling tumult of that Four-corners, turned west, and a second after entered the massive entrance of the glorious successor of the Fleischman Baths, which, expanded far beyond the corners of Sixth Avenue, and no longer restricted to the attic of a building, filled a magnificent structure opposite Bryant Park, itself changed into a brilliant bed of flowering plants.

The place was famous. It was called The Agonistarch, and roofed every sort of athletic diversion and comfort. Huge swimming tanks, mimic race tracks, boxing tiers, gymnasia, offices of rest and relaxation, whimsical levities, like curious tilting stairs that went up and down jolting into dizziness their walkers, queer rippling falls of carpet upon which the shouting players rolled and tumbled, whirling tables that shot the tranced occupants off into surrounding piles of cushions, pulsating rugs that threw their human loads up into the air, and strange downy sheaves that passed a man over and under, twisting him into a curling line, health lifts, and precipices of painted wood, from which the players jumped into a sea of satiny leaves, a vast medley of conceits, where the youth of the city resorted for repair and exercise.It was crowned by a luxurious garden with swings and captive balloons hedged in with coppiced fig trees, with sprinkling fountains, perfumed winds, a theatre, big aquaria, with cages of animals, singing birds, with mossy banks, glistening with tiny lizards, little canals of water on which floated huge nut shells, their capsules opening and admitting couples into the silken lined cups within them, with clay pipes or funnels playing in great bowls of glycerized soapsuds and blowing up iridescent bubbles, that came off in dancing streams rising and falling and exploding, and in the midst of this garden rose a pair of the steel antennae which emitted that wonderful mysterious light which at night bathed the air above the city with a wizard sheen. At night too the garden itself thrilled with a delicately tinted illumination brought from a host of lamps.

Adjoining the Agonistarch was a hotel called the Anchorite, where Rudolph Amato had his chambers. This hotel connected on every story with Agonistarch, and its name was a palpable cynicism. But Amato’s residence there need not be misconstrued. The Cardinal had brought up his charge with the keenest sense of the enormous value of physical perfection. It was a pet theory of the Cardinal’s, that the demand of the flesh had two issues, one in lust, the other in exercise; that these excluded each other. He acted on that principle with Amato, and in him he had virtually trained a splendid athlete.When Amato returned from his visit to Europe—sent there by the Cardinal for the purposes hinted at in their interview—Amato had reached his conclusion to slowly extricate himself from the implied obligation to his spiritual guardian to enter the Church. He had begun by assuming the control of his own fortune which, now as he was of age, came under his own guidance, though he had allied with himself in its direction, a business man, a lawyer of the city, and a friend of Cardinal Fenton’s. This opening to a more independent existence successfully accomplished, Amato began to consider the serious problem—yet unsolved—of his life, for with him the mere position of wealth could not abrogate the value and meaning of occupation. His well balanced nature forbade it, and the consciousness of availability in himself for some good ends forbade it even more imperiously. The incidents attending these silently working changes had kept him from the Cardinal, and that perspicacious observer had not failed to note his absence, together with the greater freedom of his charge in choosing his own way. It was indeed in response to a remonstrance, that he had been at the Cardinal’s on that day, when—as he felt—the meeting with Miss Lorimer suddenly illumined his future with purpose and decision.

He had been attracted to the conveniences of the Anchorite from its nearness to the excellent gymnasia of the Agonistarch and perhaps too in an awakened desire of dalliance with the world. Its brilliancy and the raging life around it, inwoven with the swirling activities of commerce on all sides of it attracted him. His visit to Europe had administered many shocks to his opening nature, and the reaction from study and seclusion, playing in a system appropriated by its psychology and make-up to the uses of a social life, gave him momentarily a sense of conflict.He knew but few persons in the city; the Cardinal had been his confidant and friend, filling almost entirely the relations of a father to him, and so successfully had the Cardinal kept him from the usual intercourse of life, that the young man was practically a stranger in the city of his birth. He had been a fairly good student, excelling in language and in art, with no inconsiderable power of memory, showing a predilection for history, which he most appreciated on its poetic and artistic sides. Carefully guarded by the Cardinal in his religious feelings his fidelity and admiration for the Catholic Church were most sincere, and his observances of his religious duties, onerous and constant. The plan of making him a priest of the Church had been the Cardinal’s, with the delighted approval of his Mother, and he had himself felt a pleasant predisposition for this end.

And now returning to the customary anticipations of life, his youth and nature thirsting for their satisfaction he felt a sort of mental confusion. He had taken a rather sumptuous suite of rooms at the Anchorite, consisting of a larger sitting room, used as a library, and study, a smaller inner room devoted to his meals, which were individually served to him, and a third room—his bed-room. This group of chambers was pleasingly furnished, and, as he had chosen his position near the top of the building, their outlook commanded the city to the south. The comfort of it, was very real, and, bordering as it did upon luxuriousness, Amato realized its pleasure, but experienced a slight inner impatience and self rebuke for enjoying them.Of course he had been well observed by his neighbors in the establishment, and particularly admired, in his gymnastic exercises, for his physique and abundant skill, and his good looks. Many had made overtures to him in a friendly way, and while Amato received these advances with perfect courtesy, he seemed to repel familiarity, and he offered no encouragement to that servile and indecent <i>facetia</i> which marred the conversations around him; he was conscious of it and despised it. This attitude was not long in developing a natural anger among the habituées of the place, and Amato, feeling this, had about determined to go elsewhere, though the charm and perfection of the material side of this popular caravansary still held him. Among these fault-finding and petulant critics of Amato, was a particularly large and gross fellow who took occasion again and again to make himself obnoxious, and by a silent effrontery caused Amato’s resentment. A mean sneer disfigured the face of his unpleasant physiognomy when Amato appeared in the dressing rooms, or in the baths, or in the gymnasium, and it was too evident that he waited for some opportune moment to openly fall foul of the young man. Amato remarked to himself, with a little trepidation, that the man wanted to disable or injure him, and sought some occasion for a quarrel.

It was only a day before Amato’s interview with the Cardinal that this man’s—his name was Gynt—sullenness and envy, if it was envy, came to a conclusion of open violence. It was customary after the exercise in the gymnasium for the athletes and young executants to go to the splendid bathing room, and plunge in the broad and deep bath or pool, after enjoying perhaps a shower, in one of the many adjoining closets.At one end of this pool, and raised somewhat above its surface, was a jumping platform not accommodating easily more than three, to which the men in turns ascended, leaving it as quickly as they reached it, and so that a line of plunging figures followed each other in an almost continuous parade into the boiling waters of the tank.

The man Gynt was on this platform, and Amato came up behind him for his own turn, and the big occupant of the board noticing this, planted himself squarely on the end of the jump, and with evidently no intention of using the board for its usual purposes, enjoyed emitting a few stupid and vulgar railleries at his friends in the water.

Amato waited a reasonable time, and then saw that the moment of a collision had come. Moving up on the board he quietly asked the man to move. The giant turned contemptuously, grinned at Amato, and with unconcealed malice said “He guessed his young nibs could wait for his betters,” and winking at the men in the water—, many of whom saw the trouble brewing—, resumed his fixed and dogged attitude, with spread legs and folded arms.

Amato then requested him to make way for himself, if he was not going to leap, and pushed against him. In a flash, as if expecting and coveting this very attempt to pass him, the bully seized Amato, and before Amato could resist effectually hurled him off sideways in the tank with an oath. Amato fell fortunately upon his head in the water, and suffered no shock, but there was a half stilled cry of “shame,” as he rose and swam to the egress from the water.The ruffian still kept his place on the board, and was laughing, though not perhaps with the best confidence, the unfairness of the trick appealing to the settled code of honor among the men. He was looking at Amato with a strained attention, and his eyes were joined by those of every other man in the room, in the same scrutiny.

Amato was soon out of the water, scrambling up at one of the stairways, and he was angry all through. His rage mounted to his face, and he felt that this fierce rebellious temper surging now in him formed another suddenly new break with the placid and regulated past. He ran through the bathers around the tank, and came back to the steps leading up to the platform, where his assailant, now less mirthful from a sense of the general disapproval, and some sharp reminders of the prowess of Amato, stood grimly waiting the next move of the young man, and apparently expecting an assault, for he turned towards the front of the stage.

The next instant Amato had passed up the steps and confronted him. Gynt did not take advantage of his superior position to resist Amato’s return, but remained at the end of the platform. He wore his provoking sneer, and though evidently a little inclined to make a joke of it, his attitude suggested his readiness for a fray. His expectations were not to be disappointed. Amato sprang quickly in front of him, and in a low voice said; “Mr. Gynt you’re a loafer. Defend yourself.”The words had scarcely passed his lips before he countered and struck Gynt such a smart blow that the big fellow staggered and almost fell backward into the tank. He recovered, however, and then with his massive body plunged into the smaller but much quicker man in front of him. The stage was too narrow for successful evolutions of the sparring type. Amato saw this, and as Gynt rushed at him he cowered, bent, and steadying himself with all his might and main, caught Gynt’s body around the middle, stopping its impetus with a drive forward. He overpowered Gynt’s motion, and the next moment with his arms wrapped around the bulky form of the man, who had lost in part his balance, by this sudden arrest, and was sprawling over Amato’s back, he rose, and lifting the fighter off his feet, flung him helplessly writhing, and dazed with astonishment, on his back into the water.

He fell heavily and sank. The walls of the tank bristled with crowding forms as the men watched the foaming indentation on the surface. Gynt appeared and lamely swam to the stairway, evidently hurt. He was breathing heavily and in pain. Then Amato did something that, in the turn of a hand, won him the admiration of most of the men there. He jumped from the platform swam swiftly to the point where Gynt was being assisted, and coming behind him supported him up. Gynt was in a faint; he had fallen in the water too violently and suffered from an impacted side. As he came to the floor of the room he sank into the arms of his helpers and among these was Amato. Rudolph looked sorrowfully at the man, and then gazing around him said; “My friends, I believe you can bear me witness that this act of mine was not unprovoked. I am sincerely sorry that it has turned out so badly. But would any of you have done differently? I was incensed, and I forgot myself—but my resentment was surely not unnatural.I had been insulted and I had never done this man any wrong. Do you exonerate me?”

“Yes, yes, yes,” came loudly from many sides; “it was Gynt’s fault. He was peevish and ugly. You’re all right. He isn’t hurt much. Help him out of this, and get him some brandy,” were the various comments, and Amato taking almost bodily the limp shape assisted the men to carry their comrade into a dressing room, where restoratives brought him to his senses.

He came to slowly, and when fully conscious roamed the room weariedly with his eyes until they fell upon the watching Amato; his face contracted and an impulsive shudder shook him. He seemed to dread Amato, and turned his face aside. Amato leaned over him and said, “I am very sorry. Will you give me your hand?”; but the man remained silent, and then Amato slowly moved off and walked away.

The incident gave the habituées of the Agonistarch a very salutary impression of Amato’s great strength, and during the rest of his stay there, he was a momentary favorite. His exclusiveness and repression were forgiven, and many good words came to his ears, unsolicited, praising his magnanimity, while he learned that Gynt had already won some enmities among the weaker men by his overbearing and bullying style. He left the Anchorite a few days later, with a good reputation, and a moderate popularity.

Amato was indeed now an example of a discovered temperament.Brought up with a carefully inculcated confidence in the Church, with an assiduously perfected environment of religious considerations, with the cultured superintendence of affectionate and learned tutors, and imbued himself with the dawning powers of a Rossettian taste for the mysterious and moonish, he, at first, in his younger years put on all the aspects of a recondite and spiritual nature. These appearances really covered a strongly human and virile sense, in which all the appetencies of the man had grown into rounded and perfect size. Like a rolling away of a cloth concealing some hidden growth which at the first touch of the generous sun spawns again into a vivid flourish of flowers, Amato’s movement out upon the scene of the world’s powerful appeals, its pictures, and its wreathing invitations, had torn from his eyes the sapient tapestry of his artful schooling, and he leaped like an actor from the dark flies of the stage into the pageant of the play’s procession.

But he came upon that stage “with modest eyes and still conclusions,” though his heart panted with desire. The skill of his education kept him restrained, and the serviceable disposition bred in him, stiffened his senses with admonitory cautions. And thus by an almost heavenly opportunity—the form, face, and character, of Helen Lorimer came before him. That “psychological moment,” so much maligned and misnamed, was here a potent fact and deep reality. Limited by training, and habit, and ingrained teaching, from catching, as it were, the world in his arms—arms that ached to embrace and enjoy—there walked right into the focus of his raised eyes this beautiful and noble woman.That explained in a sort of way his sudden passion, his convinced determination. And here again there was a paradox. To a man, whose intercourse with women had always been on the decorous extremes of civility, strengthened by what had been assumed as a foregone abnegation, Amato’s fervor and unhesitating self-confidence seemed abnormal, if not grotesque. It was so. It arose exactly from his inexperience. His love flamed upward like a discharging igneous flood. It knew no barriers; it cared for none. Circumspection was ridiculous, apprehension impossible. The wind blew, his sail was fixed, he knew his port.

_So that when Hope’s deep source in fullest flow,
Like earthquake did uplift the stagnant ocean
Of human thoughts—his shook beneath the wide emotion_.

It would have amused the travelled courtier of the world, this unquestioning certainty; Amato’s mind fled even beyond the hour of possession, and he had begun to lay his plans for the mutual service he and Helen Lorimer might render the world. He exulted in these reveries. In them his love, his religious earnestness, his ambition, his feeling of accountability, blended delightfully. The young man walked in clouds, but he was not so far above the earth, the hard ground of day life, as to neglect beginning his suit. Contiguity was important.

He deserted the Anchorite, leaving it, yet unreconciled with his enemy Gynt, and established himself at the southern boundary of Carrara, in the Arrowhead Inn.To-day that half metamorphosed villa, turned to the uses of entertainment, under old oaks, and glimpsing the river at 168th Street, and the Lafayette Boulevard, down a creased hillside, is a promising eerie for dreams and good dinners. In the year 2000 it would still retain its summery charm, under yet greater shadowing oaks, but much changed, expanded into a red tiled mansion of three stories, with a bulging skirt of balconies at every story, and fitted for the seclusion of all those who required or cared for retirement, and divested of its present day publican features.

It was a delightful nook, and Amato found it most convenient and inviting. A pale of trees hid the crowning glory of New York—Carrara—to the north; there was not much to wish to see in the east, another wall of umbrage shut off the south, but westward through artificially prepared arches of boughs, the noble river, with its cargoes, cargasons, and excursions, fed the eyes with interest.

The night that Amato left him, the Cardinal passed into the cathedral, for his evening devotion. The late twilight of a bright day bathed the interior—walls, clerestory, pavements, and flaming windows—with a vanishing light; the shadows crept upward against the pale illumination, that shrank more and more into the higher recesses, or spun itself in ropes of mote deluged rays from the apertures in the roof. A few supplicants knelt here and there in the darkened pews, the vestal light burned before the high altar, while guttering tapers in front of the shrine of the Holy Mother, gave forth an unsteady radiance.The Cardinal advanced through the gates of the chancel rails, and, prostrating himself at the very foot of the sanctuary, stayed, thus bowed in a prayer, that was half meditation, half uttered petitions. His heart was indeed heavy. He had regarded Amato as a spiritual son and he had wished to embrace him as one with himself in the transcendental service of the altar, and now—this precious offering to God had turned away his face, regained the world, released himself from that immaterial bondage of the spirit, which meant the eternal liberty of Heaven, and would no longer be his companion in the mystical company of the Priesthood.

Ah! he too—the great Cardinal—had his affections, his heart yearned with the human implanted hunger for love, thirsting perhaps with a grievous loneliness for that metaphysical union of soul with soul, which, in its dim and spectral way, stands for those—involved in self immolation to the world—for the material partnership of the earth. The Cardinal prayed for Amato’s joy, his success, his usefulness; he struggled with himself to give those prayers true potency, and he fought off the complaining regrets that he really felt. He prayed for Helen Lorimer, he prayed that this union of two whom he had watched and guided, who represented to him a very virtual excellence, might redound to the glory of the Church, might form a new and blessed nucleus for the spreading of truth.

And, even as he prayed, his heart rebelled. <i>He knew that.</i> But before he left that throne of grace, though the night was spent in the vast effort he would subdue the warring flesh—if flesh it was—and, devoutly, humbly, yield up his life-long hope to the ordained purposes of his God.The twilight faded, the poor wanderers from the street who had entered to grasp some crumbs of comfort in the still holiness of the place, had, one by one, wonderingly left. A desperate woman came to the chancel rails and sobbed her heartbreak there, and she, with a muttered ejaculation of surprise, had gone away, the sexton snuffed out the tapers, and observing the Cardinal yet motionless on the altar grades, had crept off in a keen bewilderment; darkness muffled nave and apse and transept in the black gloom, save as a penumbral light smeared the colored windows, and broke disheartenedly through their red and purple shades; the whispering noises of a deserted habitation were heard, and with them came anon cries, or rumblings, or beats of trotting horses, or uncertain whizzes, impossible to understand, and yet—the unmoved figure of the Cardinal like a blacker clot stained the lightness of the altar steps.

But now he rises; the victory is won; he has surrendered his heart’s fondest desire, and his soul in contentment, with submission, bows to the new change. But were the darkness less deep, one standing near his stumbling path out of the sanctuary, would have detected tears on the grave face, and lines beneath the tired eyes.

Still later in the night, as when the dawn was first preparing the eastern skies, a reeling man leaning against the wall of the Bishop’s house stared upward and saw a light in a lower window, and he saw into the room, and saw there too a man dressed and reading; it was the Cardinal, and the page he so steadfastly perused held these words; they may be found in <i>De Imitatione Christi</i>;“My son renounce thyself and thou shalt find Me. Give up all choice, all personal inclination, and thou shalt even be the gainer. For greater grace shall be given thee as soon as thou resignest thyself unalterably.

“‘O Lord how often shall I resign myself, and in what respects am I to renounce myself?’

“Always every hour: in small things as well as greater ones. I except nothing; but wish to find thee in every respect naked. How otherwise will thou be able to be Mine, and I thine, unless thou have been deprived of every personal inclination, respecting either external things or internal feelings? The sooner thou dost accomplish this the better it will be with thee, and the more completely and thoroughly thou doest this, the more wilt thou please Me, and the greater will be thy gain?”

The drunken man fearful a little of the apparelled priest, and not unobservant of the approaching steps of a policeman, stole away in the deserted street, where the fading light of the lamps yet struggled with the misty lightening of the sky.

And just at that same hour, the luxurious Amato pushing his feet out of the sheet upon the rugged floor, and seeing the same dawn paint with its dewy rays the opposite wall, rubbing his winking eyes straightened himself, and staring at the colored picture of a full sailing ship upon the rolling green waves of an interminable sea, suddenly leaped to his feet and cried;“It was the same. Yes, the lady in the box at the Mayor’s speech in the Carnegie Hall, was Helen Lorimer.”

<b>CHAPTER X</b>

THE MAYOR’S COURTSHIP

Admiration throughout the City-State had been growing over the administration of the Mayor, its Governor, and it had not been confined to any class. He had exhibited exacting industry, careful judgment and unexpectedly evinced the qualities of poise and decision, virtues which the observant Mr. Briggs had so hugely doubted he possessed. Nor did he appear less advantageously on the dress occasions of the city’s public ceremonies. He had made a really remarkable public speech in which he discussed many of the coming questions of the day and century, emphasized the absolute supremacy of Law and Order and, by an adroit mixture of rhetoric and facts, succeeded in holding an audience composed of every grade and circumstance, and which departed uttering golden opinions over his sense and his eloquence.

He had not hesitated to explain his action in allowing a reprieve to the convicted anarchists—an action which for a time brought down upon his head the fulminations of the better papers, and exposed him to the scurrilous abuse of his enemies—and defended their complete right to obtain a new trial. Without divulging the nature of the new evidence in their behalf, he boldly declared an injustice had been done and that, if judgment was suspended, his course would be universally endorsed.He talked straight to the hearts of his hearers, in this matter, and appealed to their American sense of fairness. He had consulted Helen Lorimer about this address, and almost with rapture he had inserted in it many of her suggestions, jealously preserving their exact language.

Her cheeks flushed as she recognized her own words, and she perceptibly leaned forward a little to catch the well adjusted sentences, joining with proud elation in the tribute of applause as he ended.

The speech had been given in the Carnegie Hall—still after so many years, the chosen forum for public discussions—and all through it the young speaker turned his eyes constantly to the box in which the Lorimers were sitting, not with too puerile an attention, but just so much as would enable him to see the face of the lovely woman, and catch from her approval, inspiration, for his task. His eyes were not the only ones which sought that place, for Helen Lorimer never seemed more beautiful. Her presence gave the occasion distinction, though it was conjoined with the gratified recognition of the aristocracy of Carrara. The new Mayor pleased them. He had been seen often at the Lorimers, and the impression he made was admirable. His intentions were suspected, and that very fact threw into his public appearances—to them—a fresh interest. Not indeed that much was said about it, and nothing was known.

It was understood in some indirect way, that the Mayor and Helen Lorimer were together interested in the forthcoming trial, and while Miss Lorimer’s social movements were rigorously watched, the dispensations of good taste were not at any time forgotten.Her splendid public activity belonged to the history of the times, and the frequent presence of the Governor in her house, could be readily explained in other obvious ways, than by the gratuitous assumption of any more delicate relation, than the calls of the state. A group of malcontents—those whose machinations with Longtinsky were suspected by Helen Lorimer herself—and who belonged to the rich classes, were very envious of the Mayor’s popularity with their own friends, and viewed with positive hatred his assured entrance within the confines of their social prestige and privileges.

Many men among them, and some women, convinced themselves that the Mayor was a masked demagogue, at heart desirous of a political revolution, that his clemency to the anarchists was a cheap trick, and that his popularity was a menace and also his talents. To these people Helen Lorimer, though the most distinguished member of their order, was a dreamer and a dilletante. But some of them felt again their own danger, from the probable exposure of their tricks and conspiracies. The Mayor <i>was not</i> popular with Theodore Lorimer.

Helen Lorimer sat in a box between her uncle and her mother at the front, and a group of neighbors, in which a few handsome men appeared, assembled behind her. Hats were not removed, and the purple velvet Gainsborough exquisitely formed, and becoming her dark abundant hair gave her a most charming effectiveness. As the Mayor gazed at her from his seat on the platform, she became an Aurora, this minute his own dawn, and the rushing years, full of work, of usefulness, of conquests, the glorious endless day of Life.Never before had he felt such gratitude for what powers he knew he owned, and he fairly grew impatient to begin, that the tide of his powers thus awakened might flow unimpeded—flow, as it were, to <i>her</i> feet.

His heart sprang to his lips, and the words as if attuned to some inner harmony of love, were rich with gracious melody, and strong with manly thought. Mr. Briggs stood near the stage in a side aisle with Tom Ratler, and it was amusing to note his slowly gathering astonishment. His old doubts as to the character of the new Mayor were surely giving way before the man’s manifest power, but Mr. Briggs had still obstinately—perhaps a little vainly—clung to the conviction, that it was he—BRIGGS—that shaped his ends, and lest the Mayor should fail to understand this, Mr. Briggs plied him more vigorously than ever with long winded speeches, and animated apostrophes, and educational diversions. All of which the Mayor with grace and good nature accepted heartily, though behind his secretary’s back his smile prolonged itself into a wearied grimace of forbearance.

The Mayor’s heart was not the only one stirred, not the only one which beat with quickened pulses. Never did Helen Lorimer think so well of him as then; she made the confession to herself, and she made it with blushes bending, by one knows not what spell, to the witchery and command of words—mere words—And yet—the Mayor was not deprived of the charm of physical interest, though in his case it was subtly wrought in with intellectual expression.His winning play of features, the earnestness and beauty of his eyes, a peculiar and native grace of motion in the tall lithe frame, and the constant attractiveness of the ingratiating mouth, framed for speech, told effectively upon his audience, and it was even fondly noted in its details by one, whose lightest thought for him was priceless.

And in that sea of faces, on the occasion of this memorable address of the Mayor, was a face belonging to one, who by the magic of the destiny ruling the ways of men was drawn—not by accident, for there are no accidents in the uses of fate—into his audience. It was Amato.

He was then still an occupant of the Anchorite, and had not yet had the interview with the Cardinal, that we have just witnessed. He was placed far up against the ceiling of the enclosure, having walked through Fifty-sixth Street, and lingering for a minute on the opposite side of the way, noticed the broad signs announcing the speech of the Mayor. There was no room anywhere else in the packed and eager house. Amato had heard of the new Mayor, and the fame of his successful combination of warring policies in the last election, winning the choice of the great city by his naïve but skillful appeal to hostile parties. It had been commented on in the papers both of Europe and the States. He felt curious to see this rising political Cicero or young Alcibiades—as he musingly remarked—and so fought his way up to the top gallery of the hall. And none listened with more open and delighted ears than Amato.Emancipating himself from the narrow seclusion of his student’s life, responding intuitively to the power of personality and of talent, the Mayor’s eloquence, the fine modulation and grasping insistency of his voice, and the breadth and culture of his views, allied with a certain artfulness of phrase, carried the young ascetic off his feet. His applause was not very apparent, and one observing him might have thought that he was not pleased, but the conclusion would have been a stupid misunderstanding.

Amato became so interested, and so instinctively endorsed the Mayor’s attitude that he forgot the customary tribute, and while others were clapping, was himself revolving the Mayor’s weighty conclusions. And it was just then, in one of the outbursts of applause, that his wandering eye caught sight of Helen Lorimer, for she too joined in the enthusiasm. She was far away from his lofty perch, but Amato, awakening to the attributes of beauty, feeling the flourishing power of his senses, and innately luxurious and tasteful, saw her, and noted her charm. It was only after his interview with the Cardinal that—as he arose from his slumbers the next morning—he consciously identified the face he had seen at Carnegie Hall, with the visitor of the Cardinal’s.

The industry of the Mayor was remarkable; he had begun a splendid effort to reform the clerical service of the city, and to bring into operation a new system of accounting. The enormous growth of the city during the first half of the twentieth century had quadrupled its debt, and made its yearly budget formidable.The outcry against extravagance had been gaining in strength, and there seemed some reason for it, though it was in a measure exaggerated by a popular subserviency to the sensational mongerings of newspapers, and rabid articles in the monthlies. The great city with its almost unexampled generosity towards all sorts of public enterprises, its stupendous educational system, and the vastness of its improvements, had indeed assumed heavy burdens, reflected in a mounting tax levy. The century had developed a strong tendency towards a hidden sort of socialism, and while the organization of society was unchanged, heavy taxes imposed upon the property of the rich and upon corporations, lent a semblance of reality to the claim that the city government—and it was symptomatic of municipal conditions throughout the world—had become socialistic.

The Mayor confronted a discontented public when he assumed office as the governor of the little municipal state. Capable and experienced, and gifted with invention, inclined himself to view in a new light the dawning tendencies of the time, which really urged a greater participation of the many in the aggregate returns of the state, he met upon his inauguration a double problem. He was expected to give a business administration, curbing excess, limiting expenditures, where they were foolish or unnecessary, diminishing waste, and also, by some process of education, making the entire city realize that it must succumb to the refreshing influences of an increasing desire to advance the weal and general happiness, introducing—not recklessly or with demagogic aims—an idealism of sympathy between all classes.Helen Lorimer had herself engaged in the latter problem, approaching it from the side of Class, with the strongest instincts of Individualism, forming—if she could—an aristocracy of Worth, which should assume a guiding hand in the control of affairs, and involve itself in a perennial ministration of mercy and helpfulness. Unseen to her there lay in all her thoughts the latent socialistic image; to the Mayor that image was clearly seen, and attacking the problem more from the side of the Commoner, he was never deceived or misled.

This community of serious interest had greatly heightened the flavor of the Mayor’s acquaintanceship with Helen Lorimer. Their most significant qualities of mind, had been mutually seen, and while they very earnestly differed, there was no loss of admiration on either side. More secretly and more profoundly they felt the collision in themselves of two irreconcilable entities, the religious idea, and the scientific idea. On that ground their tempers were inclined to take fire, but the Mayor’s constraining love corrected his impulse, and Helen’s slowly increasing surrender and engrossment oftentimes closed her lips.

The evening of the fourteenth of July was spent by the Mayor at the villa of the Lorimers’. The company—not a large party—ate in the salon the reader has already visited, and perhaps for the first time—or almost the first—some whispered conjectures were hazarded, that the Mayor was decidedly interested in Helen Lorimer, while eloquent glances told the same story. None of those present at the dinner—with one exception, the count and his wife—at which Miss Lorimer so boldly threw down the gauntlet of her convictions as to the transplantation of the Pope, were this evening among the little throng.It was most variously composed, though the young people predominated; the few elders being well preserved old ladies, whose composure it would have been dangerous for the younger set to have disturbed by any frivolity. These old ladies were solid exemplars of propriety, well dressed and effectively garrulous. The banquet was an informal collation, the usual service of the young men to the ladies prevailing, the latter sitting about the room, or, in many cases, out of it, in such inviting nooks and retreats as offered less surveillance for their coquetry and the interchange of vows.

In this confusion, amid the decorous collision of flying Ganymedes, replenishing emptied plates from the overburdened table, the suave exit and entrance of servants, the noisy expostulations of the avid dowagers, and the murmurous tumult of comment, pleasure, and platitudes, the Mayor and Helen talked alone at one end of the room. Here they were soon joined by a shaking but emphatic old gentleman—a Livingston—and a middle aged banker—a Gunther—whose cushioned perfection of deportment, finish of address, and scrupulous attention to the removal of crumbs from his attire, contrasted amusingly with the older man’s carelessness, nervousness, and vituperation.

“You spoke well Mr. Mayor at Carnegie Hall,” it was the nervous old gentleman speaking, “but you don’t use the meat axe—the meat axe—as you should. I tell you Sir, that fine words never buttered parsnips, and if you try pretty words on these socialistic renegades you’ll get them back in your ears charged with curses. Hound them out—out. You had no justification for interference. Let ’em hang!”“But surely,” Miss Lorimer began, “Mr. Livingston you don’t wish to be so unjust as to slay the innocent. I cannot think you aspire to the detestable distinction of being a murderer.”

“A murderer, Miss Lorimer? The term can be given some meanings that take away its reproach. War is not murder although we kill innocent and guilty alike. Intimidation is worth more than just flat justice in this matter. Besides in the case of pestilence those exposed to the plague must be quarantined along with those who are sick. That’s plain—very plain—and it’s furthermore plain to me that a new trial means procrastination—compromise—escape.

“It’s futile—futile—Yes! it’s knavery to think of it!”

“Ah now,” this time the intercessory banker, “really Mr. Livingston I think you mistake the nature of the evidence. Let us be judicial—strictly judicial—the judicial course is the safest course. It is sanctioned by authority and reason.”

“What! the Law’s way!” cried the irate representative of a family which had given its members to the State and the Bench, “I tell you Sir, the Law is the enemy of sense. It plunders our pocket-books, and what does it give back?—demurrers and writs, and bills, and motions. Martial Law, Sir I believe in, and martial law is the cure-all just now. But your ordinary law, your court law—why! it’s a mouse in a gally-pot—a buzz-fly in a pounding barrel! Surgery, <i>no</i> medicine!”The Mayor had not spoken; he was still seated watching with a curious semblance of avoidance of debate, the face of Helen—he was beginning to be audacious enough, softly to whisper to himself “his Helen”—but now he stood, and turning to Mr. Livingston who was likely to fly into a rage at his exasperating silence spoke, while at the same moment Count Schinazi and his approving and inseparable wife approached the group.

“Mr. Livingston you are afraid of the peace and permanency of our State, because of these socialists, to whom you, on that account, deny justice. Do you recall this fable of La Fontaine? A ruler of a realm was told by the messenger of a hostile country that his master could summon an army from all his dependencies whereupon a wise courtier told the trembling monarch that it recalled to him a vision that he had dreamt; he had seen a dragon with many heads and only one tail on the other side of a hedge, and, thus affrighted, he had stood dumb, and powerless with fear, when he saw that because it had many heads it could not pierce the hedge between them; but, thus recovered from his dread, the next instant he saw a dragon with ONE head and MANY tails approach the hedge; this last easily passed the hedge with all his tails, one making a path for the next. The Dragon with many heads is harmless.”The rejoinder was a little unexpected, and the irascible critic was non-plussed, to recover however with the muttered ejaculation that he knew a fable worth two of that, “the mouse who gnawed the nets of the lion and let him loose”; at which the Mayor laughed, and Mr. Livingston, shaking his head ominously, retired.

“Veery vell put your Honor,” it was the voice and grinning phiz of the Count, “bit I tink de gentlemans who vas so, as you say, peevish, might have, as you say, got you von better; vas it not a leetle like dis, you haf de bone in de mouf like de leetle dog in de storie, and ven you look in de vater you drop de bone to geet de shadow and you loose bofe. Ees it not day vay?”

“I hardly think so Count Schinazi,” the mayor had made the acquaintance of the eccentric foreigner and the two men rather liked each other, “at any rate the bone is not yet <i>out</i> of my mouth, and if I am chasing shadows, we sometimes say rainbows, I am not likely to disappoint any one but myself—or am I?” The last words were directed to Miss Lorimer, and the smile and reproach that dawned on her face again told the Mayor that he was something more to her than a name and a friend simply.

Just then the voice of Theodore Lorimer was heard requesting silence; there was an instant suppression of the social hubbub, and the same voice told the company that if they would follow him they would be led to the roof of the northern towers, where there would be music, and a view of some few fireworks, and where there was also furnished, upon one of the broader spaces a carpet covered square for the dancers.This announcement was received with unconcealed admiration, and the chorus of applause from the many couples, summoned hither and thither from their coverts and hiding spots was overwhelming, causing Mr. Lorimer to remark to a business neighbor “that Terpsichore was probably a more popular deity than Columbia.”

The path taken by the guests led through a remarkable series of halls and up many broad stairways graced with niches, holding bronzes and marble figures, with walls decorated—not lavishly—with paintings, and at the end of the ranges led into a spiral stairs, from which eyelet windows covered the adjacent valleys. The hilarious company emerged out upon a roof flanked by four towers, in a sort of uncovered room, over which a trellis hung, carrying pendent vines, a summery pergola. The towers flanking this space were pierced by doorways, and these opened within upon narrower ascents, to the tops of the towers themselves. In the centre of the room was the dancing carpet, and the musicians, set to one side, already welcomed the guests with pleasant melodies.

Almost the last to reach the outlook was Miss Lorimer and the Mayor, and when they came, the place was in a surge of enjoyment; the musicians breathed the enchanted measures of a Waldteufel waltz, the dancers were noiselessly circling over the middle area, while in all sorts of ways the rest of the group was engaged, in talking, in rapturous comments upon the beauty of the night and the scene, or taking restricted walks about the parapets. Helen Lorimer had pressed forward, and, with the Mayor following, both with an unusual eagerness, quite oblivious of the others, who for their part were equally indifferent advanced to one of the tower doors, entered, and disappeared.An instant later Helen Lorimer came to the top—a round enclosure with a low wall—and after her the Mayor. How was it that the woman put back her hand, as if to assist him, and how was it that it was so smoothly taken, and then—Oh you artful deities who watch such things and watching smile—how was it that the man kissed it and kept it? That kiss Helen felt, not alone upon her hand but very mightily upon her heart; yes the confession rose to her, as with the sudden spring of a concealed conviction, that, rushing in all the tides of her being, she had hitherto shunned and repudiated.

The mounting color told the abject fact, though in the softly illumined night, <i>that</i> was hid from the man at her side, her pulsating heart told the same story, though those rapid beats none could hear but herself, certainly not he who seemed indeed dazed with some dizzying emotion, for he left her side and gazed upward silent. Nor was it alone the color in her cheeks, or the tumult at her heart that thus miserably proclaimed her conquest; her limbs proved traitorous, and the brave, strong woman sank upon a narrow stone seating, that surrounded the wall, suffocated with her own intolerable defeat. Defeat—yes in one way as women feel it—but after the shock of surrender has passed, then the pent up tide inundates the whole being,

        _till strange love grown bold,
Think true love, acted, simple modesty_.

The Mayor came back to her side and standing there, leaned nearer and whispered; “Have I offended you Helen?”He had never spoken her name before, and the sound of it from his lips startled her, with even a keener realization of the sudden change in herself. And yet not sudden. The blossom that to-day blushes on the branches of the rose bush means an inevitable process of formation, a graduated range of growth from the tiniest bud to its challenging beauty, the mute unfolding of an invisible yet discreet nourishment that built up its silken petals, gave to it its subtle fragrance, and painted it with colors blended from the earth and sky. These steps in nature are not sudden, and yet when the bush, that yesterday was but a wealth of buds, to-day is seen laden with flowers, the change seems sudden—but it is SEEMS only; the process unmarked by the eye, has taken time and if, with favoring circumstances, with greater heat and light, it has advanced more quickly, not a single step in its adventurous course has been missed or skipped.

After his first declaration on the night of their conference in the museum, the Mayor had withdrawn into a modest silence, though his affections were clearly understood by Helen, and this diffidence itself charmed her. His excellent mind, the powers of thought that he showed, a peculiar earnestness, together with a reticence, delighted her. He took pains to understand her projects and even to criticise them. He did no longer conceal his own prejudices but clothed in his ingenuous speech they seemed less dreadful to her. He had a boyish way, blent with an extreme elegance of manner, a certain distinction of address, and this favored in him a personal brightness of appearance, hard to describe, but easily recognized. And then their quarrels—why they so often dissolved in laughter!It was on her side, Helen’s, a preposterous antipathy to the scientific dryness and rigor of exact conclusions, on his, a humorous enmity of too much belief in the unknown, a quiet skepticism that showed itself unabashed before her intense appeals, and yet was always unobtrusive. At times they did discover how far apart lay their ultimate goals of tendency, and then Helen mourned that it was so, and for that very reason loved him better. Her steadfast heart, bathed in a deep catholic spirituality, wanted dearly to bring him to her own standpoint of view, and in her prayers his name was not omitted.

There were indeed moments when they rebelled against each other, when they felt the chasm of a deep difference lying between them, and yet why was it that even then they felt their arms stretched above it, towards each other, as if to bridge and close it? In both there was an inherent honesty that forbade compromise, and yet they were not reluctant to hide their feud. Often it came to this, that the Mayor said, he thought better now than he ever could have believed that he would think, of religion, <i>because of her</i> its precepts gained an unexpected beauty. And they would both grow serious or perhaps both smile, and in those smiles lurked the honey of an increasing love between them.

And as for the Mayor the rich tide of life into which he entered when with Helen Lorimer, her personal glory united with her elevation of mind which his nature adored, her goodness, and the fine touches of her delicate fancy almost swept him from his feet. He loved passionately, and in spite of that he kept his footing, strained to the last effort of exertion by his will, his dominant sense of fitness, and by the call of his duties.He felt somehow that he even maintained a supremacy in mind, and this helped him to abstain from violence and entreaty. He felt too, with intoxication that the end must come as he willed it, and as he felt that, the surges of desire rising about him almost overwhelmed him.

They had seen a great deal of each other. There was the trial to rehearse, upon which depended so much for the reputation of the Mayor, and the overthrow of the enemies of order and the enemies of justice. There were conferences with Mr. Moser the lawyer, who was to open the new trial. There was Helen’s museum projects, and her new plans of the Settlement House. There were broader, grander imaginings of the Mayor himself, for the great city, to whose service he bent all his powers, wedding it with the love that now controlled him to the last fibres of his being. These plans were also revealed to Miss Lorimer and discussed with her—how eloquently!—and her views sought!

And yet by the strangest of collusions there had been no gossip among the news mongers, no whisperings in the public prints, no express admissions in SOCIETY, nowhere any concurrent wonderings and guesses and intimations. And all because both the Mayor and Helen had, in their intercourse, admonished suspicion by their own discretions. Both were eminent, and withdrawn somewhat in that fame, were less carelessly discussed. Observation too passed beyond them to the great trial of which now the general mind was full.There was unrest in the city over the socialistic propaganda on the east side, for though the office of The Beehive had been seized—Mr. Briggs had attended to that, and Tom Ratler had been his good agent in finding out the new location of the dangerous paper—no further move towards its suppression had been made, and it was realized that the turbulent organ yet maintained itself, and that an underground circulation of its inflammatory appeals was going on, “making surer” as Mr. Briggs said, “every day the eventual explosion and with rapine and conflagration.”

There was some national alarm besides over threatened war, and it seemed as if, for the last time, the convulsed earth stood on the brink of a terrific combat, its elements shaking into compact orbs of allies, whose circling collision would remake the civilized world.

But three persons in the huge city knew of this growing love. Helen’s mother, Julieta, knew it and the Mayor’s mother knew it, and Tom Ratler knew it. But not one of these could tell the others, Julieta knew it, less by any visible witness, than by the safe conclusion of her mother’s heart, and her woman’s instinct; the Mayor’s mother knew it because her son had told her; and Tom Ratler knew it from the moment he had seen the Mayor accompany Miss Lorimer down the City Hall’s steps, and caught the Mayor’s eye following her vanishing vehicle. With such as Tom, love is everywhere, in the daily air, in each turn of the head, and a man not in love is a man not alive. Tom was himself so dreadfully fettered, that his aching soul was only at peace when he could see others in the same predicament, while his mind, with a ferret’s ease, caught the faintest whiff, as it were, of love stirring in the air.And now Helen and the Mayor knew it themselves. It filled their hearts, as a wine the chalice, but it could not stay there, for it was a rising torrent and dauntless, sped to their lips and spoke.

“Offense? How can there be offense, if I could have wished it?” it was Helen’s answer.

“Oh Helen, I have waited for this; I have prayed for it; it has come to me in dreams, and when I awoke the dream, and the dear thing I dreamt, seemed hopelessly far off and impossible. “How wonderfully you have stimulated and inspired me during this last month, I can hardly tell you. The poetic nature was strong within him; it had been sagaciously apprehended by Mr. Briggs and that sage observer felt in it a danger to the state and an inscrutable foe to his own complete possession of his superior. In books, in stolen contemplations, the Mayor had secretly fed his cravings, though the hard aspects of life, and the unflinching tyranny of events had robbed poetry to him of much of its meanings. How strange it was, that here in the very garden of fruition he felt the <i>darkening blight of storm</i>! was it his own timidity, or was it the inversion of his own happiness, inverted by its own excess:

<i>dark with excessive light</i>?

_A soul dissolving odour, to invite

The lights had become more numerous in the heavens, and the dancing fires of the wavering or shooting aeronauts added a stranger splendor to the air. An instant later a sharp crack emitted somewhere from the highest southernward tower accompanied by a sputtering line of sparks, surprised the gathering, and the huddled crowd watched with amazement a shooting winding sheet of flame surround the pinnacle, rise, and light a set piece of pyrotechnics at the top. There had been some fear about the fireworks, there was considerable agitation over the discovery of the interesting relation of Helen Lorimer and the Governor of New York. Do you blame me?”Mr. Theodore Lorimer was a business man; he was also a widower, and though no doubt he had had his love affairs, his demeanor to the tenderness of life was a little austere and unbendingly practical. He fully appreciated his niece’s talent and beauty; had a rather vainglorious pride in the Lorimer family, and he estimated with the keenest pleasure their exact wealth. His hopes with regard to Helen were certainly indefinite, but he felt the Mayor’s humble origin, as the phrase goes, and while he knew his ability, and his possibilities of progression and promotion, he somehow yet stuck to that invincible theory of caste, of class, the inbred feeling of separation. He was loyal to American ideas, he was patriotic, but he treasured the aristocratic formula—as indeed Helen herself did, but with a difference—and he believed in making that aristocratic ideal a very influential and beneficial factor in American public life, under just those incentives reviewed in the earlier pages of this story. He was not—to be really truthful—satisfied with the Mayor.

His family too was a Catholic one, and though creed offered no rifts among the rich associates of Carrara it did sensibly suggest an antagonism to a man like the Mayor, who was not of them, and who had, in his earlier life, actually written a book upon the needlessness of religion in the current meaning of the word. Then he suspected the Mayor of socialism, which he thought already tinctured unfavorably his own niece’s views, and socialism was to Theodore Lorimer a monstrous sin. The very word brought a frown to a face uniformly serene. He stood in a very close relation to his niece since her father’s death, and with her mother constituted a strict guardianship, which Helen respected, and, after the Catholic fashion, filially honored.Besides, and in addition, he experienced a vague resentment against the Mayor for the, to him, unnoticed insinuation of himself in the favor of his niece. There was a suggestion in it to Theodore Lorimer of something surreptitious and vulgar, it contravened the dignity of the method he should have preferred. Somehow it seemed to him that under the guise of conferences—conferences which he had regarded askance, as in his heart he had small sympathy with the end in view—about a public question, the Mayor had succeeded in injecting a love mission which was neither creditable nor desirable.

During the hour between the letting off of the <i>feu-de-joie</i> when the speaking isolation of the Mayor with Miss Lorimer was discovered, and the departure of the guests, and the Mayor’s personal appeal, Theodore had been working himself into a state of mind. This became more bitter as he reviewed the chances—the matrimonial chances—his niece could hope for. That extravagant and stupid idolatry, which rich American girls suffered from, for foreign placards and names, had greatly disappeared, at the beginning of the twentieth-first century, although the ludicrous hunt on the part of impecunious and wasted and morally unfit scions of noble houses for American money still persisted. But the gloss dazzled, and the lure of name yet exerted its malign and fallacious attractions. The malady had taken a more aesthetic and reputable form. The offers from Europe were more carefully scrutinized, and they were themselves less mercenary though seldom could the taint of a money thirst be utterly excluded.The Lorimers had been much in Europe, and Helen’s charm had gathered about her many shining lights, handsome and striking figures in its public and social circles, and none among these had to the observant eyes of Julieta and Theodore seemed more acceptable than the young Count of Monte Ferrato who had also shown his immediate interest in Helen. Some such marriage would have frankly pleased Mr. Lorimer, and while the matter to Julieta properly remained, as it should in a woman’s thought, in the realm of ideation and emotion, she too regarded the young foreign notable with partiality. As for domestic unions, as Theodore said to Julieta, Helen “had the world to choose from.”

The insoluble element was Helen herself; she neither leaned with prestige or property, as might have easily been discerned. Her Americanism was of the just type and she lived in ideals political and religious, and the former sprang from the latter. Certainly she had responded with appreciation perhaps to the admiration she excited, but none of her adorers had touched these springs of feeling which open a woman’s heart and win her. This was the bothersome part of it to Theodore Lorimer, and while Julieta was amused, noticing her brother-in-law’s vexation, she felt too the responsible task of guiding Helen in her final choice, if she made it, and yet was bewildered by the unusual nature of her daughter, which rather defied coercion. She had endeavored in talking with Helen to get some inkling of her feelings, but she only disconsolately admitted that it was impossible, as Helen felt as yet no overpowering choice.With that she dropped it, believing that Helen would take care of herself in the great concern of her marriage, and having no inconsiderable admixture of romantic sympathy in her own nature was content to leave things to the God of Love, who indeed, while not infallible, does adjust things of this sort fairly well.

“I would like to talk this matter over with you Mr. Mayor. It is a subject upon which I feel strongly and perhaps my views may not fully coincide with yours. While the two men, not moving together, but behind each other, thus made their way through the scattered and departing friends, Helen had resumed her neglected duties. Beware of opening out thy bosom to it.
Lest thou myself, and all thy flock should catch
An after ague—fit to trembling_.

Mr. Lorimer led the Mayor to a room in the villa used by him as a private office whose one window looked out upon the Kingsbridge road.There was not much furniture in it, a large table littered with papers, with a glass of roses decorating the centre, and filling the air with fragrance, which to the Mayor seemed an imprisoned effluence warning him. It was small wonder, thought the Mayor as his eyes eagerly perused the big picture, that Helen was beautiful; the ordinary course of heredity would have been strangely perverted if from such parents beauty had not sprung.

It had been painted at the time (about the middle last of the twentieth century) when there had been a temporary, but most improving, return to the fashions of the beginning of the nineteenth, when high collars with spreading tips and stocks, erect coat collars, constricted waists, and voluminous scarfs, conferred an ancient dignity to the imperial dudes. Of that there could be no doubt, for he also knew full well the sinewy resistance of his own nature, and how quickly the fires of his heart kindled into resentment, when insult, or rebuke, or any form of immoderate opposition stumbled in his path. Not that he was unreasonable, but that he also was proud, with the pride that was first born in him, and with some natural increase from his life’s material success. Mr. Lorimer drew forward a chair and motioning to his visitor, sat down himself upon the opposite side of the table which thus was between them. Mr. Lorimer had hardly up to this moment kept his gaze fixed upon the Mayor. Mr. Lorimer had retired after his talk with the Mayor in a very ruffled frame of mind, and, as it made him nervous and sleepless, he charged his disorder and discomfort upon that unfortunate official. The waking hours of the night only fortified in him a resolve to prevent the union of his niece and the Mayor, and he determined to hasten—were he successful in diverting his niece from this unwelcome infatuation—Helen’s nuptials with some one to his mind more suitable, and more agreeable to himself. The gentleman however understood the strength of will in his niece, though with that was conjoined, as he also knew, a tenderness of love and obedience, that he had often put to the test.

But he felt uneasy over any discussion of the subject with Helen, and he too keenly realized that his own discretion in the matter needed her mother’s assistance, and this he prepared himself to obtain. Her mother seldom, he knew, disturbed Helen’s “visionary schemes,” but in some ways her influence upon her daughter was decisive, and if—working upon these ideas of caste benefit, and the public protection of an aristocratic superiority, which she preached—her mother could make her see the importance of her reserving the union of her family within a class restriction, his point might be gained.The Mayor was an excellent man (even then Mr. Lorimer made reservations)—Yes—an able man—Yes—a man with a future—Yes—but he was a self-made man, a man of the people, and he was <i>bourgeoise</i>, and that to the pinched apprehension of Mr. Theodore Lorimer was an incredible predicament. As Mr. Lorimer had known very little about any sort of Love, that ingredient in the problem gave him no anxiety, though as a cultivated man he recognized certain poetic obstinacies connected with a lover’s prepossessions.

When he opened the Mayor’s letter he could not repress a motion of gratitude towards the writer; “Amazingly sensible,” he repeated; “I will assure him of our political support,” and with a renewed cheerfulness in his admiration of his own astuteness, the stilted gentleman stepped into the dining-room, and after saluting his sister-in-law watched for Helen’s appearance with undisguised impatience. And, when she came, she seemed the same divine and happy being he had always known; perhaps he noticed that she ate but little, and seemed preoccupied, but that was mere physical weariness. Surely this calm and serene being suffered from no insurmountable disappointment. AMATO’S COURTSHIP

The summer was passing along and the hot days began their painful visitations to the self-indulgent residents of Carrara. They fled from the heat with varying speed, and, strangely, few fled to Europe. The fashion of emptying New York, for the benefit of Europe, had changed into a domiciliary fever for summer homes in the remote and enticing counties of New England. The young, to see the world, as the saying is, made their pilgrimage abroad, but even with them the delights of the noble homes perched upon the Yankee hills, or embosomed in their green vales, proved effectively more attractive, and kept them in America. Many of the descendants of those who in previous centuries had exiled themselves to European cities, had deserted their foreign houses, and encamped again upon the domestic soil, bringing with them accumulations of art and a somewhat unamericanized habit of thought, expression and manners.

Living in the United States had indeed, in the year of grace, 2000, reached among the rich much of that exquisite comfort, which had distinguished it in Europe, enhanced by the many luxurious pleasures, and expensive delicacies created by American ingenuity, wealth, and fastidiousness.

The Lorimers were almost the last to leave the city. They had a fine estate on the hills overlooking the sweet valley of Stowe, at the foot of Mount Mansfield in Vermont, a place occupied a hundred years before by the Tabers of New York.The wandering and comfortable house hung on the edge of Cobb’s Hill, a broad large spacious villa on the brow, looking directly against Mansfield, and over the wonderfully varied and beautiful intervale of farms and woodland, hardly in any way changed from its present aspect. The atmospheric charm was scarcely less delightful than the rich abundant picture of physical features, with the Hogback ranges to the east, and the sculptured colossus of Mansfield in front, to the west.

Helen had expected some further word from the Mayor, and his utter silence wounded, and abused her. She was left with no explanation of his sudden change of will, no word of that reversal which she, in her heart, believed was accompanied in him with pain and suffering. It seemed to her so strange and indefinable, that she was willing to attribute it to some psychological eccentricity, to a kind of mental diversion, that she perhaps could not begin to understand, but which she, by guessing a little wildly, saw, with certain nervous and imaginative temperaments—and she knew the Mayor belonged to these—might be an extravagant accompaniment (and outside of their control) of their emotional make-up.

And yet——those words of love which sounded so sweet to her, which seemed so sincere and fetched from an inward need, past doubt or misgivings, how was it possible that they could be gainsaid? To so quick an intelligence as Helen Lorimer’s the thought had not failed to come, that her uncle might have interfered; that he might have wounded her lover by some repulse,—and yet, though she saw this possibility, by an interior conflicting pride, and desert too, she would not mention it to Theodore Lorimer.Nor did that plotting guardian say anything to her. He noticed the absence of the Mayor, the abstraction and nervous reticence of his niece, and he safely concluded that the best way out of this dilemma was for him, until directly appealed to, to let things smooth their way as they would, and the end would justify the means. For it was now clear to him that the young protegée of the Cardinal more nearly met his wishes and expectations for his niece, and he felt also, in his floundering and stubborn way that Amato appeared at exactly the right moment to administer consolation.

He felt that there was a necessity for that, and, without much intuition in the matter—rejecting indeed any appeals to think about it,—Amato’s various gifts, his cultured sympathy, his adroit and incessant courtesies, his even gravity, not unmixed also with mirth, and above all his splendid physical perfection, would, he felt, increasingly banish the Mayor from Helen’s mind, all the more as that obliging individual kept out of sight.

And though his dull perceptions cruelly deceived him at many points, yet as nature is so made in us all, there was a practical and real efficacy in the companionship of the young man for Miss Lorimer; and, for the aspirations of the young man himself, her inward craving for help—which at such crises grows very strong with vivacious and intellectual natures——were favorable. And so it came about that while the Mayor remained hid and silent, suffering his great hurt and sickened in heart, the judicious spirits ruling over human affairs advanced Amato’s suit.And in the Mayor’s behalf there had been adjustments. A curious succession of turbulence had startled the state. There had been strikes and mass meetings of denunciation, on the part of tax-payers, and the risings of a new party and an unexpected recrudescence of Socialism involving a great number of discontented or unemployed people, while beneath this again was the slowly seething leaven of the anarchistic revolt, nurtured and spread by The Beehive.

The Mayor’s precocious mind grappled with these problems, his spirits were brightened by just such conflicts, and he felt the renovating ardor of combat. He saw—as Mr. Briggs already had seen, but with a different spirit—the insurmountable tendencies of the times approaching a broad cleavage in society between the well-to-do, the generally contented, and a mass of workers, to whom indeed the world did not deny the usual concomitants of satisfied existence, but who, brought in sight of an overwhelming luxury and undreamed of self-indulgence, experienced the pangs of envy, had begun to question the reasonableness of the present social <i>status</i>. And joined with these were a large body of men, holding the franchise, who were inoperative and unproductive, largely weak and sensual, whose natures craved immense satisfactions, to whom wealth had but one purpose, just such satisfaction and who would welcome any change that might bring within their uses the boundless aptitudes of money.The aristocratic leaders saw the same thing, and some among them, as Helen Lorimer, attempted to stem its progress by assimilating the vast army of workmen, artisans, small shop keepers, merchants, peddlers and street sellers into a group of admiring and enlightened dependents upon the richer classes. It was meant that they should realize the practical importance of the rich element in society and becoming attached to it by ties of gratitude surround it with a measurable force of protection, in case of its political attack.

With Miss Lorimer this movement was divested of selfish motives entirely, and she worked under the inspiration of a true idealistic altruism, conjoined with certain theoretical conceptions of a graded and classified social state. Other aristocratic leaders actively entered the service of the state, and proposed to use to its uttermost the advantages of the franchise, assuming the adherence of the conservative middle classes. This latter constituted the third class—a class to which the Mayor belonged or at least from which he had sprung—and which, by its instincts and the promptings of its cool common sense and unimaginative acceptance of the substantial limitations of life resented interference with its satisfied pursuit of money, and its pleasant enjoyment of customary and legitimate pleasures.

The Mayor had not lost his poise of judgment on account of his disappointment, and the gnawing sense of humiliation, at the hands of Mr. Lorimer, but, while he estimated the real value of a rich society justly, and felt that the aesthetic and cultured aspects of the city won an immense support from the rich classes,conscious of their educational relations to the rest of society—as indeed the aristocratic nuclei of the twenty-first century were themselves—yet he relied upon the stubborn fixity of the <i>bourgeoise</i> for the stability of the state. To this latter element he addressed his attentions, and he employed all the strategy and skill of a practised politician, as well as an astute lawyer, and an eloquent advocate, to strengthen his administration among them. He very narrowly watched the proceedings of the legislature, and analyzed pitilessly all their measures, exposing those which had the socialistic taint, and often went to the extreme precaution of justifying his action in public addresses. This was unusual but it proved immensely popular.

In the meanwhile all eyes were fixed upon the coming trial—set for the beginning of November—of the incendiaries. It was generally understood that there had been new evidence discovered, that would liberate the accused and convicted men; intimations prevailed that the Mayor himself had assisted in procuring this evidence, and that he had been convinced of its existence by the famous philanthropist, humanitarian, and scholar, Miss Helen Lorimer, herself a representative aristocrat. Of course, this action displeased many of the richer class, especially those who were themselves implicated in the treacherous plot to destroy Longtinsky, by creating for him an opportunity for mischief.The conservatives among the middle class were about equally divided as to its merits, and sagacity, but the socialists were immensely pleased, and the more candid did not conceal their approval of the Mayor. For the instant the Mayor attracted the attention of the whole city in a very supreme way, and to a heart and mind responding eagerly to the incentives of ambition, this popularity and intellectual domination gave him satisfaction. For the moment, and happily, these distractions served the purpose of repressing the poetic, emotional, and dreaming proclivities of the Mayor, though again too often he felt their intolerable cravings, and his smarting heart kept him sleepless and despairing.

The repulse of the Mayor by Mr. Lorimer had defeated another scheme, the murderous intrigue of Longtinsky and Poniatowsky. Longtinsky had calculated that Miss Lorimer at the closing in of the foundations of the new Settlement House, the plans of which had been published, enclosing the novel idea of the Museum, would attempt some public ceremony, and distinguish the occasion with an address. It was then that he hoped to precipitate a riot, and in the melee actually attempt her life, while at the same time a concerted rising might add to the confusion, and—it was his deliberate conviction—lead to an overthrow of the government.

This Clodian madness had been temporarily delayed by the indisposition of Helen Lorimer herself, who ordered the continuance of the building, but desisted from the especial corner-stone services which had been at first contemplated.She longed to escape the city, and the recurrent pictures of the gentle valley of Stowe, the morning and evening face of Mansfield with its strong lineaments like a sermon in philosophy and—unlike most sermons—made poetic by cloud and sunshine, mist, rain and starlight, these, rising in her memory, filled her with an insatiable longing; everything was hastened, and at the beginning of August the Lorimers had set out for Stowe.

Young Amato had been well received at the Lorimers. It could not have been otherwise; his introduction was influential, his own talent prepossessing, his manner charming, and the essential earnestness and a kind of dignified naïveté in the man, delightful. Helen acknowledged his admirable address, and his style of liberal Catholicism pleased her. A Catholic’s religion is an actual part of his or her life; it is not placed outside of the daily transactions of business or pleasure; in the devout it holds a lucid sway over the motions of the heart and mind, and its admonitions neither discourage happiness nor check speculation. Amato and Helen discussed together very often the project of the Pope’s coming to America, and for the instant this plan, looming larger and larger to her mind, suggesting—it may be foolishly and vainly—immense moral consequences, became a veritable comfort. She wrought it in her plans of social betterment, into her designs for the religious awakening of the state, into her dream of an established formulary for society, and with true Catholic ambition—often incontinent and fatalistic—a reorganized Catholic realm in the New World, free and triumphant, bringing to earth new treasures of devotion and character, swam temptingly before her mind.And with all these blissful imaginings Amato was associated. Nor was the association insincere. The same enthusiasm kindled in Amato’s heart, and, with him, it mingled with his love. And both desires added to one another their especial quality, which improved each. Amato’s love became idealized, his religion gathered from the earth the intensity and colorings of passion.

It was the last evening before the Lorimers, Helen and her mother—Theodore Lorimer had already left the city—went away, that Helen and Amato sat upon the tower’s roof, where the Mayor scarcely three weeks past, had received her surrender. Helen Lorimer was pale and silent. She remembered the very accents, and the voice and words returned, as if syllabled by haunting spirits. How dismal this desertion seemed. She stood up and moved about the little circle along the edge of the parapet, and looked over upon the lights beneath. The fire-flies sprang hither and thither in the trees and gloom, shouts came up from the Kingsbridge road, and out over the Hudson, somewhere beyond the dark wall of the Palisades, a shot was heard. She swayed perceptibly, leaning on the stone railing and Amato caught her tenderly.

In an instant the weakness passed, and she thanked him. His love sprang to his lips. In the half darkness, for the young moon, trembling in a vaporous sky, afforded only a transient light, she appeared to him more beautiful than ever, and his own burning heart beat impetuously. It seemed an enormity of carelessness not now to say one little word! Hitherto he had been utterly impassive, in speech at least, and his restraint had done him service in the estimation of the proud girl. She had found him helpful, necessary, and companionable.And he wondered—was this faintness in Helen Lorimer, some feminine tell-tale yielding? He drew back almost giddy with the sharp joy of the intoxicating thought. Ah! he knew so little about women; that clouded past, its isolation, amid books and students with a perverted intention! It thrilled him with wonder that he had escaped it; he knew now, past peradventure, that if he had surrendered to the Cardinal’s design, and this woman had crossed his path, the war within him would have slain him. Did she wish him to speak?

“Are you ill Miss Lorimer? Perhaps a little wine—?”

“Oh no,” she answered, “it is the heat of the day. It is high time we were away!”

“Where is Stowe?” he queried, as they sat down upon the stone bench, while over their heads whisking strips of light, from some searchlight in the harbor, flitted right and left, and made her more visible to him. Her hands were clasped to her throat and tears were in her eyes.

“Oh I beg you Miss Lorimer, tell me if I can help you. To see you unhappy hurts me so deeply,” he whispered.
