HOLBROOK'S DRAMATIC READER.

TO THE CHILDRENThese little plays--well-known stories done into dialogue--were written for children who like to imagine themselves living with their favorite characters in forest, in palace, or in fairyland.

It is hoped that you will enjoy these old friends in their new dress almost as well as you loved them in the old. When you read the words of bird or tree or prince or child, try to speak with the voice and manner which you think that character would use. Thus you will make the reading a joy to yourselves and a great satisfaction to your hearers.

To try to put oneself in the place of another is very good training for the imagination. It also teaches us to be more kind to others and to all living creatures. We learn that most persons are striving to do better and to be better, and we grow in understanding and sympathy.

May these little plays help you to the enjoyment of the great dramas which you will read when you are older.

LITTLE RED RIDING-HOOD

PERSONS IN THE PLAY--LITTLE RED RIDING-HOOD, MOTHER, BIRD, WOLF, MILLER, GRANDMOTHER

<b>SCENE I.--_At Red Riding-Hood's Home</b>

<i>Mother.</i> Would you like to go to grandmother's to-day, my child? The sun is bright and the air is warm and pleasant.

<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> Yes, mother, you know I always like to visit dear grandmamma.

<i>Mother.</i> Then you may go. You may carry your little basket, and I'll put some honey and a jar of butter in it for grandma.

<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> Oh, that will be a nice present for her! And may I take her some flowers?

<i>Mother.</i> Yes, dear child. Gather some of those you like best.<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> Here they are, mother--roses and pansies! Aren't they pretty?

<i>Mother.</i> Very pretty and sweet. Now put on your little red cloak and take the basket. Be very careful as you pass through the wood, and go directly to grandma's house.

<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> Yes, dear mother. Nothing will harm me. All the birds and animals love me and I love them.

<i>Mother.</i> Good-by, little daughter. Give me a kiss and take my love to dear grandmother.

<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> Good-by, mamma: good-by!<b>SCENE II.--_In the Wood</b>

<i>Little Red Riding-Hood (singing).</i>

Good morning, merry sunshine,
How did you come so soon?
You chase the little stars away
And shine away the moon.
I saw you go to sleep last night
Before I ceased my playing.
How did you get 'way over there,
And where have you been staying?

How pretty it is here in the wood! Oh, what a lovely bed of moss! You must come with me, pretty green moss, to grandma's house. Good morning, pretty bird: will you sing to me this morning?

<i>Bird.</i> Yes, little Red Riding-Hood. I will sing to you because you love all the birds and can understand my song. Soon I'll show you my little birds who are just big enough to fly.

<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> Thank you, dear bird, I shall be glad to see the cunning little things. But now I must hurry to grandmother's with the butter and the honey. Good-by!

<i>Bird.</i> Good-by, little friend! Chirp, chirp; chirp, chirp!<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> Now the little bird has flown away. I must put this moss in my basket and then hurry along--

<i>Wolf.</i> Ugh, ugh!

<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> Oh! how you frightened me, Mister Wolf! Where did you come from?

<i>Wolf.</i> From my pretty cave, far, far in the dark wood, little girl. What is your name?

<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> Why, don't you know me? I'm little Red Riding-Hood.

<i>Wolf.</i> I'm a stranger in this place, little girl; but I shall know you the next time I see you--ugh, ugh! What have you in your pretty basket, little Red Riding-Hood? It smells like honey.

<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> It <i>is</i> honey, Mr. Wolf. I am taking it to my dear grandmother.

<i>Wolf.</i> Are you all alone in the wood, my child? Isn't your mother with you? Aren't you afraid?

<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> Afraid? no, indeed! Why should I be afraid? All the animals are my friends.<i>Wolf.</i> Oh, yes, of course they are all your friends! But is it far to your grandmother's house?

<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> No, Mr. Wolf, only about half a mile. You go down this path to the mill and then turn to the right, and the first house you come to is my grandmother's. It's a little red house.

<i>Wolf.</i> Oh, that is very easy to find! But I know a shorter way through the wood. Let us run a race and see who will get there first.

<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> All right, Mr. Wolf. Good-by!

<i>Wolf.</i> Ugh, ugh; good-by!

<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> How fast he runs! I know he will win the race. How surprised dear grandma will be when Mr. Wolf knocks at the door! Now I see the mill. I will sing the pretty mill song we learned in school the other day.

[<i>Begins to sing, then stops suddenly.</i>]

Oh, there is the miller. Good morning, Mr. Miller! Have you seen Mr. Wolf go by?

<i>Miller.</i> No, little Red Riding-Hood. Have you seen a wolf in the wood?<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> Yes, Mr. Miller, and he said he would race with me to my grandmother's house.

<i>Miller.</i> My dear child, I will call the men who are chopping trees in the forest and they will catch Mr. Wolf. He is no friend of ours, and you must not talk with him, for he is cruel and will do you harm.

<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> Will he? Then I will never say another word to him. But I must hurry on to dear grandmother's.<b>SCENE III.--_Grandmother's House</b>

<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> Here I am at the door; I will knock. May I come in, dear grandmother?

<i>Wolf</i> (<i>in the house</i>). Open the latch and walk in.

<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> Here I am, dear grandmother! I am so glad the bad wolf did not get here first. Are you so sick you must stay in bed? See the nice butter and honey that mother sent you. And see the pretty flowers I've brought you.

<i>Wolf.</i> Thank you, my child.

<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> How rough your voice is, grandmother!

<i>Wolf.</i> That's because I've such a bad cold.

<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> But how bright your eyes are, grandmother!

<i>Wolf.</i> The better to see you, my child.

<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> How long your arms are, grandmother!

<i>Wolf.</i> The better to hold you, my child.<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> And how big your teeth are, grandmother!

<i>Wolf.</i> The better to eat you--ugh! ugh!

[<i>The miller and the wood choppers rush in.</i>]

<i>Mr. Miller.</i> Here's an end to you, Mr. Wolf! These men with their axes will stop your cruel deeds.

[<i>The wolf runs out, followed by the men.</i>]

Come, little Red Riding-Hood, don't be afraid. The wolf can't harm you now. Here is your grandmother, who has just come home from the village. She will take care of you.

<i>Little Red Riding-Hood.</i> Dear grandmother! I thought that the wolf was you.

<i>Grandmother.</i> Darling little Red Riding-Hood! How glad I am that you are safe. Now you must stay with me till your mother comes, and we will tell her how the brave men saved you and me from the hungry wolf. Won't she be glad to see her little Red Riding-Hood again?

GOLDILOCKS, OR THE THREE BEARS

PERSONS IN THE PLAY--GOLDILOCKS, THE DOLLIE, FATHER BEAR, MOTHER BEAR, BABY BEAR<b>SCENE I.--_Goldilocks in the Garden with her Doll</b>

<i>Goldilocks.</i> O dear! I do wish mother would come home. I am going to meet her. She told me not to go out of the garden lest I should get lost; but if I keep in the road, I <i>can't</i> get lost! Come, Dollie, you and I will go just a little way to meet mamma.

How warm it is in the sunshine! I think we shall go into the shady wood a little while. Let us pick some of these pretty flowers to make a wreath--won't mother be surprised when I show her all these flowers. Here is a lovely red one; and here's another like a daisy.

How dark it is here! I cannot see the road. I wonder if I'm lost! O mamma, mamma! I'm afraid. Dear Dollie, I'm glad you are with me.

<i>Dollie.</i> But I'm afraid, too!

<i>Goldilocks.</i> Please, dear Dollie, don't be afraid. Why, there's nothing to be afraid of--oh!

<i>Dollie.</i> What is the matter, Goldilocks?

<i>Goldilocks.</i> Look, what is that?

<i>Dollie.</i> I don't see anything.<i>Goldilocks.</i> I thought I saw a bear.

<i>Dollie.</i> Well, I hope not. I don't like bears.

<i>Goldilocks.</i> But there is a little house. Isn't it a funny little house? I wonder who lives there!

<i>Dollie.</i> Dear Goldilocks, please, don't you think we'd better go home? I don't like strange little houses in the wood.

<i>Goldilocks.</i> Perhaps a kind fairy lives there who will show us the way home.

<i>Dollie.</i> Yes, or perhaps she is the Gingerbread Witch who will turn us into gingerbread for her supper!

<i>Goldilocks.</i> Don't say such uncomfortable things, Dollie. She couldn't turn you into gingerbread, anyway.

<i>Dollie.</i> Well, I know I'm made of sawdust, but she might make mush of me for breakfast!

<i>Goldilocks.</i> I know you're fooling now, dear Dollie. Let's look in the window. I don't see anyone. I'll knock at the door. No one answers. Come, Dollie, we'll open the door and walk in. How nice and warm it is. There is a good fire in the kitchen stove.<i>Dollie.</i> Yes, and I smell something good to eat.

<i>Goldilocks.</i> Here it is on the table--what pretty bowls--one, two, three! I'll taste the porridge in the big bowl first. O Dollie, it is too hot! I burned my mouth.

<i>Dollie.</i> Try the next bowl. Perhaps the porridge in the middle-sized bowl is not so hot.

<i>Goldilocks.</i> No, indeed, it isn't; but it is too cold.

<i>Dollie.</i> Aren't you hard to please? I'm so hungry I could eat anything.

<i>Goldilocks.</i> Now this in the little bowl is just right. Sit down, Dollie, and we'll eat it all up.

<i>Dollie.</i> Do you think it is very polite for us to eat it all?

<i>Goldilocks.</i> You should have spoken of that before. It is too late now when it is all gone. Come, let us go into the parlor.

<i>Dollie.</i> Don't you think we'd better go home?

<i>Goldilocks.</i> How can we when I don't know the way? I'm tired, and I think I'll rest awhile in this nice big rocking-chair. But it's too high; I can't get into it.<i>Dollie.</i> Don't move it out of its place.

<i>Goldilocks.</i> Never mind! I'll try the middle-sized chair. I don't like this, it is too low.

<i>Dollie.</i> Well, Goldilocks, you must not put chairs out of their places!

<i>Goldilocks.</i> Oh, it won't hurt them. Now let us try this pretty little chair. Come, Dollie, I'll sing you a song:

Rock-a-bye, Dollie, in the treetop,
When the wind blows, the cradle will rock;
When the bough breaks, the cradle will fall
And down will come Dollie, cradle and all!

[<i>Chair breaks.</i>]

<i>Dollie.</i> Well, something broke then!

<i>Goldilocks.</i> Yes, the cradle and all came down that time. Dear, O dear! I wish I hadn't rocked you so hard. I wish I hadn't run away! [<i>Crying.</i>]

<i>Dollie.</i> Don't cry, dear Goldilocks. Let us see what we can find in the next room. Perhaps some one is in there who will take us to your dear mother.<i>Goldilocks.</i> O Dollie! I'm a naughty girl not to mind my mother. If I'd only stayed at home in the garden!

<i>Dollie.</i> Oh, see the big bed!

<i>Goldilocks.</i> I'm so tired I believe I'll climb in and go to sleep. But I don't like it. This big bed is too hard.

<i>Dollie.</i> And this middle-sized one is too soft.

<i>Goldilocks.</i> But this little one is <i>just right</i>.
Go--to--sleep--Dollie--<b>SCENE II.--_The Bear Family in the Wood</b>

<i>Father Bear.</i> Well, little son, aren't you about ready to go home?

<i>Sonny Bear.</i> Oh, no, father! Let me play just a little longer. Here are such good places to hide in the shady wood.

<i>Mother Bear.</i> No, dear little sonny, we must go home now. It is getting late. It's time for you to have your supper and go to bed.

<i>Sonny Bear.</i> All right, mother dear. I believe I am hungry, and your porridge is always so good.

<i>Mother Bear.</i> Most children like porridge. Perhaps you can have a nice red apple, too.

<i>Sonny Bear.</i> Oh, goody! Little sonny bears always like apples, don't they, papa?

<i>Father Bear.</i> Yes, my dear. Mother, let me take your knitting basket. What are you making now?

<i>Mother Bear.</i> A warm cap for sonny. Isn't it pretty?

<i>Father Bear.</i> Very pretty, and he should be very glad he has such a good mother.<i>Sonny Bear.</i> She <i>is</i> a good mother, and you are a very good father, too.

<i>Father Bear.</i> Well, here we are at home again. But the door is open. I'm certain I closed it when we went away. Who has been here?

<i>Mother Bear.</i> Let us take off our wraps and have our tea.

<i>Father Bear.</i> Why, somebody has been tasting my porridge.

<i>Mother Bear.</i> What? Let me see! Some one has left a spoon in my porridge, too.

<i>Sonny Bear.</i> Oh, mamma! Look at my bowl! Some one has eaten my porridge all up.

<i>Mother Bear.</i> Never mind, sonny boy, you may have some of mine. But I wonder who has been here. Let us go into the parlor and see if anyone is there.

<i>Father Bear</i>. Who's been moving my chair?

<i>Mother Bear.</i> Some one has been sitting in my chair!

<i>Sonny Bear.</i> Look, mother! Some one has been rocking in my chair and broken it all to pieces! O dear! my nice little chair!<i>Father Bear.</i> Never mind, Sonny Bear; don't cry. I'll buy you another chair at Mr. Wolf's store to-morrow.

<i>Mother Bear.</i> And now it is time for us to go to bed. Our little son is tired and sleepy.

<i>Father Bear.</i> I'll carry him up stairs. Come, sonny, there you are up on my shoulder.

Ride a cock horse to Banbury Cross
To see an old woman ride on a white horse.
With rings on her fingers and bells on her toes,
She shall have music wherever she goes!

Well, who's been in my bed, I'd like to know?

<i>Mother Bear.</i> Why, look at my bed. Some one has been lying on my bed!

<i>Sonny Bear.</i> Come quick, Mother! Father, come! Some one is in my bed.

<i>Goldilocks</i> (<i>waking and frightened</i>). Oh, see the three Bears. Come, Dollie, let us jump out of the window. [<i>Runs away.</i>]

<i>Mother Bear.</i> The little girl has gone, dear. Now you must go to sleep.

THE BIRD WITH THE BROKEN WINGPERSONS IN THE PLAY--THE BIRD, THE OAK TREE, THE MAPLE, THE WILLOW, THE SPRUCE, THE PINE, THE JUNIPER, THE FOREST FAIRY, JACK FROST

<b>SCENE I.--_In the Woods</b>

<i>The Oak.</i> See that flock of birds coming! The winter is near and they are flying south.

<i>The Maple.</i> I hope they will not light on my branches; I like to keep my leaves in order.

<i>The Willow.</i> So many birds will break my tender twigs. I am sure I do not want them either. Here they come!

[<i>The birds fly over the trees.</i>]

<i>Little Bird.</i> Oh, I can fly no farther! My wing is broken and I cannot hold it up. I am so tired and cold and hungry! I must rest to-night in this forest. I am sure some big strong tree will give me a resting place. I will ask this tall Oak, he looks so strong and his leaves are so thick and warm! May I rest in your branches to-night, great Oak Tree? I am a poor little bird with a broken wing and I am cold and tired and hungry.

<i>The Oak.</i> I am sorry; but my branches are all engaged by the squirrels, who are getting their acorns in for the winter. I have no room for strange birds.<i>Little Bird.</i> Oh! I am so lonely, so tired! Surely the handsome Maple Tree will take me in. She has no acorns and so the squirrels will not be in her branches. Kind, lovely Maple Tree, may I rest to-night in your branches? I am a poor little bird with a broken wing. I will not harm your pretty leaves.

<i>The Maple.</i> My leaves tremble to think of taking in strange birds! My house is in perfect order and I cannot think of disturbing it. Please go away!

<i>Little Bird.</i> Oh, what shall I do? The Oak and the Maple are so unkind and I am shivering with cold and weak with hunger. Surely <i>some</i> tree must be kind. Dear Willow, you are kind, are you not? Will you take me upon your graceful branches just for to-night?

<i>The Willow.</i> Really, Mr. Bird with the broken wing, I think you should have gone on with the other birds. I cannot take you in. I do not know your name or anything about you. Besides, I am very sleepy, and so, good night!

<i>Little Bird.</i> Oh, my dear bird friends, how I wish some of you were here! I shall perish with the cold if I must stay on the ground. Where can I go? The Oak, the Maple, and the Willow have all turned me away and the night is coming on.

<i>The Spruce.</i> Dear little bird with the broken wing, come to me! Can you hop up into my branches if I hold them down to you? See, here I am! I am not so handsome as the Maple tree, but my leaves grow thick and I'll try to keep you warm through the night. Come!<i>Little Bird.</i> Dear Spruce tree, how kind you are! I did not see you at first. Yes, here I am, on your lowest branch. How cosy and warm I feel. Oh, you are so good, and I was so tired and cold. Here I'll rest. I wish I could ever thank you enough for your goodness.

<i>The Spruce.</i> Do not speak of that, dear little bird; I am ashamed of the proud, selfish trees that would not shelter you. Should we not all be kind and helpful to one another?

<i>The Pine.</i> Well said, sister Spruce. And I will do my best to help you. I am not so strong as the Oak tree, little bird, but I will stand between you and the cold north wind. Rest warm and safe in the branches of the kind Spruce tree.

<i>Little Bird.</i> I thank you, tall Pine tree, for your kindness. You are a good brother of the Spruce and I shall rest well while you are both taking care of me.

<i>The Juniper.</i> I cannot keep the strong north wind from you, little bird with the broken wing, but if you are hungry, you may eat of my berries. Perhaps then you will rest better.

<i>Little Bird.</i> Thank you, dear Juniper tree. Why are you all so kind to me? Your berries are good, and now I am cold and hungry no longer. I'll go to sleep. Good night, dear trees!<i>Trees.</i> Good night, little bird, and may you have sweet dreams!

<b>SCENE II.--_Midnight in the Forest</b>

<i>Jack Frost.</i> Here I am in the great forest. How I dislike to touch all these beautiful leaves; yet I must obey the orders of King Winter. Here comes the Forest Fairy. Do you know why I have come, dear Fairy of the Forest?

<i>Forest Fairy.</i> Yes, Mr. Frost. I know that you must touch all the leaves, turning them into brilliant hues of gold and crimson and brown. I dislike to have them go, and yet you and I must obey the commands of King Winter. But,--

<i>Jack Frost.</i> But what, dear Fairy? You speak as if you had some wish to make--what is it?

<i>Forest Fairy.</i> I must tell you. Such a dear little bird came to the forest this evening. He had a broken wing, and he was cold and very tired. He asked shelter from the great Oak, the proud Maple, and the graceful Willow,--and all refused. I was so ashamed of my trees!

<i>Jack Frost.</i> What! did all the trees refuse to help a poor, tired little bird?

<i>Forest Fairy.</i> Listen! just as I was intending to speak to the trees, I heard the Spruce tell him to come to her branches and she would give him shelter.Then the Pine tree offered to keep the north wind from him, and the Juniper gave him her berries to eat. Could you, dear Jack Frost--

<i>Jack Frost.</i> Yes, yes, I know what you would ask. Such kindness as this should meet with some reward. The leaves of the proud Oak, the Maple, and the Willow shall fall to the ground when the cold of winter comes; but the Spruce, the Pine, the Juniper, and all their family shall keep their leaves and they shall be green all through the year. They shall be called the Evergreen Trees.

CORNELIA AND HER JEWELS

PERSONS IN THE PLAY--CORNELIA, NYDIA THE MAID, JULIA, ELDER SON, YOUNGER SON<b>SCENE.--_Home of Cornelia</b>

<i>Nydia.</i> Madam, the lady Julia waits to salute you.

<i>Cornelia.</i> Bid her enter, I pray. It is not fitting to have her wait.

<i>Nydia.</i> She is at the door, gracious madam.

<i>Cornelia.</i> Welcome, thrice welcome, fair Julia.

[<i>Nydia carries Julia's casket.</i>]

<i>Julia.</i> Thanks, dear Cornelia, for your kind greeting. May you and all your household have peace and joy.

<i>Cornelia.</i> And may those blessings be yours also, dear Julia. But tell me, what treasures have you in that charming casket?

<i>Julia.</i> A few poor jewels, fair friend. Bring me the casket, Nydia. These are some presents my parents and husband have given me.

<i>Cornelia.</i> I am so glad you have brought them to show me. You are very kind, for you know I greatly admire beautiful jewels.

<i>Julia.</i> See, here is a pearl necklace.<i>Cornelia.</i> How lovely! Let me clasp it about your neck. It is very becoming. And what other gems have you?

<i>Julia.</i> Here is a girdle my mother gave me for a wedding present. Isn't it pretty?

<i>Cornelia.</i> Pretty! my dear, it is exquisite! Your mother showed much good taste when she chose this for you.

<i>Julia.</i> And here are some rings from the far East. See these emeralds and rubies; how they flash in the sunlight!

<i>Cornelia.</i> How well they look on your white hands! But I see something else.

<i>Julia.</i> Yes, this is my handsomest jewel, a diamond bracelet. This I like best of all.

<i>Cornelia.</i> They are all lovely, my dear friend, and I am glad you have such beautiful things.

<i>Julia.</i> But, dear Cornelia, where are your jewels? All Rome knows how rich your famous father, Scipio, was, and surely he gave you many handsome ornaments. Please show them to me.

<i>Cornelia.</i> Oh, no, dear friend. But hark! I think I hear my sons. Nydia, tell them I wish to see them.<i>Nydia.</i> Here are the children, madam.

<i>The Boys</i> (<i>running in</i>). Dear mother! darling mother!

<i>Cornelia.</i> Tell me, my Caius, what did the pedagogue teach you to-day?

<i>Caius.</i> O mother! It was wonderful! He told us how Horatius kept the bridge in the brave days of old. Wasn't that a great and noble deed, mother mine?

<i>Cornelia.</i> Yes, my darling. And you, my Tiberius, have you been pleased with your lessons?

<i>Tiberius.</i> Mother, how you must honor our grandfather, the noble Scipio! Our teacher told the boys of his great campaigns in Africa and how the Senate called him Africanus after the war was over.

<i>Cornelia.</i> Yes, my son, such work and such lives are lessons worthy of study. They teach the young how they too may live and die for their beloved country.

<i>Caius.</i> I shall try to be a brave man some day, too, dear mother.

<i>Tiberius.</i> And I, mother, shall try to be worthy of our noble family.

<i>Cornelia.</i> My dear, noble boys! Julia, these are my jewels.<i>Julia.</i> How you shame my vanity, noble Cornelia! What are all the precious stones in the world compared with these noble boys! Daughter of the famous Scipio, the world will remember you through the great deeds of your sons, and all mankind will honor you as CORNELIA, MOTHER OF THE GRACCHI.

CINDERELLA

PERSONS IN THE PLAY--CINDERELLA, MOTHER, FATHER, KATHERINE, ELIZABETH, FAIRY GODMOTHER, PRINCE, HERALD<b>SCENE I.--_Cinderella's Home</b>

<i>Mother.</i> I am so glad we are all invited to the ball at the Prince's palace. You know, my dear, that it will be a great pleasure for our girls.

<i>Father.</i> Yes; and I suppose you will all have to buy new ball dresses.

<i>Katherine.</i> O mamma! isn't it lovely! May I have a blue silk dress?

<i>Elizabeth.</i> And may I have pink, dear mother? And shall we get them to-day?

<i>Mother.</i> Yes, my child; and you may both go with me to buy your dresses and slippers.

<i>Cinderella.</i> Dear papa, may I go to the ball at the Prince's palace?

<i>Father.</i> You, my child! Aren't you too young for parties? Ask your mother.

<i>Cinderella.</i> May I go to the ball, mother?

<i>Mother.</i> Nonsense, child! what are you thinking of? A ball is no place for a child like you. You are better off at home by the kitchen fire.

<i>Cinderella.</i> But I'm fourteen. Sister Katherine, won't you coax mamma to let me go?<i>Katherine.</i> No, indeed, I'll not! What would you do at a ball? a silly thing like you!

<i>Elizabeth.</i> Don't be a goose. Wait till you're older and better looking. There's no room in the carriage for you, and you are too young, anyway.

<i>Mother.</i> Come, girls, it is time for us to go down town to buy our new gowns. Cinderella, go to your lessons. Don't think any more about the ball. You can't go, and so that's the end of it.<b>SCENE II.--_Cinderella's Home</b>

<i>Father.</i> Come, girls! aren't you ready yet? Is your mother coming?

<i>Katherine.</i> Yes, father, in just a minute.

<i>Mother.</i> Here we are, dear. Don't the girls look sweet?

<i>Father.</i> Yes, yes! but, come on, for we are late now.

<i>Mother.</i> Good night, Cinderella. Be a good girl and go to bed at nine o'clock.

[<i>All go out, leaving Cinderella alone.</i>]

<i>Cinderella.</i> Good-by!--Now they have gone and I am all alone. Oh, why couldn't I go, too! How pretty they all looked! I would not take up much room, and I don't like to be left here by myself when they are having such a good time. Oh, dear! I believe I'm going to cry, but I can't help it. [<i>Cries.</i>]

[<i>Enter fairy godmother.</i>]

<i>Fairy Godmother.</i> Why are you crying, Cinderella?

<i>Cinderella.</i> Who is that? I thought I heard some one speaking to me, but I can't see anybody.<i>Fairy Godmother.</i> What is the matter, Cinderella?

<i>Cinderella.</i> Oh, <i>lovely</i> lady! who are you?

<i>Fairy Godmother.</i> I am your fairy godmother, my child, and I wish to know why you are crying.

<i>Cinderella.</i> Oh, dear! I'm crying because they have all gone to the ball; and I wanted to go, too, and they wouldn't take me!

<i>Fairy Godmother.</i> Never mind, my dear. Stop crying, and I will let you go.

<i>Cinderella.</i> Oh, dear fairy godmamma! will you, really? But how <i>can</i> I go in this old dress?

<i>Fairy Godmother.</i> You'll see. Tell me, Cinderella, have you a big yellow pumpkin in the kitchen garden?

<i>Cinderella.</i> Yes, I think so. I saw one there yesterday.

<i>Fairy Godmother.</i> Go, get it for me.

<i>Cinderella</i> (<i>runs out, and returns with the pumpkin</i>). I've found it! Here it is!<i>Fairy Godmother.</i> Yes, that is a fine pumpkin. I'll touch it with my wand. What is it now?

[<i>The pumpkin is changed to a carriage.</i>]

<i>Cinderella.</i> Oh! oh! how lovely! Such a beautiful, big, yellow coach! Why, it is much finer than papa's black carriage.

<i>Fairy Godmother.</i> I am glad you like your coach. Now do you think there are any rats in your rat trap?

<i>Cinderella.</i> I'll go see. Yes, here is the trap with two big rats in it. What long tails they have!

<i>Fairy Godmother.</i> Wait till I touch them with my fairy wand. Now what do you see?

<i>Cinderella.</i> Oh, dear godmother! what a wonderful wand to change rats into great handsome horses with long manes and tails! You dear horses! I'll get you some sugar to eat.

<i>Fairy Godmother.</i> Don't stop to pet them now, but fetch me the mousetrap.

<i>Cinderella.</i> Here it is with two cunning little mice in it. What will you do with them?<i>Fairy Godmother.</i> Touch them with my fairy wand and turn them into a coachman and a footman. See, the coachman is on the box with the reins in his hand, and the footman holds the door open for you. Will you step in, Cinderella?

<i>Cinderella.</i> In <i>these</i> clothes, dear godmother?

<i>Fairy Godmother</i> (<i>laughing</i>). That wouldn't be nice, would it? Well, let us see what my wand can do for you. Now look in the glass and tell me what you see there.

<i>Cinderella.</i> Oh, what a pretty lady! Why, I do believe she is myself! What a beautiful dress! And look, dear godmother! see my pretty glass slippers!

<i>Fairy Godmother.</i> Yes, my dear, you are all ready for the Prince's ball. I want you to have a happy time, but remember this. You must start for home when the clock strikes twelve or your pretty clothes will change, your coach will turn into a pumpkin, your horses to rats, and you will have to walk home.

<i>Cinderella.</i> I'll remember, dear godmother, and run away on the first stroke. Thank you so much! Good-by!

[<i>Enters the coach and is driven away.</i>]<b>SCENE III.--_The Prince's Palace</b>

<i>Cinderella.</i> Here I am at the palace. Please announce me as the Lady from Far Away.

<i>Herald.</i> The Lady from Far Away!

<i>Prince.</i> What a lovely lady! she must be a princess. Tell me, fair lady, are you a princess from the land of flowers?

<i>Cinderella.</i> I am not a princess, sir, but only a girl from the land of happy thoughts.

<i>Prince.</i> You say well, fair lady, for no one can look upon you without thoughts of love and joy.

<i>Cinderella.</i> And you, great Prince, have thoughts of great and noble deeds, have you not?

<i>Prince.</i> Yes, I have thoughts of great deeds, of brave men and fair ladies, of games and victories,--but now I have forgotten all but you.

<i>Cinderella.</i> Will you remember me to-morrow or shall I fade away like the dreams of night?<i>Prince.</i> No dreams could be fairer, but I hope you will not vanish as they do. If you do, I am quite sure that I shall find you!

<i>Cinderella.</i> Don't be too sure, for I am not what I seem. I am a princess only in your thoughts; really I am--

<i>Prince.</i> What? a flower, a star, a goddess?

<i>Cinderella.</i> No, only a woman--

<i>Prince.</i> The best of all, a woman! And now will the dream-woman dance with me?

<i>Cinderella.</i> With pleasure; what lovely music!--and so many pretty women. What beautiful rooms!

[<i>Cinderella, the Prince, her father, mother, sisters, and two gentlemen dance the minuet.</i>]

<i>Prince.</i> Will you not tell me your name and where you live?

<i>Cinderella.</i> Both are a secret.

<i>Prince.</i> It makes no difference to me, for I know you, and that is enough.

<i>Cinderella.</i> I hear the clock! What hour is it striking?<i>Prince.</i> Twelve--but that is early. You need not go?

<i>Cinderella.</i> Yes, I must, and quietly. Do not try to keep me, Prince--good night!

<i>Prince.</i> She is gone! and I do not know where she lives. How can I find her? I'll give another ball and hope she will come again.

[<i>All go out.</i>]<b>SCENE IV.--_Cinderella's Home</b>

<i>Father.</i> Well, girlies, did you have a pleasant time at the ball?

<i>Katherine.</i> Oh, yes, papa, splendid! But did you see the lovely princess that came so late?

<i>Elizabeth.</i> She was the prettiest girl there. I wonder who she is!

<i>Mother.</i> So do I. It seems to me I've seen her somewhere. Perhaps I've met her in my travels; but I can't remember where it was.

<i>Father.</i> What is her name?

<i>Katherine.</i> I heard some one say she was Lady Far Away. But that's not a real name.

<i>Elizabeth.</i> Perhaps she is a princess in disguise.

<i>Cinderella.</i> Tell me, sister, how this princess looked.

<i>Elizabeth.</i> Oh! she is lovely! Golden curls and blue eyes and such a sweet smile!

<i>Katherine.</i> She wore a beautiful dress that shone like the moonlight.<i>Elizabeth.</i> Did you notice her pretty slippers? They looked like crystal.

<i>Mother.</i> The Prince danced with her all the time.

<i>Father.</i> Why, here comes the Prince's herald. I'll see what he wants. Here is a note. It is an invitation to go to the Prince's palace again to-night. Do you all want to go?

<i>All.</i> Yes, yes, father, please!

<i>Father.</i> All right, we'll go!

<i>Cinderella.</i> Can't I go this time, mamma?

<i>Mother.</i> No, my dear. When you are a little older you can go, but not now.<b>SCENE V.--_At the Palace</b>

<i>Prince.</i> I wonder if my fairy princess will come to-night. I've been looking for her for more than an hour. Oh, here she is! Dear lady, I've been hoping you would come.

<i>Cinderella.</i> So you have not forgotten me?

<i>Prince.</i> No, and never shall. Will you go with me to see the flowers?

<i>Cinderella.</i> What lovely flowers! This is certainly the home of the flower fairies. See the roses nodding at us. They almost ask us to love them.

<i>Prince.</i> May I give you this dainty pink one? It is the color of your cheeks.

<i>Cinderella.</i> Remember I am from the land of Far Away and I must vanish at midnight.

<i>Prince.</i> Tell me where your father lives that I may call upon him.

<i>Cinderella.</i> Not now; but sometime I may tell you about my fairy godmother.

<i>Prince.</i> There! I knew you must be a sister of the fairies. Does your fairy godmother have a fairy wand?<i>Cinderella.</i> Yes, and she does wonderful things with it--but my father and mother do not know about her.

<i>Prince.</i> Of course not. Only very young people know about fairy godmothers. But we know, don't we?

<i>Cinderella.</i> Hark! I hear the chimes ringing. It must be twelve o'clock, and I must go.

<i>Prince.</i> Do not go, dear princess. Stay here in my palace, always.

<i>Cinderella.</i> The fairies are calling me and I am late. I must go. Perhaps I can come again sometime. Oh, I am afraid--

<i>Prince.</i> Afraid of what?

<i>Cinderella.</i> Good-by, good-by!

<i>Prince.</i> She's gone! What was she afraid of? I cannot see her! Who is that child running down the stairway? She must be one of the servants who has been watching the dancers. I wish I could see my princess. What is that shining thing on the stairs? She has lost one of her crystal slippers. Now I know how I shall find her. To-morrow I shall send a herald through the city to find the owner of this pretty little slipper.<b>SCENE VI.--_Cinderella's Home</b>

<i>Cinderella.</i> Mamma, mamma, here is a man on horseback who wants to see you.

<i>Mother.</i> What is your errand, sir?

<i>Herald.</i> I am sent by the great Prince of our country to find the owner of this slipper. He says he will marry no one but the lady who can wear this little crystal slipper.

<i>Mother.</i> I'll call my daughters. Katherine! Elizabeth! We were all at the ball at the Prince's palace. Katherine, is this your glass slipper? Try it on.

<i>Katherine.</i> Yes, mother. My, how small it is! I cannot get my foot in it!

<i>Elizabeth.</i> Perhaps it will fit me. My feet are smaller than yours. No, I cannot push my foot in, no matter how long I try. It must be a magic slipper.

<i>Cinderella.</i> May I try on the slipper?

<i>Mother.</i> My dear child, why should you try on the slipper? It belongs to the princess who went to the ball.

<i>Katherine.</i> And you were not at the ball, Cinderella!

<i>Elizabeth.</i> Your foot is too big for it, my dear little sister.<i>Herald.</i> Pardon me, ladies, but the orders of the Prince are that every lady, young or old, must try on the slipper, and when the owner is found she must go with me to the palace.

<i>Cinderella.</i> Give it to me, please. See how easily it slips on my foot--and here is the mate to the glass slipper in my pocket. Dear Mother, I am the fairy princess you saw at the ball.

<i>Mother.</i> You, my dear! and I did not know you!

<i>Herald.</i> Now, lady, please come with me to the Prince's palace. You shall be a princess.

<i>Cinderella.</i> Good-by, dear sisters! Good-by, dear mother! I am going to the Prince's palace.

THE PIED PIPER

PERSONS IN THE PLAY--MAYOR, FIRST COUNCILMAN, SECOND COUNCILMAN, THIRD COUNCILMAN, TEN CITIZENS, PIPER<b>SCENE I.--_The Mayor's Office</b>

<i>Mayor and Councilmen, sitting around a table.--Citizens come in.</i>

<i>First Citizen.</i> Our Mayor is a noddy!

<i>Second Citizen.</i> Look at our corporation sitting in the gowns we pay for, and doing nothing!

<i>Third Citizen.</i> See here, how the rats made a nest in my Sunday hat!

<i>Fourth Citizen.</i> When I was cooking dinner the bold rats licked the soup from my ladle!

<i>Fifth Citizen.</i> They are so bold they are always fighting with the dogs and cats!

<i>Sixth Citizen.</i> Yes, and they kill them, too!

<i>Seventh Citizen.</i> My baby cried in his sleep, and when I went to him there was a big rat in his cradle.

<i>Eighth Citizen.</i> What are you going to do about it, Mr. Mayor?

<i>Ninth Citizen.</i> You'd better wake up, sirs! Don't go to sleep over this!<i>Tenth Citizen.</i> I tell you, you'll have to do something to save us from this army of rats!

<i>First Councilman.</i> What <i>can</i> we do?

<i>Second Councilman.</i> I'm sure we've tried everything, but every day the rats grow worse and worse.

<i>Third Councilman.</i> I'm sure it isn't very pleasant for us to have the city overrun with the creatures!

<i>Mayor.</i> I'd sell my ermine gown for a guilder! It is no easy thing to be mayor and I wish I was a plowboy in the country! Try to think of something to do.

<i>First Councilman.</i> It is easy to bid us rack our own brains!

<i>Second Councilman.</i> I'm sure my head aches trying to think.

<i>Third Councilman.</i> I've wondered and thought, till I've no thoughts left.

<i>Mayor.</i> Oh! if I only had a great big trap! Yes, a thousand big traps! Bless us, what noise is that? Is it a rat?--Come in!

[<i>Enter Piper.</i>]<i>First Councilman.</i> Who is this who dares to come into the Mayor's office without an introduction?

<i>Second Councilman.</i> Hasn't he a funny coat?

<i>Third Councilman.</i> But what a pleasant face! He smiles all the time.

<i>Mayor.</i> He looks like the picture of my grandsire. What is your name, and your business, my man?

<i>Pied Piper.</i> Please your honors, my name is Pied Piper. My business is to play upon my pipe. I can charm with the magic of my notes all things to do my will. But I use my charm on creatures that do people harm, the toad, the mole, and the viper, and rats--rats!

<i>Mayor.</i> Rats! Well, then, you're the man we want. We'll pay you a thousand guilders if you'll free our town of rats.

<i>Piper.</i> A thousand guilders! Done! It's a bargain!

SCENE II.--<i>Same as Scene I. The Mayor and Councilmen looking out of window</i>

<i>Mayor.</i> There he goes down the street.

<i>First Councilman.</i> What a strange looking pipe he plays!<i>Second Councilman.</i> I believe it must be a magic one.

<i>Third Councilman.</i> Do you hear the music? What is that other noise?

<i>Mayor.</i> Look, look at the rats! Did you ever see such a sight!

<i>First Councilman.</i> The streets are crowded with them! Big and little, brown, black, and gray, they are tumbling over each other in their hurry!

<i>Second Councilman.</i> Sir! he is going toward the bridge.

<i>Third Councilman.</i> They must think he is playing a tune of apples and cheese!

<i>Mayor.</i> There they are at the river. They are plunging in! they will be drowned!

<i>First Councilman.</i> Good for the piper!

<i>Mayor.</i> Ring the bells for the people. Tell them to get long poles, poke out the nests and block up the holes!

<i>Second Councilman.</i> Here comes the Piper.

<i>Third Councilman.</i> That was well done, Mr. Piper.

<i>Pied Piper.</i> Yes, all the rats are drowned and now I've come for my pay.<i>Mayor.</i> Pay! why what have you done? Just played a tune on your pipe. You must be joking.

<i>Piper.</i> You promised--

<i>First Councilman.</i> You impudent fellow! You certainly don't think a tune on your pipe is worth one thousand guilders? There is no work in that.

<i>Second Councilman.</i> The rats are dead and can't come to life again, I think!

<i>Mayor.</i> My friend, we are much obliged, of course. We are much obliged and will gladly give you fifty guilders. You know your time is not worth more.

<i>Piper.</i> No trifling, pray. I'll have what you promised, or you may find that I'll play a tune you do not like!

<i>Mayor.</i> What! do you threaten us, fellow? Do what you please. Do you think we care? Play on your old pipe whatever tune you wish.

<i>Piper.</i> Listen, then, and look from your window when I play again in the street below.

[<i>Goes out.</i>]

<i>Mayor.</i> What does the lazy fellow mean by his threats?<i>First Councilman.</i> Hear his wonderful music! Listen.

<i>Second Councilman.</i> Oh! what is he doing! See the children!

<i>Third Councilman.</i> They are following him. There is my son. Where are you going, my boy? Come back!

<i>Mayor.</i> Let me see! O woe! there are my own three lovely children. Run, some one, and stop them!

<i>Third Councilman.</i> I'll go; I'll go.

[<i>Runs out.</i>]

<i>Mayor.</i> It is useless. Every child in our city is following the magic sound.

<i>Second Councilman.</i> The music seems to say: "Come, children, to the wonderful land of play. There flowers and fruits will welcome you. The birds and beasts will play with you, and you will never be sad or sorry in the wonderful land of play." No wonder the children follow the Piper.

<i>Third Councilman</i> (<i>enters</i>). The children and the Piper have all disappeared! A mountain opened and let them in!

<i>First Councilman.</i> The children, the blessed children, have gone! What shall we do without the children?<i>Mayor.</i> Oh, wicked man that I am! Why did I break my promise? Why did I not give him the thousand guilders?

<i>Second Councilman.</i> Yes, we are all wicked men, and we are punished for not keeping our word.

<i>Mayor.</i> Let us write this sad story on a column so that all may read; and let us paint the picture of the Piper with our little ones following him, on a church window, so that all men may know how our children have been stolen away.

<i>First Councilman.</i> And may this sad story teach us all to keep our word with every one.

MOTHER GOOSE'S PARTY

PERSONS IN THE PLAY--MOTHER GOOSE, JACK GOOSE, MOTHER HUBBARD, DOG, A-DILLAR-A-DOLLAR, MARY (AND HER LAMB), OLD MRS. SHOEMAN, HER SONS (TOMMY TUCKER, JACKY HORNER), MISS MUFFET, BOY BLUE, BO-PEEP, NANCY ETTICOAT, LITTLE BOY WHO LIVES IN THE LANE, OLD KING COLE, MAN IN THE MOON, TOM THE PIPER'S SON, MISTRESS MARY<b>SCENE I.--_Home of Mother Goose</b>

<i>Mother Goose.</i> I really think I must give a party. All my friends have been so good to me and I have been entertained in so many homes! Wherever I go I am sure to see one of my Mother Goose books, and the children all seem to love it so much. Let me see! whom shall I invite? I think I'll ask Old Mother Hubbard to take tea with me and we'll talk about the party together. Jack, Jack!

<i>Jack</i> (<i>enters</i>). Yes, mother dear, what is it?

<i>Mother Goose.</i> Jack Goose, I wish you to run over to Mother Hubbard's house and ask her to take tea with me this afternoon. Now be nimble, Jack,--be quick!

<i>Jack.</i> Yes, mother dear. See me jump over the candlestick! Isn't that fine jumping?

<i>Mother Goose.</i> Very fine indeed, Jack. Now do your errand, and hurry home.

<i>Jack.</i> Yes, mother, I will. Good-by.

<i>Mother Goose.</i> Good-by.<b>SCENE II.--_House of Mother Hubbard</b>

<i>Jack</i> (<i>knocking</i>). I wonder if Old Mother Hubbard is at home. Hark! I hear her dog barking. Yes, and I hear her step. Here she is!

<i>Mother Hubbard</i> (<i>opening the door</i>). Who is this knocking so loud? Oh, it's you, little nimble Jack! Will you come in?

<i>Jack.</i> No, thank you, Mrs. Hubbard. My mother wishes you to come over to our house for tea this afternoon. Will you come?

<i>Mother Hubbard.</i> Yes, thank you, Jack, I will. Tell your mother that I'm just going to market to buy my poor doggie a bone.

<i>Jack.</i> O Mother Hubbard! <i>please</i> let me play with your dog. He's such a dear old doggie! Do you remember how he danced a jig the other day?

<i>Mother Hubbard.</i> Yes, Jack, I do; and I think you danced with him. You are both nimble young things and both like to dance. Well, good-by, now. Have a good time together and I'll bring you something little boys like.

<i>Jack.</i> Thank you! Good-by, good-by! Now, doggie, let's dance.

Old Mother Hubbard, she went to the cupboard,
  To get the poor doggie a bone;
But when she got there, the cupboard was bare,  And so the poor doggie had none.

<i>Dog</i> (<i>sadly</i>). Bow-wow, bow-wow, bow-wow!

<i>Jack.</i> Oh! you don't like that song! Never mind, old fellow! Mother Hubbard has gone to the butcher's and she'll get you a bone, I'm sure. Wait till she comes back.

<i>Dog</i> (<i>gayly</i>). Bow-wow, bow-wow, bow-wow!

<i>Jack.</i> I thought you would like that. Here she comes now. We've had a lovely dance, Mother Hubbard, and now I must hurry home.

<i>Mother Hubbard.</i> Thank you for staying and taking good care of my dog. Here are some fresh Banbury buns for you.

<i>Jack.</i> Oh, thank you, Mother Hubbard. I'm very fond of Banbury buns. Good-by!

<i>Mother Hubbard.</i> Good-by, Jack. Tell your mother I'll be over soon.

<i>Jack.</i> Bring your dog with you, and we'll have another dance. Good-by.

<i>Dog.</i> Bow-wow! bow-wow! bow-wow!<b>SCENE III.--_Mother Goose and Mother Hubbard at the Tea Table</b>

<i>Mother Goose.</i> I am pleased to see you, Mother Hubbard. I hear that your cupboard is no longer bare and empty, and I am very glad you are able to give your poor dog all the bones a good dog should have. Now for our tea. Shall I put two or three lumps in your cup?

<i>Mother Hubbard.</i> Three, please. I like my tea very sweet. And now tell me, Mother Goose, what is the reason you sent for me to-day?

<i>Mother Goose.</i> Well, I am going to give a party and I wish to ask your advice.

<i>Mother Hubbard.</i> Indeed! Whom do you think of inviting?

<i>Mother Goose.</i> First, the dear Old Woman who lives in the shoe--

<i>Mother Hubbard.</i> What! and all her children?

<i>Mother Goose.</i> No, only the two eldest. You know the party is for my son Jack, too, and we must have the young people as well as their parents. Old King Cole will come and bring his fiddlers three to play for the young folks who dance.

<i>Mother Hubbard.</i> I hope you won't invite Tom the Piper's Son, or My Son John as his mother calls him,--or Humpty-Dumpty.They are not good boys for your son Jack to play with!

<i>Mother Goose.</i> I suppose not; but I like them all, and I dislike to leave out anyone. I don't wish to hurt their feelings.

<i>Mother Hubbard.</i> There are little Bo-Peep and Boy Blue, who are good children, although rather silly; and there are little Miss Muffet and Nancy Etticoat, both very pretty little girls; and there are Jacky Horner and Tommy Tucker and the Man-in-the-Moon and Taffey and Daffey-Down-Dilly and--

<i>Mother Goose.</i> I'll have to give a garden party if I invite all those! I can't leave any out, and I think I'll have the party out-of-doors.

<i>Mother Hubbard.</i> That will be fine! I only hope it will be a pleasant day. When will you give it?

<i>Mother Goose.</i> Two weeks from to-day, the first of May.

<i>Mother Hubbard.</i> That's May Day and a very good day for a party out-of-doors. Well I must go home now. Good-by! If I can help you, please call upon me.

<i>Mother Goose.</i> Thank you, Mother Hubbard! Good-by, and thank you again for coming over.<b>SCENE IV.--_At the Party</b>

<i>Mother Hubbard.</i> What a lovely day you have for your party, Mother Goose! The sun shines so bright and warm, and the flowers are lovely. Is there anything I can do?

<i>Mother Goose.</i> No, thank you. I'm glad you came early. Have you seen the tables?

<i>Mother Hubbard.</i> They are lovely! Where did you get such pretty flowers?

<i>Mother Goose.</i> From Mistress Mary, quite contrary. You know she has a garden

With cockle shells, and silver bells,
And pretty maids all in a row.

<i>Mother Hubbard.</i> I see some one coming.

<i>Mother Goose.</i> Why, how do you do, A-Dillar-a-Dollar! Are you always in such good time?

<i>A-Dillar-a-Dollar.</i> I'm afraid not, Mrs. Goose. They call me

A ten o'clock scholar,
Why did you come so soon?
You used to come at ten o'clock,And now you come at noon!

<i>Mother Goose.</i> And here comes Mary with her little lamb. Do you like the lamb better than a Teddy Bear, Mary?

<i>Mary.</i> Yes, indeed, I do. Because the lamb loves me, you know.

It followed me to school one day,
  Which was against the rule;
It made the children laugh and play,
  To see the lamb at school.

<i>Mother Goose.</i> Here comes the Old Woman who lives in a shoe, and her two oldest boys. Dear Mrs. Shoe-woman, I am very glad to see you! How did you leave all of your children?

<i>Mrs. Shoe-woman.</i> Oh, dear, Mother Goose! I have so many children I don't know what to do: when they are naughty I give them some broth without any bread, and whip them all soundly and put them to bed.

<i>Little Two-Eyes.</i> Come, little goat, here is some green grass for you to eat. I wish that my sisters loved me and that my mother was not ashamed of me. Oh, why do I have two eyes just like all other people? I am so hungry, Oh, dear! Oh, dear! (<i>Cries.</i>)

THE WEEK--MONDAY, TUESDAY, WEDNESDAY, THURSDAY, FRIDAY, SATURDAY, SUNDAY

<i>Monday.</i> Well, I am glad to be here at last. Certainly my work is very important. As the first working day of the week, I begin all business; and I have always heard that if a thing is well begun, it is half done. People call me Moon-day--isn't that a pretty name, the day of the moon? How beautiful the moon is, riding in her silver chariot across the dark blue sky! I am proud of my name. The moon is constantly changing and I like change. I like brightness and cleanliness too, and good housewives wash their clothes on Monday. How white and clean they look hanging on the line! The sun and wind play hide and seek and help to cleanse the clothes. School begins on Monday and the little children run and laugh on their way to school. Every one seems happy that another week has begun.

<i>Tuesday.</i> I am named for Tui, the god of war. In the countries of the north I am greatly honored by all the people. Soldiers when going to war call on Tui for help, and they like to begin a battle on Tuesday. Monday likes to begin work, but I like to make some progress. The children always know their lessons better on Tuesday, and are happier than on Monday. The white clothes are sprinkled and rolled, and now the maids iron the pretty baby dresses and the house linen. They sing and laugh over their work. The world is all running smoothly on Tuesday, and I think I like my work the best.
