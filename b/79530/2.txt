They walked in the direction of Blackberry Hill, and for the first time in his life Tod felt the lure of houses. There was something very fascinating in poking about in those back streets, finding your house all empty and waiting, in unlocking its door and exploring all its possibilities and its secrets. Tod developed a keenness and an enthusiasm that surprised Mossup. They measured rooms, inspected rusty kitchen stoves, ferreted about in lugubrious back yards, took note of cupboards, coppers, sanitary arrangements and the invariable absence of a bath. Mossup was a serious and prematurely old young man. He would stand and meditate like a stork on one leg, his weak eyes glazed behind his spectacles, a tuft of flaxen hair standing up on his head like a plume. His ambitions were very modest. His romance ran to a rental of £20 a year, with the furniture for one bedroom and a sitting-room, and the stocking of a kitchen. There was something pathetic about his cautiousness, and the way he kept consulting a little notebook in which the adventure was tabulated to the last penny.

They were poor little houses, obvious little houses, cheap, shabby, and hopelessly ugly. All the windows looked at other houses of exactly the same type. One or two of them had a very grimed and greasy air. An occasional whiff of sewer gas sent Mossup sniffing into dark places like a solemn and suspicious dog. There seemed a lamentable absence of fresh whitewash everywhere, and Tod could not see any appetising corner where you would care to keep food. The larders and little sculleries were—well, you just said “Poof!” Yet the fascination of the quest gripped Tod. Mossup interested him. He was equally interested in the house in which Mossup might choose to live.“We have to be very careful—very careful,” said Mossup, jotting down notes in his book.

“I don’t like any of these houses.”

“Don’t you, Western?” and Mossup looked pained.

“They’re dirty and stuffy and ugly. Where’s the gardens, too? I don’t call any of those cat-walks gardens.”

“Can’t afford more than twenty pounds. You have to put up with things, sometimes.”

“I don’t see it,” said Tod. “Why can’t things be arranged differently. They could be, if somebody bothered.”

Tod spent two more evenings in house-hunting with Arthur Mossup. At last they found a little old house, in a blind lane off Paradise Row, one of those solid and unaffected little houses that had lived in the country until the splurge of the Shaston suburbs had swallowed it. It had a green latticed porch, green window frames, a tiled roof and a bit of garden. Mossup grew greatly excited. He wanted to rush down to the agents and take the place that very night.

“Somebody else may get it. Olive will just shout when she sees this cottage.”“Plenty of time,” said Tod, “go round first thing in the morning. Rather wish I was a house agent. Must be quite jolly finding homes for people.”

The idea fermented. It was working in Tod’s head when he strolled along to the bandstand on Wednesday evening in search of Mr. Burgetrode. He found him in a bath-chair by the parade railings, and looking as though he hated it. The bath-chair man was leaning over the railing, smoking a pipe.

“Smith,” said Mr. Burgetrode.

“Sir?”

“Go and get a drink; go and get two drinks, and come back in half an hour. Here’s the money.”

Tod perched himself on the railings.

“How is your foot, sir?”

“My foot doesn’t interest me, Western; let it alone. We’ll leave it with the doctor. I expected to see you before, young man.”

Tod looked down unabashed into Mr. Burgetrode’s fierce brown face.

“Sorry, sir; I’ve been house-hunting.”“What?”

“House-hunting with a friend who is getting married.”

“Why doesn’t he do something else! And did you find a house?”

“After three evenings at it,” said Tod; “we looked at all sorts of mouldy places where you wouldn’t expect to find anything but blackbeetles and cats. Houses are very interesting, sir, don’t you think?”

“Almost as interesting as men, Western, and not quite such fools. Houses are alive.”

“Yes, aren’t they?” said Tod. “I have begun to feel I should like to live with houses, know all about houses, put people into houses.”

Old Burgetrode gave a quick lift of his head.

“You don’t like your present place, then?”

“No,” said Tod.

“Why not?”

“They don’t want you to tell the truth.”“Ha!” said Mr. Burgetrode fiercely.

Tod edged himself nearer to the bath-chair. He had a feeling that he could talk to Mr. Burgetrode.

“They want you to lie about things. It was the same in the other shop I was in, and I got chucked for that—and something else.”

Mr. Burgetrode was watching him with great intentness.

“So you don’t like it?”

“I loathe it,” said Tod. “Why should you have to lie to people?”

“It means money, Western, more money.”

“Well, we ought to get rid of money. Or supposing there was so much money that it was cheap as the shingle down there, people wouldn’t tell lies to get it.”

“That’s a problem, young man. You seem to have discovered the trick of running your head against problems.”

The Shaston Corporation Orchestra who inhabited the bandstand, woke to sudden life after an interval of twenty minutes. Mr. Burgetrode grew impatient.

“Take me away from that noise, will you?”Tod descended from the railings and trundled the bath-chair in the direction of the Hotel Majestic.

“That will do,” said Mr. Burgetrode; “it will take my friend Smith another ten minutes to find us. Do you play any games, young Ajax?”

“Cricket,” said Tod, “and I played right-half for Shaston last winter. I can swim a mile, and box a bit.”

“Keep it up, Western. What did you want to be before you got trapped in a shop?”

“A doctor.”

Mr. Burgetrode did not seem prejudiced in favour of such a career. He gave Tod to understand that there were doctors and doctors, and that if you wanted to become a first-class surgeon you might count on earning very little money for fifteen years. He talked rather strangely about doctors trying to patch the roof, when the foundations were rotten. He said that the doctors were not to blame, and that you could not get at the foundations, because the Vested Interest fence kept you out.

“What’s a Vested Interest?” Tod asked.

“You have got to find that out,” said Mr. Burgetrode.Then he made a sudden suggestion.

“Would you like to take up houses, Western?”

“I think I should, sir.”

“Study them inside out, and from top to bottom, how they are built, and how they shouldn’t be built?”

“That’s it,” said Tod.

Mr. Burgetrode looked hard at him with those queer, fierce eyes of his.

“Very well, Ajax. I shall be at that hotel for another month. If you want me, you will know where to find me.”<b>Chapter Twelve</b>

Someone had once nicknamed Mr. Burgetrode “Old Mischief.” That was ten years ago, and if Shaston had been asked to place and describe Mr. Julian Burgetrode, Shaston would have stared at you uncomprehendingly, or would have asked whether you were referring to “that old chap who stayed at the Majestic?” Even the Hotel Majestic knew very little about Mr. Burgetrode. He was sarcastic, and fierce and laconic. He had an abundance of money, and he appeared to despise money, judging by the size of his tips. People gossiped about him. He was mysterious, eccentric, fabulously wealthy. The old ladies who sat in the drawing-room and read Marie Corelli, and Mr. Garvice and “The Rosary,” spun all sorts of romances about Mr. Burgetrode. They said he was a misogamist, an atheist and other unwholesome and unnatural things. His wife had committed suicide, or run away with another man, and he had chronic dyspepsia, and took drugs. Mr. Burgetrode did not care at all what the old ladies said of him. He disliked old ladies, disliked them as much as a fierce dog hates cats, and he never attempted to compromise.

“Reynolds,” he once said to the head waiter, “I’ll give you ten pounds a week if you promise not to put one of those minute guns within ten yards of me.”

“Minute guns, sir?”

“Old women, Reynolds, old women.”

“Rather difficult, sir; you see——”“Yes, I know; it rains old women and caps and artificial fringes, and bead bags and novels, in a place like this. Put something young, or something in trousers at those tables, something that doesn’t want to grin at you like a Cheshire cat.”

“I’ll see what I can do, sir,” said Reynolds.

The queer part of it was that the “staff” of the Hotel Majestic liked Mr. Burgetrode. They rather sympathised with him about the old ladies. The maid who looked after his bedroom and private sitting-room laid it down as an axiom that she would rather “do” for ten Mr. Burgetrodes than for one of those old cats. “He’s a sport,” she asserted; “he’s got a tongue on him, but if you’ve got anything straight to say, he don’t mind you answering back.” The lift boys were Mr. Burgetrode’s devoted slaves. Nor did largesse account for it all. There was something fiercely human about the old man; he could be extraordinarily severe and extraordinarily kind.

There were, however, one or two people in Shaston-on-Sea who knew who and what Mr. Julian Burgetrode was in the larger world up north. He came south to escape from the roar and the smother of the factory and the market, Shaston was a sort of toy-town to him, and he strolled about and looked down on it like an amused Colossus. Yet there was a memory behind it all, a smell of the blue sea, a glory of golden broom and white black-thorn, a girl and a boy honeymooning in one of the old white Georgian houses under the cliff. Ten days of marriage, and then death, death in the blue sea on a sweet spring morning.In one of the Old Town churchyards there was a stone that told how Elsie Burgetrode had been buried there nearly forty years ago.

Such was Shaston’s sentimental hold on Julian Burgetrode. He liked the slothfulness of the place after the hard shallowness of the North. He came south when he wanted to think, and went tramping for miles over the beech-covered hills and into the soft blueness of the Weald. Away in the north men were dominated by the idea of the machine. They had forgotten to wonder how green life started in the dim slime of primeval seas. Burgetrode was lord of many machines, but never the slave of one of them. He could still jump a fence, delight in Anatole France, and sit up thinking with Bergson.

Shaston was his toy-town. How much of it was his and what a grip his brown hands had on it, very few people suspected. One lawyer knew; so did a bank-manager; so did Rathbone Cragg of Rathbone Cragg and Son, Estate and House Agents. And when Julian Burgetrode limped into the office of Rathbone Cragg in Caroline Place, old Cragg fell from his perch of supercilious greed and flopped and squawked most unctuously upon the floor.

“Good morning, sir. Will you please come in my private room, sir.”

“I want a few words with you, Mr. Cragg.”

“Quite so, sir. Thomas, will you see that we are not disturbed?”Burgetrode detested Mr. Cragg. The man was such a Lord Chief Justice to little people, yet he was so easily mastered by a dog bigger than himself.

“You would be able to find room in your office for a youngster of eighteen or nineteen?”

“For instruction, sir?”

“Yes, in the ordinary way of business. He would start at the bottom and work his way up. I happen to be interested in a boy whom I want to train. When he had spent two or three years with you, I should put him to a builder.”

Mr. Cragg nodded.

“It shall be done, to oblige you, Mr. Burgetrode. I presume——”

“The boy is not to know that I have any ultimate interest in his future. I want you to understand that, Mr. Cragg. Any breach of confidence——”

“You can rely on me, sir, absolutely.”

“Very good. The youngster’s name is Western. He will call on you one day with a letter from me. I want you to treat him as you would treat any other clerk in your office. Good morning.”Tod guessed nothing of Mr. Burgetrode’s sudden interest in him. It had not struck Tod that he was something of a phenomenon, a lad in a shop who was sturdily set against the telling of lies. Mr. Burgetrode had been handling men all his life, dull men, grumbling men, good men, bad men, men who were contented provided they got beer, men who would have been discontented if they had been drowned in that beverage. Then there was the new type of man, the keen-witted, implacable revolutionist, who knew what he wanted and meant to get it. Mr. Burgetrode was making a special study of that type of man, for he saw in him the man of the future. He needed mellowing; he needed a little more honest pudding in his stomach, and less poison under his tongue. At present the new man was ugly, and Mr. Burgetrode detested ugliness.

He had been impressed at once by the unusualness of Tod. He liked the lad’s intelligent square head, his brown face, and alert unfearing eyes. The boy was sensitive too, and not in the least arrogant. He looked at life and asked questions, the very questions that a thinker like Julian Burgetrode knew to be the great questions that the world had to answer. The boy’s sincerity was a thing of wonderful significance. It was like one of those magic swords, hammered out by goblins in the underworld, and then belted to some blue-eyed, innocent, fate-predestined hero.

Meanwhile Tod was feeling very unheroic in John Snape’s shop. The regular dispensing of prescriptions was but a small part of the business. There was plenty of honest stuff to sell, from photographic plates to toothpicks, but Tod could never forget those quack medicines and the sham doctoring that was carried on by Snape and his assistants. Tod foresaw that his days were numbered.He was getting discredited. Some day there would be a row, and Mr. Snape would suggest his returning to the farm.

Tod felt the storm gathering. Mr. Snape had taken to bullying him, and using a dry sarcasm on every possible occasion. For a pink, well-fed man he had a most ingenious and spiteful tongue.

“Is that the way you wrap up bottles, Western? Here, let me show you. You’ve got no snap or neatness in your fingers, bodgering a bottle in paper till it looks as though it were dressed in a dirty nightshirt!”

“A place for everything, Western, and everything in its place. For goodness’ sake get some method in that head of yours. And your hair wants cutting.”

“You had better oil that voice of yours, young man. This isn’t a school playground.”

Tod took all this heckling as a good boxer takes punishment, but it was providing him with the justification for getting in a good straight punch to Mr. Snape’s jaw. He was not like poor old Wisdon with his wife and three children and his eighty pounds a year. Wisdon was not a certified dispenser, he was just so much casual labour, living in perpetual fear of being out of work. Snape bullied Wisdon, and daily cut the throat of his hired man’s self-respect, and Wisdon went like a sheep to the slaughter, offering the neck of his man’s pride so that his children might live.Tod saw Mr. Burgetrode on several evenings, and entered into long discussions with his new friend. He put forward the case of “Mr. Any-think-else,” arguing hotly that a man of Wisdon’s age should not be so placed in life that he could be bullied and sneered at by a man younger than himself.

“Money again,” said Mr. Burgetrode; “your Mr. Snape seems something of a swashbuckler.”

“I couldn’t stand what old Wisdon has to stand,” said Tod; “I’m just waiting, and one day I’ll land Snape one and walk out of the shop.”

“Just for love, young Ajax?”

“No, sir, I’m waiting till he comes at me crooked over something I should have lied about, and then I’ll hit out straight.”

“Ha!” said Mr. Burgetrode in his fierce way; “come and tell me about it when it happens.”

It happened the very next day. About half an hour before closing time a pale woman with tired eyes walked into the shop, and it fell to Tod to serve her. Cook had left early; old Wisdon was in one of the stock rooms upstairs looking out trusses; Tod knew that Mr. Snape was in his office at the back of the shop. Tod noticed that the pale woman looked rather shabby, and that her face made him think of his mother as he remembered her six months before her death.“Have you got Bartrup’s Consumption Cure?”

“Yes,” said Tod.

“Is it—is it any good?” asked the pale woman.

Tod happened to know something about Bartrup’s Consumption Cure. Cook had told him that he had seen the analysis of that very popular remedy and that the best thing to do with it was to pour it down the drain.

Tod looked straight into the woman’s tired eyes.

“It’s rubbish,” he said.

“Rubbish!” echoed the woman.

“I’d see a doctor,” Tod added, “before I touched any drugs.”

The woman thanked Tod and left the shop, and it was then that Tod became aware of Mr. Snape standing at the far end of the counter like a little pink Napoleon who had caught one of his sentries giving secret information to an enemy patrol.

“So Bartrup’s Consumption Cure is rubbish, Mr. Western, is it?”

“I have been told so,” said Tod stubbornly.“And who gave you that valuable information?”

“It’s not my business to tell.”

John Snape lost his temper, and came and stood very close to Tod, so close that he spluttered in Tod’s face.

“You’re a damned fool, that’s what you are, Western. Nice sort of idiot for a man to have in his shop. Get me discredited—discredited! D’you understand? Supposing Bartrups were informed that their preparation had been called rubbish in my shop? You’d get me tabooed by Bartrups; they might take legal action——”

Mr. Snape drew breath, and Tod waited, blinking a little because he did not like Mr. Snape’s splutter.

“Where’s your loyalty to me? Didn’t I take you in here to give you a chance, and all you can do is to blab silly lies over the counter!”

“They’re not lies,” said Tod; “half the quack stuff we sell is just cheating.”

“Cheating!”

Mr. Snape gasped, and took a deep breath, but Tod got his blow in first.

“I’m leaving this evening,” he said, “this sort of business doesn’t suit me.”“Well, of all the impertinent young cubs——!”

“You needn’t call me names,” said Tod; “I’m not going to be responsible for Bartrup and Co., that’s all.”

<b>Chapter Thirteen</b>

The intervention of Mr. Julian Burgetrode initiated one of the good seasons of Tod’s career. Some big hand seemed to push open a heavy door and draw back a curtain, and Tod stepped easily into a new and more spacious life.

The office of Rathbone Cragg and Son, Auctioneers and Estate Agents, had a certain Victorian calmness, a deliberate and leisurely way of doing things that was decent and almost pontifical. The Craggs, both father and son, were very polite people, and with a politeness that suited the more civilised end of Shaston and the ritual beloved of the symbolists who attended the church of St. Alban the Martyr. Mr. Rathbone Cragg, lean, bald-headed, round shouldered, with that emaciated and cartilaginous throat of his, and the head of a benignant vulture, served as a sidesman at St. Alban’s Church, and also as a Justice of the Peace. His son, a younger and more anæmic sort of vulture, with a queer axe-shaped head, its edge fronting forwards in chin and nose, clove his way into the intellectual life of West Shaston with an indefatigable and supercilious seriousness that made more full blooded young men hate him as buoyant savages must hate your irrepressible missionary. Frederick Rathbone Cragg was for ever improving himself. He knew too much; facts and figures rolled off him like turnips off an overladen cart.He was the sort of young man who sat solemnly and grimly at breakfast with a part of Handsworth’s Encylopædia in front of him, munching eggs and bacon with teeth of virgin gold.

Tod discovered a new atmosphere. If you walked down Beacon Hill with the western sun behind you, you had a vision of a fresh and vivid Shaston, Shaston as the Georgians had schemed it, shorn of its base speculative excrescences in yellow brick and grey stucco, a white town strung between the blue of the sea and the green of the hills. In the beyond loomed the White Cliff, a little hazy with the smoke of the Old Town, the yellow sand making a dim gleam between it and the sea. There were the masts of the Shaston fishing fleet, black and crowded like the ships of the Greeks beached before Troy. Above the White Cliff the lighthouse painted a white streak against summer skies. The parade in front of the tall houses had breadth and dignity, and could stand and answer the roar of a rough sea. On the hills above the town the beeches and holm oaks and wind-beaten pines of Effingham Park, broke up and softened the firm white lines, and spread a rolling frieze between the white houses and the yet whiter clouds.

The Craggs had their office in Caroline Crescent, a bow of solid houses with the parade as its bowstring. Caroline Crescent suggested the Regency, gentlemen in gay coloured coats and white breeches, high-stepping horses, “tigers” hanging on to the backs of yellow-wheeled dog-carts, young gentlewomen with side curls, Pickwickian worthies with Hessian boots, and solid, round, white-breeched stomachs.The houses had balconies with long and steeply sloping green roofs, and from any of these balconies one of Marcus Stone’s sentimental young ladies might have thrown a red rose at some quite impossibly handsome and romantic young man on a horse. Such things had ceased to happen in Caroline Crescent. A little colony of doctors lived there, two solicitors and a dentist. Brass plates were as thick as gold teeth in the mouth of a nigger.

The Craggs were very discreet and restrained, and confined themselves to gold letters on the lower windows of No. 10. Blatancy did not pay in the west end of Shaston. Notice boards, posters and advertisements of houses to be let and sold were to be found only in the big hall and in the main office. Once upon a time there had been litigation in Caroline Crescent over the keeping of a cockatoo that screamed. The Craggs did not scream. You were careful of your manners when you practised your profession in the company of gentlemen who felt pulses or drafted people’s wills.

Tod felt all boots and hands on that first morning when he entered the establishment of the Craggs, and stood in the middle of the big office with Mr. Burgetrode’s letter of introduction between thumb and forefinger. A young woman with a high forehead and pince-nez was typing at a table. Two clerks were busy at their desks, one of them making entries in a ledger, the other sorting out a chaos of keys. Tod stood and waited till the younger of the two clerks chose to discover him.

“Yes?”“I’ve a letter for Mr. Cragg.”

“All right. Hand it over. Any answer?”

“I’ll wait and see,” said Tod.

The letter and the clerk disappeared through a glazed door on which “Mr. Rathbone Cragg” was painted in black letters. Tod noticed that there was a second and similar door with Mr. Frederick Rathbone Cragg’s name upon it. He stood and stared at the bills on the walls, at the maps and photographs of houses, at the typist clicking sedulously on her machine, until she stopped and stared at him in turn. Tod veered away, and felt uncomfortable. The young woman behind the table made him absurdly conscious of the fact that he was a creature that wore clothes.

The clerk reappeared with a new geniality towards Tod.

“Mr. Cragg will see you at once, Mr. Western.”

That interview puzzled Tod. Mr. Cragg stood up behind his desk and shook hands with him, and Tod felt embarrassed. This hard-eyed old gentleman who gobbled in his throat like a turkey, and was so jerkily and parentally polite, made Tod wonder what Mr. Burgetrode had said concerning him.

“Sit down, Mr. Western. So you want to be an estate agent?”Tod sat down.

“I’m interested in houses,” he said.

Mr. Cragg appeared to approve.

“Enthusiasm is the great secret of success. I have this little note here from Mr. Burgetrode, and it is possible that I may be able to oblige him. I understand that you are an orphan, Western.”

“Yes, sir.”

Mr. Cragg tilted his head like a benignant bird.

“A great deprivation, Western. And—about your education?”

“I was at Chipperton’s school at Pentworth for five years, sir.”

“An excellent school too. Well, let me see, you wish to enter my office immediately?”

“If you can take me, sir.”

“I think I may say that I can.”It all seemed so absurdly easy. There were no clouds, no complications, nothing but geniality and sunshine and an avoidance of all awkward questions; no reference to Mr. Flowerdew or Mr. Snape. Mr. Cragg suggested terms that made Tod feel a plutocrat. And then Mr. Frederick Rathbone Cragg was called in and presented to Tod as though he were a friend of the family.

Frederick Cragg gave Tod’s hand a hard and earnest squeeze. He stared impressively at people when he was introduced to them, and he stared very hard at Tod.

“Very glad to meet you, Western.”

Tod felt ten years older. He began to wonder whether he ought not to have come in a bowler hat.

Two months later Tod had become an enthusiastic student of houses, houses of all sorts and descriptions, stolid houses, frivolous houses, snobbish houses, houses that were disreputable yet happy. And Tod too was happy though he was in danger of being sucked over the edge of his youth into that respectable and genteel world that is death and destruction to any adventurous soul. Mr. Frederick Rathbone Cragg had taken control of Tod; he had a penchant for cultivating boys and young men as though they were puppies, and trying to teach them his own tricks. He was interested in houses, but his interest was different from Tod’s. He was a bit of commercialised intelligence, yet in spite of his fish blood and flat feet he mimicked all sorts of strange people, like a vain and clever ape who copies a gesture without understanding its real meaning. Young Cragg was an intellectual.He had read Browning and Henry James, Nietzsche and Bernard Shaw. In fact he had read a little of everything, and he was the secretary of the St. Margarets Literary Society. He posed. He liked to hand out queer and startling opinions. Nothing pleased him better than to drag around a youth like Tod, let off intellectual fireworks and see the little fellow skip and open his eyes very wide.

Tod, in his innocence, was flattered. He was impressed by “Young Supercilious,” as some of the scoffers called Mr. Frederick Cragg, the energetic person who had an answer to everything. He trailed Tod round with him and talked mediæval architecture with the dogmatism of a Ruskin, and was so much the philosopher and the man of the world that Tod had ears and no tongue.

“You always want to know the right people, Western; it’s just the same as going into the right sort of house.”

Very soon he was criticising Tod’s clothes, and telling him with an air of great impressiveness that such and such things were not worn.

“You mustn’t do it, old chap. No pink or red ties, and don’t wear brown boots on Sunday. Good form, you know; it counts, it counts with the right people.”

Mr. Burgetrode had gone north again, and Tod was left to the earnest egotism of Mr. Frederick Cragg. Life in the office was not unpleasant, and Tod’s growing enthusiasm flattered the pride of the firm. Mr. Burgetrode had hinted that Clarence Todhunter Western was an original child, and with such a sponsor life in the house of Cragg and Son was bound to be easy.They had been very ready to make the best of Tod, and Tod the learner was keen and indefatigable.

“That boy has got a future,” Fred said to his sister.

Miss Bertha Cragg was always tripping up the toes of her brother’s impressiveness.

“Because old Burgetrode has taken a fancy to him? Nice sort of wet nurse you make, Fred!”

“You women don’t understand.”

But he continued to make a sort of East End Settlement of Tod and to cultivate that young heathen. Tod went to Hanson’s the gentleman’s tailor, and had a suit of clothes made to measure. His ties grew more conservative and refined. He ceased to wear a cloth cap on the back of his head. Frederick Cragg rescued him from the bathos of Mrs. Budd’s cottage, and persuaded him to take a bed-sitting room in St. Helen’s Terrace in the house of an impoverished gentlewoman who read Tennyson and genuflected regularly at the church of St. Alban the Martyr. He lent Tod books, went for country walks with him, declaimed Keats and Rossetti, and talked a lot of nonsense that sounded very clever and rather shocking.

Caroline Crescent was teaching Tod all sorts of things about real estate.Tod had no immediate vision of the financial aspect of the business, and he had lost some of the distrust that the world of Flowerdew and Snape had roused in him. The Craggs were polite people. They were always and ever your most obedient servants, and they appeared to let or sell you a house for the pure social joy of doing you a service. They taught Tod about leases and agreements, the writing up of houses, the making of inventories, the valuing of fixtures, the business of insurance and of rent-collecting; and all the ritual of a successful sale. They had little to do with the cottage property of old Shaston. They confined their activities to the gentlemen’s residences and the red villas of St. Margarets and the Grand Parade; moreover, they were the “county people” and much county property had passed through their hands.

Young Cragg had taken Tod in to tea at “Clovelly,” the Craggs’ house in Salisbury Road, but Tod’s real emergence from the caterpillar state dated from the day when he was invited to tennis.

“My mater wants you to come up on Saturday, Western, and play tennis. Just a little party, you know.”

Tod confessed that he knew nothing about tennis.

People wondered what the Craggs thought about it, and Bertha Cragg in particular, Bertha who had been so conspicuously interested in Mr. Burgetrode’s protégé, but the Craggs satisfied nobody’s curiosity. Tod lapsed gradually from their circle—that was all.

Bertha Cragg dominated the family during that obscure social crisis, and impressed her spirit upon them. She bathed and played tennis with more than her old enthusiasm, and went about with such an air of healthy cheerfulness that people had no excuse for referring to her as “poor Bertha.” She snubbed her brother when he thought it his duty to be confidential and impressive. After all, it was a very trivial matter, a boy of twenty falling in love with a golden-haired shop-girl, very trivial and very natural. You did not stand on the seafront and draw everybody’s attention to the remarkable fact that the sea was coming in.

When she met Tod she showed him the same old frankness, and Tod’s eyes were never opened to the woman in her. She forgave him because she realised his innocence, and because she was rather unusual. The bitter jest was against herself, and she accepted it, making no moan and showing no malice; but she hated the girl who had captured Tod—hated her almost as much as she hated her own plain face.

She had been ambitious for the boy—her boy.“I think I could have made him happy, and I know I could have helped him,” she said in her own heart.

But that other girl? What would she make of Tod?

So poignant and strong was her curiosity that she went more than once to the shop of Horrox & Horrox, and contrived to be served by the girl with the red mouth and the flamboyant hair, the girl whom she had seen sitting with Tod in that shelter. These two women were antipathetic, fire and snow, even across a haberdashery counter.

“I want a silk scarf, please.”

“Yes, madam. Any particular colour?”

“A soft yellow.”

Scarves were brought, and Miss Hornblower betrayed all the faults of the bad saleswoman. She was casual, superior, ready with a vague sneer if a purchaser asked for something that was less expensive. She was Venus caged behind a counter, bored, absorbed in the cult of her own comeliness.

“Madame should never wear yellow.”

Bertha Cragg flushed and let the impertinence pass.
