The town of Shaston-on-Sea had shaped itself very much like the Nile, that Lily of the Desert with its flower-head on the sea and its stem trailing towards the equator. Shaston had flowered along the seafront in solid masses of white Georgian and grey stuccoed Victorian houses, and the Edwardian red villa craze had coloured the fringe of its petals a hard red. The high, commercial part of Shaston lay in the calyx of the flower, grouped about King William Street that joined the seafront to the stalk of the town, and there the simile ended. The Georgians had planned solidly and well, but the commercial individualists who had followed them had been men in a hurry to make money. The haste and the hideousness of it showed everywhere in the long valley behind the town. Bricks and stucco and slate had been slopped down in great yellow puddles that had trickled and met and intermingled till the ugliness was complete. There were rows upon rows of flat-faced, wall-eyed houses, crowding back to back with miserable little spaces, called “gardens,” between them. The creators of this base, cynically cheap rubbish heap may have taken Bermondsey or Bethnal Green as their models. Some sardonic humorist had flung names about. Blackberry Hill, Alsatia, Bilson’s Bottom, Rope Alley, Mug’s Lane, Victoria Road, Sea View Terrace. All this nastiness was within two miles of the Whiteholt beech woods, and less than a mile from the sea.Tod Western accepted Shaston-on-Sea as a healthy boy accepts his dinner. It was there, on the plate, waiting to be eaten, and Tod swallowed it with relish, not yet having arrived at that analytical stage when a man worries about his proteids, fats and carbohydrates. The place was new to Tod, and full of life and mystery. Mr. Flowerdew let the lad out every afternoon the first week, not from any wish that Tod should enjoy life, but rather to let him satiate himself with Shaston.

“Let a boy gorge himself with strawberries and he’ll give up wanting them.”

Tod explored Shaston from the fashionable quarter of St. Margarets in the West to certain picturesque slums called “The Shingles” and “Red Ruin” that lay under the shadow of the White Cliff. He went on the pier, tried the various penny-in-the-slot machines, listened to the band, went for a sail in one of the pleasure yachts, roared with laughter at Punch and Judy, got up early and bathed and made friends with a swarthy young man from Horrox & Horrox’s drapery establishment who promised to teach him to swim. This Mr. Robert Twine appeared to find Tod very breezy and refreshing. He was always calling him “You kid” or “You jolly green apple.” Tod was green, healthily and innocently green. He accepted life, and he accepted Shaston, putting his penny in the slot of each day and getting his chocolate or his nougat.

At the end of a week he was whole-heartedly in love with Miss Kitty Flowerdew. She was a shy girl who had gathered up her brown hair and lengthened her skirts less than six months ago, and whose prettiness was the prettiness of a soft dark pansy.She played the piano, and sang in a pleasant, bird-like way, such songs as “The Carnival,” “Good-bye” and “Annie Laurie.” She was religious, going to the weekday services at St. Jude’s, and never missing early communion on Sunday.

Tod fell into such depths of shyness in her presence that he became very slow over his meals, and was always late for pudding. His adoration gazed and gazed, and might have gone on gazing in the same blind and devoted way had not Miss Lena Flowerdew taken it in hand.

“Wake up, moon-calf.”

Quite a number of things began to puzzle Tod.

Edwin Rook puzzled him; he was such a cold fish, such a queer, sarcastic beast. He showed extraordinary suavity to anyone entering the shop, and talked with a glibness that fascinated Tod. Rook’s real self came to life again when the customer had returned to the street. He used to indulge in little snarling phrases.

He almost forgot Mr. Flowerdew, and the aggressive voice that insisted on reminding God that he was a “mis-er-able sinner.”

An incident that occurred late in the autumn taught Tod to look at life and Kitty Flowerdew with a new alertness. Tod’s love could hold back no longer. Here was his most dear lady, persecuted, humiliated, and all because of her kindness to him.

“Kitty! You know I love you, Kitty. I can’t stand them being such beasts to you.”

Tod was puzzled, but he suppressed the hot chivalry of his youth and the desire to do something heroic. The Flowerdews’ house was not a tilting ground for heroism, and the more Tod pondered the matter, the less sanguine he felt. Sir Galahad would have been met here with ridicule and a shower of rotten eggs. The lad began to understand Kitty’s sensitive dread and her shrinking from the malicious vulgarities of her sister’s tongue.

Tod made up his mind to dissemble. If it helped Kitty and saved her from further domestic storms, so much the better. He told himself that he would wait. The whole crowd made Tod think of a lot of unwholesome people getting into a stuffy corner and solemnly holding their noses because of the smell. They were so drearily unhuman and self-complacent. He asked Bod Twine about it, because he had come to regard that swarthy young man as violently and savagely honest.

“I don’t see that it does anybody any good,” he argued.“Course it doesn’t kid. It’s like telling a chap who’s healthy that he’s got a worm. Twine had taught Tod to swim; he also taught him to box, and young Western began to be one of the pets of the local football club. He had shaped well as a half-back at Chipperton’s, and before Christmas he was playing right-half for Shaston Town, and was beloved of the local crowd. “Worry-’em-Western” was his nickname, and even Mr. Flowerdew approved. It was an advertisement. “That’s young Western from Flowerdew’s.” The commercial eye sees a placard everywhere, and pastes up a pious “poster” whenever it looks publicly upon its God.

Yet even Clarence Todhunter Western had his worries. The Flowerdews household had made inroads on his youth, and changed him from the irresponsible boy to the young man who was finding life rather a queer and puzzling business. His silent and restrained loyalty to Kitty made him all the more jealous and observant.He began to notice that Edwin Rook was always sneaking out of the shop and into the house when Eli Flowerdew was not about. He noticed, too, that Rook’s eyes were for ever watching Kitty during meals, but that she hardly ever looked at him in return. Rook seemed to frighten her and to reduce her to silence. Moreover, Miss Lena Flowerdew grew very snappy and sarcastic towards the shopman, and Tod caught her watching him trying to ingratiate himself with her sister. Miss Flowerdew’s face was never very pleasant or amiable, but on those occasions Tod thought her the ugliest thing he had ever seen.

Then Edwin Rook took to attending St. Jude’s church and sat in the Flowerdew pew, displacing Tod, who was pushed to the far end by the pillar. Tod was annoyed but contemptuous. He had a healthy scorn for Rook, a fellow who wore glasses, never played any games, and was, Tod suspected, a physical coward. Tod, the romanticist, blindly refused to accept Rook as a serious rival, which wild faith of his betrayed how ignorant he was of the real inwardness of the Flowerdew family.

But it was the shop and the business that were puzzling the sportsman in Tod. You bought as cheaply as you could and you sold as dearly as you could; that seemed fairly obvious and reasonable, and yet Tod was not satisfied. When some old woman received three and sixpence for an article which Mr. Flowerdew sold to a wealthy stockbroker’s wife three days later for five guineas, the gap seemed too greedy. Tod began to see a good deal of this buying and selling, and it left him with an uneasy and guilty feeling. Also it became very plain to him that in a shop you were not supposed to tell the truth. You romanced, you exaggerated—in short, you lied.“Those candlesticks, madam? Yes, we picked them up only a week ago and at a fancy price. They won’t remain with us very long; the first connoisseur who sees them——”

Now Tod knew that these candlesticks had been bought at a sale three months ago, and that Mr. Flowerdew had given exactly seven shillings for them. The lady, a visitor to Shaston, bought them for three pounds ten.

Moreover, for subtle reason known only to himself, Edwin Rook set himself to try and debauch and commercialise Tod. He was cynical, jocular, and facetious, and he took a delight in airing all the trade cynicisms, and flaunting them in front of Tod’s eyes.

“You’ve got to watch your customer, my lad. If it’s a ‘regular’ and one who knows a bit, go easy. If it’s a stranger, why then you can do a bit of fibbing and push the price.”

“Expert knowledge! What do we want to know for? Why, it’s the whole game. You can score off the people who don’t know, get ’em to take sixpence for something that’s worth a quid. That’s business.”“Sentiment. There’s no sentiment in a shop. Only oil, my boy, oil.”

Tod disliked Rook all the more thoroughly for these cynicisms of his. At first, he did not take them too seriously. Rook was a glib talker, rather pleased with his own cleverness, but as the weeks went by, Tod began to realise that Rook was telling him the truth. The discovery depressed Tod very seriously. He felt that he was getting mixed up in a sordid sort of game, a game in which your main endeavour was to cheat. Tod called it “cheating” to himself, quite frankly and with no reservations. If you tried to foul in the football field, the referee dropped on you, and the crowd, if it was not too bigoted a crowd, hooted.

And then came the incident of that book.

Mr. Flowerdew had been present at one of those “sales of necessity.” A doctor had died and left a widow and three children, and a small balance at the bank. Mr. Flowerdew bought a few lots at the sale, and among them an odd number of books that cost him thirteen shillings. Tod was sorting these books two days after the sale when he came across a queer old volume that sent him running for Messrs. Porteous’s price catalogue of second-hand books.

There seemed no doubt about it. That particular book was valued at fifteen guineas.

Tod, full of enthusiasm, announced the fact to Rook.“I say, there’s been a mistake. We have bought a valuable book mixed up in a lot of rubbish.”

“Where’s the mistake?” asked the shopman with cynical sangfroid.

“But the governor only paid thirteen bob for the lot, and this book is priced at fifteen guineas. The people who sold it didn’t know. We shall have to send it back.”

“Do what?” squealed Mr. Rook.

“Why, send it back. It wouldn’t be fair or sporting to keep it. I don’t suppose Flowerdew knew when he bought the lot.”

Rook stared at Tod, and then gave a queer laugh.

“Suggest it, old chap. If the auctioneers didn’t do their work properly, and the governor had the luck——”

“You mean you’d keep it and say nothing?”

“You soft egg!” said Mr. Rook.

Tod was challenged. He said nothing more to Rook, but waited for Mr. Flowerdew to give a judgment of Solomon.Rook was out when the interview occurred, and he missed one of the most cynical comedies that had ever been played in Mr. Flowerdew’s shop.

“Excuse me, sir, but you bought a valuable book the other day by mistake.”

Mr. Flowerdew was totting up some figures in a notebook, and he looked at Tod with interested approval.

“What’s that, Western? You are learning to use your wits, are you? What about it?”

Tod explained, and Eli Flowerdew listened with fatherly satisfaction until Tod made the extraordinary suggestion that the book should be returned.

Mr. Flowerdew’s thin lips slipped back over his yellow teeth.

“Say that again, Western?”

Tod said it again.

And then Eli Flowerdew gave Tod a solemn and biblical description of what business was and how it should be conducted. He expatiated on the necessity for loyalty between master and assistant, and how that his—Mr. Flowerdew’s—interest was Tod’s. He trounced Tod’s sentimental obtuseness, and assured him that commerce and civilisation would be impossible if people behaved in the way Tod had suggested.“You have got to get the business spirit, Western,” he said grimly, “if you want to rise in this world. Competition doesn’t say ‘After you, please.’ If you hold yourself responsible for other peoples mistakes, you are a fool, young man. Don’t you mix sentiment and business. Towards the end of the winter Tod began to be very restless. Kitty Flowerdew had been away for a month, and there had been nothing to relieve the ugliness of the house in Ponsonby Place; Tod was growing a little weary of serving like a boy Jacob for his Rachael. Edwin Rook’s cynicisms, and a more intimate knowledge of Eli Flowerdew’s methods of making money, had killed Tod’s interest in the business. He began to hate the shop and everything in it, almost as much as he hated the atmosphere of the Flowerdew house. Some of those queer sayings of his father’s cropped up like sharp flints under his feet.

“I’m not a penny-in-the-slot machine.” “This country with its penny-three-farthing soul!” “Pray publicly to God, and cheat your neighbour!”

There were elements of rebellion stirring in Tod. He lost some of his frankness, and grew rather morose and thoughtful, and he showed a new fierceness towards Edwin Rook. He began to take long prowls after closing time, and forgathered with Bob Twine and two or three other young men who liked the rush of the sea air on their faces. Tod found that it was agreed that life as a shopkeeper’s assistant was doomed to suck all the virility out of a man’s soul. Yet these rebels had no alternative paths of adventure to suggest, save joining the Army of running after girls. Mossup, a mild, flaxen-haired, and rather serious soul who wore spectacles, hoped to attain liberty by acquiring a shop of his own.

“You’re welcome to it, old man,” said Twine; “you ain’t rogue enough. You’ll be dodging bankruptcy till it makes you grey. I’m turning Socialist.”Tod happened to mention the word “Socialist” to Edwin Rook, and was puzzled by the shopman’s slimy and secretive smile.

“So you know some Socialists, do you, Western?”

“I know one,” said Tod.

The information was passed on in a casual and inconsidered manner to Mr. Flowerdew. Tod did not suspect it then, but Rook had decided that Western was in his way, and Rook set about edging Tod overboard with the methodical shrewdness of the born sneak and coward. He dropped hints that Tod was getting into loose company and loafing about the town at night. He even hinted at “girls.” All girls, save his own, were anathema to Eli Flowerdew.

Two days before Kitty Flowerdew was expected home, Rook laid a deliberate trap for Tod. A couple of packing-cases had arrived from Birmingham, and Rook called Tod to help him unpack them.

“One of our confidential jobs, my son. Now what do you think of that brass door-knocker?”

“It looks old,” said Tod.

They unpacked a pretty selection of mock antiques in metal—candlesticks, tinder-boxes, sconces, and the like. Rook chatted jocularly, and in a cynically confidential way to Tod.“The fools will have old things, so we have to get ’em.”

“Aren’t these old?” Tod asked.

“Fakes, reproductions, the whole lot of them.”

“Oh, don’t we! You’ll sell tons before you’ve done.”

Tod was kneeling, and with two hands on the edge of the case he faced Rook across it.

“I’m dashed if I will. It’s not honest.”

“I will, if he asks me. And what’s more, if I see you trying to sell the stuff as old—I’ll—I’ll——”

“Hoity-toity!” said Mr. Rook.

“Can I have a few words with you, sir?”“What’s the matter, Rook?”

“Come into my room.”

With a certain air of diffidence and unwillingness Rook described Tod’s rebellious attitude and the threats he had used.

“Of course, I was teasing the chap really, but he’s likely to be a bit of a problem, sir. He doesn’t understand being loyal, and I think Western’s getting rather wild.”

Tod was destined to strike one of those adventurous and pugilistic passages in his life before he ceased from the Flowerdew household.Romance caught him, romance and a sudden glimpse of Kitty, Kitty unseen for a whole month, and drifting in like the spring with an armful of daffodils. “Tod——”

“It’s been such a long month,” he said.

“Oh, Tod, I wish——”

He heard voices that night; Mr. Flowerdew’s voice very hard and emphatic, much the same voice that Flowerdew used at the meetings of the Social Purity League. Two days passed, wrathful, volcanic days, with Tod ready to erupt. Kitty went about the house with the strange air of a doomed thing in disgrace. All the fine fury seemed to fall out of Tod’s chivalry. He gave Mr. Flowerdew a blank and bewildered look, put his hands in his pockets, and disappeared.

Tod passed a Byronic and very miserable night. It was as though some Eastern giant had reached down a great hand, lifted the world’s lid, and shown Tod all sorts of strange secrets and knavish devices. Tod understood the Flowerdew household as he had never understood it before. He remembered that saying of Bob Twine’s that Eli Flowerdew had driven his wife to suicide, and that Kitty was the daughter of her mother. Rook wanted Kitty, he had wanted her all the while; Tod understood that now, and why Rook hated him. But Kitty did not want Edwin Rook, and the inference was obvious. Eli Flowerdew was giving the girl to Rook, compelling her, even against her great loathing, to surrender herself to this lover, this cold, weedy beast with his bland “selling” manner, and his venomous tongue.

And why?

The answer to that question stuck in Tod’s throat, but he was constrained to swallow it. Rook was a useful man to Eli Flowerdew. He knew all the chicanery of the business, and practised it like a man who loved it. It was possible that he knew so much that he had to be part of the family, part of the conspiracy.Tod tossed to and fro in bed, bit his pillow, and schemed all sorts of mad and impossible schemes for the rescuing of Kitty. He felt that he loved her as he had never loved her before, since he had seen her twisting in Rook’s arms. Rook shouldn’t have her! He would see her to-morrow and appeal to her to run away with him. Yes, and on what? Tod’s shrewdness saved him from being a mere sentimental and adventurous young fool. He knew that he had about three shillings in his trousers pocket, no very noble purse for an eloping couple.

No, he would be perfectly straight with old Flowerdew. He would stand up to him like a man and say, “I love Kitty, and I think Kitty loves me. I’m young, but I mean to get on. You can’t give Kitty to Rook.”

Yes, and that would entail his staying in the business, and swallowing all the humbug and the trickery. What a slimy and clever devil that chap Rook was! Tod realised that he had been driven into a corner, and that though he had punched Rook’s head, the fellow had him tied up like a fly. And the beastliness of it all, old Flowerdew of the Social Purity League, buying a man’s loyalty by giving him the body of his daughter!

Tod went downstairs next morning, red-eyed, and prepared to fight, but the Flowerdew household surprised and nonplussed him, and smothered the attack. Kitty did not appear at breakfast; she was locked in her room, and Tod did not have a glimpse of her that morning. He did not understand it, or guess that she was gloating over his trouncing of Edwin Rook.He had not heard that gibe of hers, “Nice sort of man you are, to be licked by a mere boy.” But Mr. Flowerdew himself was the surprise of the morning. It was the Sabbath Flowerdew that appeared at the breakfast-table, solemn, sententious, oracular, dressed in its Sunday clothes. “Now, Western, if you please.”

“All right,” said Tod, restraining himself; “I’m learning.”

He described the incident to “Mr. Any-think-else,” and appealed to him for sympathy.“I only told the truth,” Tod argued. “How was I to know whether the stuff was any good for people’s feet? Is it?”

“Don’t know,” said Wisdon sulkily.

“Well, would you tell the truth?”

“Tell the truth! Great Scott, Western, I’ve got a wife and three children.”

Tod said no more, but he thought a great deal about the problem of old Wisdon and his wife and children.

He fell in that evening with Arthur Mossup from Horrox and Horrox’s, and Mossup, who had a shy man’s affection for him, named him for an adventure.

“Like a walk, Western?”

“Right-o! Where are you going?”

Mossup showed him a handful of keys.

“House-hunting. It’s the only time I’ve got, and an agent friend of mine obliged me with these. I’m after a little place, and we have two hours’ daylight.”

“What fun,” said Tod.
