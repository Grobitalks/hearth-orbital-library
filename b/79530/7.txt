“Look at me, ma petite, right into my eyes. Have you not given me your love, and is not that all that I desire? But you overheard a little secret, a secret that was to have been kept till after our marriage.”

“I do not like secrets,” she said.

“It is no longer a secret. My big friend for whom I work, is like a father to me. He is very rich. It was his wish that he should give you a dot. I consented, because it will be such a pleasure to him, and because he has no children and is lonely.”

“But fifty thousand francs! What shall I do with such a sum?”

“It can be invested. You will have a little income of your own, for your clothes and all that a woman wants. And you will be able to send little presents to Josephine and Madame.”

Her face brightened.

“How good you are. And all those papers, and the mayor, what do they mean?”

Tod smoothed her hair back with his hands and kissed her forehead.

“Claire, I know that these good people trust me, but I wanted to show them everything, so that they could be sure.Remember, I am taking you away to England; I want them to have no shadow left behind; it is only right that I should leave them with no whisper of a doubt. Each year we will come to France and visit them; we can take our holiday here, and Josephine and your aunt can come to us.”

“Oh, how good you are,” she said, “my Tod, mon mari!”

He held her very close.

“To-morrow you will not go to school?”

“But why?”

“I have hired an auto for to-morrow. We go to Abbeville or Eu, whichever you please. Josephine can come with us. We are going shopping.”

“But, Tod!”

“Listen,” he said. “I haven’t spent any money for such a long time, and I am going to enjoy myself. We will go to the jeweller’s and the costumier’s, and visit the ladies who sell chapeaux. Yes, I am going to enjoy myself very much.”

“I have a little dress,” she began, “that will do for the marriage.”

“Of course you have,” he said, “but is that any reason why I should not enjoy myself? Besides, I want you to choose presents for all my good friends.”She looked at him with sudden, mischievous eyes.

“So you realise that I want nothing?”

“Of course I realise it,” he said, “but it is I who want a very great number of things. And what is more, I am going to get them.”

<b>Chapter Forty-five</b>

The car was waiting at Monsieur Lorellard’s door; not a very brilliant car to be sure, but what did that matter? It needed painting; the upholstering had felt the war, and the chauffeur wore a cloth cap and an old blue military overcoat. Tod looked at the tyres, and half Myonville looked at Tod.

“They are going shopping.”

“To Paris?”

“Paris! Listen to Nanette! The child’s head is always in Paris. No; Abbeville, ma chérie.”

“He is going to buy her a ring, perhaps?”

“Of course.”

“Look, they are going alone! Mademoiselle Josephine is not dressed.”“Well, it is the English custom, and monsieur is English.”

“I wish I could go to Abbeville and buy pretty clothes,” said Nanette.

Tod was handing Claire into the car. She wore a plain little black hat and a black coat and skirt, and her shoes were not new, nor were her gloves very much newer than her shoes. A white lace collar and a quaint little tartan tie showed at her throat. Yet everything was dainty, distinctive, sleek, indefinably French and charming.

“How pretty you look!” said Tod.

“These clothes? I bought them in Lille six years ago. Josephine, you traitress!”

Josephine, in apron and slippers, laughed at them from the doorway.

“No, no, I never meant to come. I should have been so unnecessary.”

André gave a push to the old car as it smoked off.

“Au revoir. Remember me to all the charming girls at Printemps.”

They did not go to Abbeville; they went to Eu; for Claire had a greater love for that more mysterious, more steeply-streeted, and more shadowy old town.There were scars of the war too in Abbeville, raw places that had not healed, memories of death and of terror. And Claire was happy, happy with the beautiful completeness of a child who rushes out to gather flowers on a morning in June. The woman in her took the joy of a child and held it against her heart. It was her day of days, her first adventure with her lover.

The old car creaked and panted along the upland road, threading the shadows of the long avenues, and thrusting its unpolished snout into the sunlight and the broad blueness of that June day. Tod was holding Claire’s hand. The Frenchman in the front seat kept his eyes on the road.

“It was just here,” said Tod, pointing to the grass bank under the beeches.

“Yes, I remember.”

“And Punch came to the rescue. Poor old Punch! I wanted to buy him, but he got lost at the Base.”

They dreamed awhile.

“Ma chérie,” said Tod suddenly, “what sort of house would you like to live in?”

She was lost in a new languor, the delicious sense of her new intimacy with this dearest of men, but she came out of her dreams and smiled at him.“A little house, just a little house.”

“You shall have it.”

“And a woman, a sensible woman to help me.”

“Will one be enough?”

“What should I do with more? I want the little house to be my house.”

“You are not always going to be in the house,” said Tod. “I shall want you to come out with me. I shall want people to see my charming little wife.”

“So you would like me to wear pretty dresses, sometimes?”

“It will be a weakness of mine, I’m afraid.”

The car sailed quite jauntily down the long hill into the valley below Eu. Tod was feeling in the breast pocket of his coat; he brought out a rather bulky pocketcase and looked half shyly and half tenderly at Claire.

“Now you are going to let me enjoy myself?”

“What do you mean?”

“I am going to buy things, all sorts of things.”She resisted, because a part of the subtle and exquisite joy of the day was in resisting, and in the ultimate surrender. A Frenchwoman loves interplay; it is born and bred in her, and she weaves it into all the tender activities of her life. Her exclamation, her smiles, her retaliations are more subtilised and less obvious than those of the Englishwoman. She has not the mental nudity of the average woman across the water. “She is always dressed,” says the critical islander, but when that dress is charming and a little unexpected, why quarrel with it? All civilisation hangs in the grace of a petticoat and the set of a hat.

Tod bent forward and spoke to the driver.

“We shall stay in Eu most of the day. Take us to the best hotel and put up the car there.”

“Bien, monsieur.”

“And make yourself comfortable, monsieur; I wish it.”

The Frenchman smiled.

There was no haste in the mood of the morning. They left the car at the hotel and set out to wander through the streets of Eu. Claire knew all the shops, but she remained dispassionate, tenderly reticent, leaving the spirit of the adventure to Tod. He should discover all the wonderful things that he desired.If necessary she could guide him without appearing to guide, resist by choosing. But she went with him as no cold creature, no prig, no careful woman seizing an opportunity. She was just as excited, just as passionately happy as Tod, but she would keep her head because this dear man had lost his heart.

“We will go in here,” said Tod, after a glance at the rings in the <i>bijoutier’s</i> window.

It was a great affair, the buying of that ring, but a most subtle victory for Claire.

“Look, it is exquisite, this little one. I love it.”

The simple little hoop with its single ruby, like a soft flower with drops of brilliant dew hanging about its petals, might not have satisfied Tod. Nor did a difference of two hundred francs satisfy the <i>bijoutier</i>.

“This other is a very beautiful ring, monsieur.”

“It is,” said Tod.

“Look, then!”

She put it on, closed her fist, and made a quick revelation of the thing’s hardness, its artificiality. Then she pulled it off, and let her hand lie softly on Tod’s sleeve.“It suits me,” she said; “it is so simple.”

She was right. There was a spirituality in her choice.

“Your taste is better than mine,” he said.

Her eyes lifted to his.

“But does it not please you better, on the hand of this little woman who loves you so?”

“You most exquisite soul,” he said.

They ignored the <i>bijoutier</i>; nothing mattered that day. But the <i>bijoutier</i> still had ambitions.

“One moment, mademoiselle; here!”

“I have chosen,” was all she said, with an air of extraordinary yet gentle finality.

The man said no more. He had studied women.

They wandered out again, Claire glancing at her ring.

“It is so sweet,” she said, “but there is yet another sweetness in it.”He smiled down at her, with a question in his eyes.

“Well?”

“You let me choose it, you let the woman in me choose.”

“I could not help it,” said Tod.

Twenty yards farther on he wheeled and drew up outside a costumier’s. It was one of those particularly French shops, small, piquant, restrained, showing a few exquisite things in the window, a dress, two hats, a rose-coloured sash, two little grey slippers standing demurely apart. Claire knew this little shop. It was a fragment of Paris, very fashionable, charming, but expensive.

“I think this should do,” said the man.

She admired Tod for choosing that shop.

“My dear, it is very expensive.”

“A few good things are better than a lot of cheap ones.”

She had no answer to that.

They entered the shop, and it was Tod who spoke to a swarthy little Frenchwoman with bright eyes and a merry smile.He stood in the middle of the square of green carpet, his hat in his hand, his blue eyes vaguely amused.

“Madame, have you any Paris models?”

“Everything is from Paris, monsieur.”

“Good,” said Tod. “May we see everything? My fiancée wishes to choose.”

The two women looked at each other. Claire smiled.

“A simple costume, madame, that I can wear at my marriage, and afterwards.”

There was some subtle interchange of sympathy between the two women. Tod, the man, was put gently on to a chair in a corner and persuaded to be a spectator. Madame and Claire began to chatter like old friends.

“Yvonne!”

A pretty girl, like a dark pansy, appeared from behind a glass door. Madame spoke to her with an air of mystery; and the dark girl disappeared, to return with an armful of Parisian creations.

They were displayed, commented on, discussed as though Claire were a daughter.“What about that pink thing?” said Tod from his corner, the blatant and enthusiastic male.

Madame was very emphatic.

“Oh, no, monsieur—never! Yvonne should not have brought it.”

Tod lapsed and became again the devoted spectator. And yet he was enjoying himself intensely, watching Claire and the two women fingering these beautiful things, Yvonne spreading the costumes over her own figure for Claire to judge them like an impressionist. He beheld a new Claire, vivacious, critical, yet ever charming, smiling at him from time to time, quite capable of holding her own with these experts, betraying an instant and instinctive knowledge of what suited her and what did not.

“That pleases me.”

She appealed to Tod.

“But it is black,” he objected.

Madame suppressed him with motherly impressiveness.

“Black, monsieur, is the most refined, the most restrained, the most cunning of colours. A charming woman never looks better than in black.”“Supposing you try it on?” said Tod meekly.

The two women, the costumes, and Claire disappeared into a room behind the shop, and Tod waited for the completion of the mystery. Half an hour passed, and then Yvonne came hurrying out.

“Mademoiselle wishes to know if monsieur is ennuyé?”

“I am quite happy, mademoiselle.”

She smiled at him archly and hurried out into the street. Something seemed to be lacking, some little refinement, a detail that was necessary for the completion of a picture. Yvonne returned with an armful of cardboard boxes, but she did not tell Tod what they contained.

Tod heard much pretty chattering in French, the blending of three women’s voices, exclamations of delight and of admiration. Tod felt thrilled, just as excited as the women, for was there not something of a sacramental mystery about all this feminine ritual, this dressing of his most dear lady? And then she appeared to him; vaguely shy, smiling in a way that was new to her, a little, subtle, tender smile, her soft lips ready to ask a question.

“Dear heart!” said Tod.

He stood up and gazed, and his eyes saw nothing but this little woman in a black chiffon velvet gown cut low in the front,threaded about the wrists and neck with twisted threads of cerise and silver, and held at the waist by a belt of silver. On her head was a close-fitting black velvet hat, carrying a little halo of silver filigree work, and underlined with cerise. Grey silk stockings and little black shoes with silver grey uppers showed below the hem of the black dress.

“Well?” her eyes asked him with a little tremble of light.

“Dear heart,” said Tod again, “it’s—it’s perfect.”

She gave him a strange melting look.

“What beauty there may be in me—is yours,” it said.

Madame clapped her hands softly, and Yvonne smiled.

“Monsieur is convinced! Mademoiselle has the beauty of simplicity.”

“It’s—it’s perfect,” said Tod once more, looking into Claire’s eyes.

And he refused to let her change that costume. It was so simple and so charming that he felt that she could have met both Myonville and Paris in those dainty and gentle clothes.

“The other dress can be packed, madame, and we can call for it.”“Certainly, monsieur.”

“And now,” said Tod, “what about the other things? I’m going out for a short walk.”

He handed his pocket-book to Claire.

“Tod—I——”

“I won’t be long,” he said, escaping out of the shop.

Claire looked at Tod’s pocket-book and then at Madame.

“I do not require anything more,” she said softly, “but a pair of gloves, a petticoat or two, and a simple blouse.”

“But if monsieur——?”

Claire smiled with her grey eyes at the other woman.

“It is sufficient—if I am happy.”

Madame smiled back at her.

“I understand. It is an admirable feeling. Yet, mademoiselle, if you drive in an auto——”“Yes,” said Claire with a sigh, “I shall need a cloak. A very simple one, one that I could wear too in the winter.”

“We have a black velour, and quite inexpensive. It will harmonise with that costume.”

“It must be quite inexpensive,” said Claire with that softly final air of hers.

Tod returned in half an hour, and wanted to know what she had bought.

“All that I need,” she told him, handing him back his pocket-book. “I have paid madame.”

Tod did not examine his pocket-book until the evening, and then to see whether he needed another cheque cashing at Abbeville. He found that he had more money left than he expected. Claire had refused to seize an opportunity.

From the costumier’s Tod carried her off to lunch at the hotel, a happily proud Tod who looked all Eu in the face with eyes that said “See what an exquisite little woman I have here. And she is going to marry me! Wonderful, isn’t it?” They had a bottle of Pommard for lunch, Claire drinking just one glass of it and looking as though she had been born in that charming frock. She was rather quiet with Tod, perhaps because she was so happy, and thought of the future without a moment of dismay.“We have more shopping to do yet,” he told her.

“For whom?”

“For you. And there are the presents for the family.”

She resisted gently.

“Ma chérie, you have bought me so much. For the others—yes, if it pleases you.”

“Have you a travelling trunk?” asked the practical Tod.

“A little one.”

“You will want something bigger. Let us run through the list. Answer correctly like a good child.”

She pretended to be extremely solemn.

“Have you an umbrella?”

“Yes.”

“A raincoat?”“No.”

Tod made a note of it.

“House shoes—pretty ones, I mean?”

She shook her head.

Tod made another note.

“Gloves?”

“Yes.”

“A little handbag for the vanities of women?”

“No.”

“That is essential.”

She held up a hand.

“Please, Tod, no more.”

There was something in her eyes that touched and restrained him.“Ma petite,” he said, “you have let me enjoy myself. It has been the happiest day of my life. I thank you, with all my heart.”

The day drew to a close, and the shabby old blue auto improvised a finale when they were within half a mile of Myonville by blowing a big burst in one of its back tyres. There was no spare wheel, only a spare tube and tyre. The chauffeur, who had lunched very well, smiled at Tod and asked them if they would remove themselves from the back seat as the tools and the jack were under it.

Tod got out.

“Why shouldn’t we walk? The car can bring on the parcels.”

They decided to walk.

The sunset was behind them, and they came into Myonville with a yellow sky at their backs and the long shadows of the trees falling across the village. One of the high avenues of beeches led them to the end of the village, and from the darkness of this avenue they seemed to step at once into the evening brightness of the wide and open street. The French folk were sitting at their doors, and the passing of Claire and Tod was the passing of a romance.

Myonville behaved like the warm-hearted and simple little place it was. Claire had nothing to fear from these good people. She went past the doorways with shining eyes, the little lady of Tod’s life, so frankly happy that she looked for no evil in the heart of this little world,nor would she have found it had she looked.

“Bon soir, Mademoiselle Vignard; bon soir, monsieur.”

There were smiles, whisperings, a bobbing out of heads when they were past, but not a grudging word, not a sneer, no English gawking, no English giggles. These people were civilised, and Claire Vignard had taught their children.

“Tiens! but what pretty clothes!”

“The Englishman has been spending his money.”

“Is she not looking charming?”

“Yes, Claire Vignard deserves it. It will not spoil her.”

“Ah, what a romance! Did you notice how he looked at her? They say the English make good husbands.”

“No man could help being good to her.”

And then a child ran out and into Claire’s arms.

“Oh, mam’selle! Pretty, pretty!”

Claire kissed her.“Am I pretty, ma petite, or is it the dress?”

“It is you,” said the child.

Tod bent down in turn and kissed Nanette.

“We think the same, Nanette; we are very wise people, you and I.”

The Lorellards were sitting in the doorway of the café, and it was Josephine who first caught sight of Claire, and Josephine’s good heart betrayed itself.

“They are coming. The auto must have broken down. Ma foi! she is lovely! He has turned her into a little Parisian angel!”

Even the bored André leant forward over his sister’s shoulder.

“It is Printemps,” he said; “Monsieur Tod has discovered Printemps.”

Claire was embraced, exclaimed over, kissed, while Tod stood and watched the pleasure and the playful tenderness of these dear people, a Tod who could not be disconcerted by André’s mischievous smile. Claire had to show her costume, her <i>chapeau</i>, her little shoes, even her silk stockings. They were like children, very lovable, grown-up children, and Tod knew that he was one of them.

“Ah, Monsieur Tod, but you are très, très gentil, très généreux.”“Am I?” said Tod. “But why? I have never enjoyed myself like this before.”

He was looking at Claire with eyes of wonder and of tenderness.

“You will spoil her, monsieur,” said André roguishly.

Madame snubbed him.

“Love never spoilt a good woman, my son. Take that to heart.”

Then Claire escaped.

“Tod is hungry,” she asserted.

“I’m not,” said her man.

“But you must be, because I am,” and she laughed back at them from the doorway. “I shall be back in one moment, and I will help to get some supper.”

“It is ready,” Josephine called after her: “we waited for you.”

Tod wandered out into the orchard, because the sunset was there and a blackbird was singing, and all his happiness seemed part of the sunset and the green leaves and the blackbird’s song. Tod had never thought that life could be so good.He idled to and fro under the apple trees, filled to the lips with a precious and innocent tenderness, a simple man whose very simplicity was his strength.

Presently he heard Claire calling him.

“Tod! Tod!”

He turned and met her where a red rose bush grew by the garden gate, and Tod stood still. She had changed her new frock and put on her simple black working skirt, a white blouse and the little apron with the blue pansies. It was the Claire of all his memories, the Claire of the schoolhouse, the Claire who had won him in her daintily shabby clothes.

“Dear God!” said Tod, “nothing will ever be better than that. It’s the woman, not the clothes.”<b>Chapter Forty-six</b>

One summer afternoon Mr. Julian Burgetrode met the Channel boat at Folkestone.

Standing behind a crane, a friend unseen, he watched the crowd on the boat’s deck and their sheep’s progress towards the gangway. Mr. Burgetrode had to look elsewhere for Tod and for Tod’s wife, for they were not among the people who pushed and struggled to extricate handbags and suit-cases.

“Silly fools,” he said, “and to think we had an army!”

Then he caught sight of Tod, Tod standing a little apart and absorbed in an affectionate argument with a little woman in black, a charmingly sturdy little woman who looked up into Tod’s face and maintained a grip of her handbag.

“I <i>will</i> carry it,” Claire was saying; “I am not so helpless.”

It was Tod who gave in, and like a big, amused, and a devoted boy, but Mr. Burgetrode was not interested in Tod; his attention was absorbed by Tod’s French wife. The Colossus had very keen sight, and he watched this little woman in black while she was wholly unconscious of being observed. He had an impression of a full round face, pale, but not with the pallor of Paris, a wreath of soft brown hair, two big grey eyes, a mouth that was both generous and shrewd, and a very firm little chin. She was dressed quite simply in that costume that had been bought at Lille before the war, and the black velour cloak, and the hat that had come from Eu.She was one of those women who combine animation and repose, who look both fragile and strong. Mr. Burgetrode had a full view of her face as she talked to Tod, and it made him think of soft white light, pure and yet sensitively passionate.

“French,” said Mr. Burgetrode to himself, “but yet not the France of Paris. A little fair girl with a white throat and a passion for one man in those grey eyes of hers. A very great passion perhaps. And she understands.”

The crowd poured off the boat, and Tod was shepherding Claire towards the gangway, making a sort of sacred space about her. Mr. Burgetrode saw him glance over his shoulder, and then set his back squarely and forcibly against the energetic person of a bullet-headed Englishman who was thinking of nothing but a seat in the luncheon-car. Tod held the man back till Claire was on the gangway. Mr. Burgetrode liked the squareness and the determination of Tod’s back.

He went forward hat in hand to meet them.

“Hallo, sir! I hadn’t seen you. It’s very good of you to come and meet us.”

Mr. Burgetrode was smiling.

“So here you are. And this is my adopted daughter? I am very charmed to welcome you, madame.”Mr. Burgetrode spoke excellent French, and for the moment he had something of the manner of a Frenchman. Standing there bareheaded, he held out a hand to Claire.

“It is Mr. Burgetrode,” said Tod.

She gave him a little smile, and then looked straight at Julian Burgetrode. Their hands met. Claire’s eyes and face were as open as the light to him; he was Tod’s big friend, and hers, a cleverer man than Tod, she knew that at once; but great with the same greatness, so she thought. She showed no fear of Julian Burgetrode, no knowledge of the necessity in an attitude that should please him. It was possible that he might be jealous of her. She smiled that aside.

“I have wished so much to see you, monsieur.”

Mr. Burgetrode still held her hand. He liked her simplicity, her unaffectedness, her dignity that was like the dignity of a child. She had come to a strange land, bravely, without tears, trusting a man. And he, too, was trusted. The bigness of him offered its homage.

“Well, now you see me, Madame Claire. Is it a surprise for both of us? I think I have the right to the greater pleasure.”

The woman in her answered the more subtle man in him.

“You will have no cause to be afraid of me, monsieur.”“My dear child!” said Mr. Burgetrode with unusual emotion; “Tod, you scoundrel, why didn’t you tell me?”

Tod looked puzzled.

“What, sir?”

A sudden smile passed between Claire and Mr. Burgetrode.

“Of course you told me! I beg your pardon. I didn’t visualise it, that’s all.”

They followed the porter and the luggage along the pier, Claire walking between the two men, and looking with eyes of amazement at all those people who seemed in so savage and helpless a hurry.

“How droll,” she said with a glance at Mr. Burgetrode, “I expected England to be so calm.”

“Calm!” echoed the great man; “no, we are not calm. We are much too discontented.”

Tod walked on to speak to the porter.

“My husband is not discontented,” said Claire.Mr. Burgetrode’s fierce blue eyes softened when he looked at this little woman.

“He is a very unusual man.”

“He is the best and most generous man in the world,” she said, and then with a passionately serious light in her grey eyes, “it is my affair that he shall always be happy.”

Tod came back to them.

“There’s a big crowd in the Customs.”

“No hurry,” said Mr. Burgetrode. “I booked three seats on the luncheon-car.”

“Will they want to open my box and disarrange all my pretty things?”

“Smile at them, madame,” said Mr. Burgetrode with an air of mischief. “Tod, you had better leave it to your wife.”

Julian Burgetrode had yet another opportunity of watching these two children of his while they were dealing with a bored and rather irritable official. Tod chose to act as spokesman. Claire stood at his side, looking up at him with eyes of adoration, motherliness, pride.

“I’m asking you if you have anything to declare, sir. It’s no use——”“I haven’t anything; but my wife——”

“Open the lady’s trunk,” said the man brusquely.

Claire looked at Tod.

“What does he say?”

“He wishes to see your trunk?”

“Quelle horreur!” said Claire.

She took a little bunch of keys out of her vanity-bag and turned to the official.

“Monsieur,” she began, using for the first time her quaint academic English, “I am married, one week. I have one Paris costume, three silk petticoats, my lingerie——”

The man stared at her. She was preparing to unlock her trunk.

“You will not disarrange all my pretty things, monsieur, please?”

Mr. Burgetrode saw that something had happened to the official. His bored face broadened to a smile. He looked at Tod and chalked the luggage.“That’s all right, sir. I won’t bother the lady.”

“You can put your keys back, ma chérie,” said Tod.

Claire thanked the official.

“It is very agreeable of you, monsieur. I have told you the truth; it is just my little trousseau.”

“Don’t mention it, madam,” said the man.

Mr. Burgetrode swept them away. He was laughing somewhere in the depths of those fierce eyes of his.

“I was wrong,” he said to himself, “quite wrong. I shall never be jealous of this child.”
