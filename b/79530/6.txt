Was not civil discipline as necessary as military discipline? Could a committee of battalion cooks have schemed and carried through a big offensive? Were not the best talkers often the worst fighters, and the least dependable of men? Tod had been living with realities. He had learnt the great lesson, that the very nature of man has a way of upsetting the most elaborate and beautiful theories. You can use workmen to build a cathedral, but you cannot use the workmen’s backs in place of stones. You can set a hundred men running a race, but the whole hundred will not breast the tape together. Life was still a race. Penalise the speedy men, and the thing would become a farce, a disgraceful strolling match for loafers. Nature would put up her starting pistol, and go in search of a better strain, of new men. Civilisation’s business was to see that there was a fair start for all, and no fouling.

In wandering over those wet fields Tod came upon a little orchard close to a long rambling red-roofed house whose eyes still seemed to be staring in a blind and frightened way at certain horrors that had been enacted close to it. The orchard had been a German howitzer position. A scattering of poplars and willows in the surrounding hedgerows had screened it well, but chance or some English airmen had spotted those squat black cannon, and death had come out of the grey sky.

The orchard was all brown with shell holes and scattered earth. Half the trees had been smashed. One big howitzer lay wallowing in a crater like some huge black beast dead and swollen. A second had been caught in the act of making off towards the road, and stood where it had been left, its snout cocked in the air as though it had been squealing in terror.The ground round about was littered with wicker cases, fuse boxes, shells, German helmets, overcoats, gas-masks, a torn and bloody tunic. Two or three dead Germans still sprawled under the hedge. Tod could picture the fierce intensity of that battle scene. The sudden plunging shells, the crash of the explosives, the spurts of dirt and smoke, the shrinking desperate men drifting away from their smothered guns, the dead left lying. What a glimpse of the last judgment on the men who had conceived and created the war!

Tod sat down on a wooden case. He was used to such scenes. This smashed German battery was a thing of the past, and Tod’s thoughts were in the future.

He had a packet of Mr. Burgetrode’s letters in his pocket, and he wanted to read them again at his leisure. Some of them were in answer to the scrawls he had sent to this big friend, the curt, frank outpourings of a soldier to a man who understood.

There were many passages in Julian Burgetrode’s letters that spoke as an answering voice to Tod’s thoughts.

“If you think you are coming back to a new heaven and a new earth, my son, get rid of that idea at once. We are going forward, but I think that we shall go forward in a way very different from what most people imagine. Read French history as it concerns a period like our own.And don’t think that England has been chastened, as the best of you have been chastened out there. It hasn’t. The grocer is still stronger than God.”

“Keep away from the people who are always calling on the State to do this and to do that. Don’t be befogged by that word ‘State.’ Be ready to take your coat off and fell your own trees.”

“After all, what is the ‘State’ to which these good people are always appealing? Such a State is nothing but officialdom. And who is your official? “The country that sits down and waits for someone to hand it its spade, bring it its dinner, and wipe its unsweated forehead, is going to the devil.”

“Tod, my boy, you must know that men have to be managed and to be moved. Most of them are just marionettes. Necessity is the great wire. Cut it and you will stop the vital current to the whole complex scheme. And an intoxicated mob of fools will be fighting like starving dogs.”

“We are still creatures of necessity, not democratic demigods. We have created our scheme of civilisation, and we have got to live with it; it’s our Sphinx, our Master. “Nature is not going to let us all wallow in a social elysium just yet. We have got to be up and doing, every man of us. Those who sit under the apple tree and smoke and wait for Mr. Peasgood Nonsuch to climb up and throw down the apples won’t get ’em. They don’t deserve to get ’em.”

Tod smiled over these letters of Mr. Burgetrode’s. They were so fresh, so pungent, so vigorous, and they echoed many of the grim facts that Tod has discovered during the war. He had learnt how to father a battalion, that little world of men in miniature.

And then he re-read Julian Burgetrode’s last letter.

“So the Bloody Business is nearly over. It has been the most religious thing in all history. The German organisation should have won. It was beaten by something higher and finer than mere organisation. Character, the good heart of the plain man.

Tod put those letters away, and began to think of other things. In this battered orchard under the grey of the November sky, his thoughts went back to Myonville and Claire—sturdy, grey-eyed, deep-bosomed Claire. That other life was claiming him again. He saw himself going back to England, to the woman who inspired in him nothing but a profound distrust. He had no letter from Millie in his pocket. “Be big. Work is more lasting and more satisfying than love.”

For one day at least Mr. Julian Burgetrode ceased to control anything. He put on the oldest clothes he could find, a shabby brown Norfolk jacket, and a pair of old grey knickers and grey stockings. The athlete in him showed when he put on such clothes, and he looked the long, gaunt, iron-muscled Viking that he was, a man of many adventures and of many victories. And yesterday he had won a victory over himself.

Tod came in from his hotel to breakfast with him, fresh-faced, clean-eyed, carrying himself with a quiet yet boyish dignity. He did not appear to notice Mr. Burgetrode’s clothes, for with Mr. Burgetrode clothes did not matter. He would have dominated and effaced a collection of rags, for the older his clothes were, the better he looked in them.

But he noticed Tod’s.

“Hallo, a new tunic and nice pink breeches!”

Mr. Burgetrode’s eyes approved of Tod, and approved of him with fierce affection. It was the same calm, frank-faced Claire, with her mouth and her grey eyes, more serious, perhaps a little sad. A pair of clean shoes were standing against the wall. Claire picked them up, entered the kitchen, and saw Tod.She dropped the shoes on a chair, and stood looking at him with round grey eyes.

“Monsieur—you!”

And Tod, the man, did not move, for he saw something that his dream had never shown him. Claire was afraid.

She sat down suddenly at the table, her elbows resting upon it, her little fists clenched under her chin. Her eyes looked straight at Tod.

“Why have you come back?”

There was a fierceness in her voice, an attack in the abrupt words she threw at him.

Tod took his chair and sat down opposite her, his elbows also on the table.

“Because I have a right to come back.”

“Why?”

“I am free. I am no longer married.”The eyes opposite him gave a kind of flicker, like a light blown by the wind. She did not say anything, but sat there, waiting.

Tod felt in his coat pocket, brought out the little brown Testament and laid it on the table between them.

“You remember?” he asked.

She bowed her head slightly.

“I remember, monsieur.”

“You were never wrong.”

The words were almost inaudible. Her fists had unclenched themselves, and her hands were curved against her cheeks. She was looking at the table now, and not at Tod, and her eyes were heavy with something deeper than fear.

“I thought I should never come back,” he went on. “Then life changed for me. And yet—I had to wait. The woman I had married had given herself to another man. It had happened before I met you, but I had only guessed it then.”She stretched out a hand and fingered the little brown book.

“You did not—not love her, monsieur?”

“I did not love her.”

“Never?”

“Once, when I was a boy and did not know.”

She bent her head a little lower, to hide the wondering and melting tenderness of her face.

“It was very hard for you, monsieur.”

One of Tod’s brown hands reached out and covered her hand that was lying on the brown book.

“Claire, I am free.”

He felt a little trembling in the hand beneath his. And the love in Tod blazed out and over her. He half rose, and leant across the table.

“Oh, beloved! is it possible that you care a little? All these months I have been thinking and waiting for this day and for you. It’s just a year ago.”“A year ago——”

She hung her head, a woman trembling on the edge of her exultation, the exultation of surrender.

“I rushed back. I couldn’t help it. You wouldn’t come down.”

“I couldn’t,” she said.

“But you gave me this. Oh, ma chérie, I love you—I love you.”

She raised her head suddenly and looked right into his eyes.

“I went to the school—after you had gone. I do not know what I taught the children. I did not care.”

“Claire!”

“Yes, it is true. I knew you would never come back to me again, that you could never come back—unless—and I am a woman, Tod!”

Tod bent over the table and took her face between his hands. There was something sacramental in the great tenderness with which he touched her. And thus they remained for a moment, Claire’s hands folded over his wrists, her face dreaming up to his, her grey eyes the eyes of a woman who gives herself utterly and without fear.“God bless you for ever and ever,” said Tod.

And then he drew back a little.

“Claire!”

Her eyes waited.

“Will you marry me? Listen. I am English, you are French; my work is in England. Would you go with me?”

She smiled up at him.

“I can speak a little English, mon chéri.”

Tod kissed her.<b>Chapter Forty-three</b>

Old Lorellard was laughing. He loved young people and the exciting and romantic things that happen when a man is young. André, slouching along with his hands in his pockets, regarded the family with eyes of amusement. André had seen the world.

“Listen!” said Claire. “They are coming!”

Claire had drawn aside and was standing with her back to the window, her hands resting on the sill—a softly radiant Claire, with shy eyes that looked at everything but Tod. Tod stood squarely in the middle of the kitchen, showing something of the air of a man ready to receive a deputation, half-smiling, half-solemn, but wholly proud.

The Lorellards had no thought of being formal. They were warm-hearted French folk, and they enveloped Tod with the vivacity of the French who can be gay and intimate without touching a note that is familiarly vulgar.

“Ah, Monsieur le Colonel!”

“What a charming compliment you pay us!”

“Oh, la la! These soldiers are wonderful men!”

“Where are you staying, monsieur? At Abbeville? But Monsieur’s old room is at his service.”

But Claire had slipped away, and no one had noticed it save André, who was sufficiently uninvolved to notice everything. The Lorellard family looked wisely and humorously puzzled.

“But she was here a moment ago!”

“You were all talking so hard,” said André, “and Claire seems to think it is quieter in the orchard.”The family looked at Tod, and Tod found himself faced with a public declaration.

The family had accepted the position of an audience, and Tod had to go on.

“I came back to Myonville because, by the English law, I am to-day a free man. I should have asked Claire to marry me a year ago if I had been free then. Monsieur Lorellard, as is only right and fair, I have brought all the necessary documents and proofs with me.”

Old Lorellard nodded, smiled and grunted something that was indistinguishable.

“I wish for the attendance to-morrow of Monsieur le Maire, a notary and an interpreter, so that everything can be examined properly.”

Then old Lorellard interrupted him.

“Monsieur, let the formalities wait. We trust you with all our hearts. Josephine, a bottle of wine.”

Josephine went to the cellar for the wine while madame collected the proper number of glasses.The three men were fraternising round the table, the two Frenchmen smoking Tod’s cigarettes, when Josephine returned with the wine.

Old Lorellard looked round the room.

Josephine was away quite a long time, and the Lorellards and Tod talked about the war. The Frenchmen still seemed fascinated by it, and by the epic of justice of those last hundred days. Tod’s face was shadowed with an almost sacred tenderness.

“I understand everything,” he said. “I have thought about everything all these months. And I will answer you, Claire, as it is right that you should be answered, not by throwing my love at you, dear, but with the truth,the truth that can be so convincing.”

Tod paused for a moment, and glanced at Claire. She was still the calm, courageous little woman, holding aloof for his sake, but Tod saw that her eyes had softened.

Claire gave a little shiver, a shiver of inward exultation.

“The English colonel had come back to marry little Claire Vignard.”
