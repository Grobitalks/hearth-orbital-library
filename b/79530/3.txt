Someone else in Horrox’s shop was interested in Tod’s love affair. Horrox’s junior employees lived in. It was a very handsome ring, and it absorbed all the money that he had saved for a summer holiday; but since Shaston had become a place of delicious enchantment Tod thought no more of holidays.

He had become absurdly and delightfully old of a sudden, a little, solemn John Bull carrying new and pleasant responsibilities. Life had undreamt-of problems and interests, and finance ceased to be a matter of no importance. Tod took a survey of his finances, and he began to wonder how much he would be worth to Mr. Rathbone Cragg at the end of another year. Tod began to consider economies. The moment had arrived when he found time to dawdle in front of furniture shops, and to understand Arthur Mossup’s preoccupation over the quest of a house. The generous and innocent Tod explained his friend’s depression by wondering whether he was rubbing his own happiness into the raw of some discontent, and whether Bob Twine yearned greatly for a home of his own.“Why don’t you get married and settle down?”

Twine stared, swallowed something, and laughed.

“I’m not made that way, old chap, and I haven’t the money.”

“But you’ll want to get married some day.”

“Talk about something else,” said Twine flippantly. “Wish I was in the army, somewhere east of Suez. God, that chap Kipling knows a bit.”

He threw the fag end of his cigarette into an empty grate, and became boisterously cheerful.

“Well, kid, you’ll be the solemn father of a family in ten years. Taking ’em down to paddle, and teaching ’em to swim. Queer, isn’t it, and you’re only a kid yourself. But—damn it—I wish you the best of luck, and I hope the girl knows you deserve it. Think I’ll be toddling. So long.”

Tod, holding a hammer in one hand and a box of tin-tacks in the other, watched the swarthy man slouch down the path.

“Poor old Bob,” he thought; “he wants a home and won’t say so.”

Twine was cursing women and himself.“I ought to have told him, but, damn it—how could I? He’s like a blessed cherub blowing about with a hammer and a box of nails. Happy? My God! Oh, you little devil, I hope you’ll play game by him.”

A week later Tod was married. It was a quiet wedding, not nearly so decorative as Millie Hornblower would have desired. Her mother was the only relative who attended it, and Tod wondered why Mrs. Hornblower smelt of drink. The two bridesmaids came from Horrox’s, and Mr. Mann of the Furnishing Department gave Miss Hornblower away.

Old Tom Lavender served as Tod’s best man. Millie had said that he was far too old for it, but Tod had proved obstinate, for Tom Lavender had a younger heart than most young men. Uncle Remus was not there, and he sent no present. And away in the north the great Mr. Burgetrode was as silent as death.

The honeymoon lasted four days, Tod not caring to leave the business to Mr. Love and the garage boy. They spent the days at Maidenhead, Millie Hornblower’s choice, and they did much lolling in a punt on the river, a punt that refused to humour Tod’s first activities with a punt pole. But who cared about progress? There were over-hanging and shadowy trees, cushions, and a girl, a girl like a soft, white, sensuous flower with lips tinted red.

For the first six months of his marriage Tod was supremely happy. He had married a highly sexed young woman, a young woman who woke the dormant man in him and taught him to delight in new physical experiences.During those months Tod was the most passionate and uxorious of young husbands, and his wife’s sensual frankness rather pleased him. She wanted him and she wanted him with passion. His habits changed. His early morning swim became a thing of the past. He ceased to play games, for sex was the game of the moment, and Millie played it in all its variations. But she did not want children.

“Just you and me together—for a while—Toddy. Besides, we can’t afford them.”

They couldn’t.

The fact became very obvious to Tod. “Carnarvon” was a thirsty little house, and Tod had begun to glimpse his wife’s social ambitions. He was astonished at the number of things that appeared to be required for the efficient functioning of a genteel house. They had no piano, but a piano seemed to be a sign and symbol of grace, even though Millie could not play it. People were beginning to call, and Millie gave little afternoon bridge parties, and the cakes these people ate, and the cost of them, worried Tod. His wife’s clothes became a problem. Two good suits were sufficient for Tod, but the hunger of a woman’s wardrobe appeared to be insatiable. She wheedled Tod into sundry extravagances; but that was not the end of the tale, for when the finery had been bought, it had to be displayed. Tod found himself taking stalls at the local theatre and going there in evening dress. He even discovered himself in church, walking up the aisle with a young woman who rustled, a young woman who would never have set foot in the place had she been dressed in sackcloth.That red mouth and those plump white arms dominated him during the first months of his marriage. He was proud of his wife, and rather proud of “Carnarvon,” and the calceolarias and the dog, and the maid in white cap and apron.

At the end of six months Tod began “doing figures.” The business was prospering, but Tod realised that its prosperity was not commensurate with Millie’s ideas upon expenditure.

Tod saw that he would have to expand.

He travelled up to town and saw the Semite of Wardour Street, produced figures, and stated his reasons for believing in a possible expansion of his venture. The result was the addition of two more taxis to Tod’s stud, supplied on a system of deferred payments; and the hiring of two more drivers. Tod had a weekly advertisement in the <i>Shaston Gazette</i>. The red-fronted garage in Duke Street had an air of industrious prosperity, and the garage boy began to call him “sir.”

But the surest proof of Tod’s success was the coming of Mr. Pumpelly. He was a succulent and cheerful old Christian with a facial resemblance to Father Christmas, and a partiality for loud check suits. Mr. Pumpelly had been the principal jobmaster in Shaston before the birth of the autocar, and he looked more the man of the horse than of the machine. Geniality was his great weapon. “Good old Josh—one of the best, do anything for anybody.” That was how Mr. Pumpelly glimpsed himself, and there was a bland and worldly cunning under his rosy good humour, a shrewdness that stood broad-bellied in the sunlight, and smiled.Pumpelly strolled into Tod’s garage, and looked round with an air of obvious approval, and then discovered Tod, Tod who had just booked an order that spelt fifty pounds.

“Morning, Mr. Western. Nice little place you’ve got here.”

“It’s not bad,” said Tod.

“I was passing, and I dropped in to see whether you happened to have a couple of 700 by 85 Avons in stock. I’m out; they’re wanted urgently.”

“Afraid I haven’t. I could let you have a pair of Speedwells.”

“No thanks. Don’t touch ’em. This your office, is it? Mind me strolling in?”

Mr. Pumpelly strolled in with the genial casualness of a friend. He looked at everything with interest, even at Tod’s stool. Then he lit a cigarette.

“I’ll wire my London agents. Yes, a snug little place, and quite handy.”

And suddenly Mr. Pumpelly’s face beamed with the light of inspiration.

“Look here, Western, here’s an idea. Why shouldn’t we two fix up Shaston between us? We’d tap different ends, and we’d keep other people out. It’s an idea, ain’t it?”“You mean we should go into partnership?” said Tod.

“That’s it. Why not?”

There was something in Tod that did not like Mr. Pumpelly, and he made one of those swift decisions that was to prove catastrophic so far as his future was concerned.

“Thanks, I prefer being on my own, but I’m obliged for the suggestion.”

“Think it over,” said Mr. Pumpelly with fatherly impressiveness; “it would be a good thing for you.”

Tod thought it over, and two days later he wrote a private letter to Mr. Pumpelly, refusing the offer of partnership. Mr. Pumpelly showed the letter to a nasty, dark, dapper little fellow who had become interested in Mr. Pumpelly’s business and was acting as manager.

“He’s one of the independent sort, Britt.”

Mr. Charles Britt passed a dirty forefinger across his little black moustache.

“If he wants competition he’ll get it. I didn’t work five years with the Solomons for nothing.”<b>Chapter Twenty-one</b>

So Tod’s boyhood died. It just faded away and left him, and he hardly noticed that it had gone, and that it lay in the grave of his dead mother. With it had passed all that was fine and adventurous and free in Tod, that light of the morning look, the spirit of the boy bathing in the sea.

He was a preoccupied young man, sometimes with a frown on his face, and an air of sulky puzzlement. The business had begun to be irritating; it absorbed the whole of Tod, was never satisfied, kept him awake at night, and yet—like a fractious baby—was none the better for all the fussiness lavished on it. All sorts of exasperating things happened, and they happened in a way that made Tod feel very angry and very helpless.

Tod had become commercialised. Delilah had shorn the young Sampson, and he had become the slave of the Philistines. The curse of the civilisation of the West was upon him, the superstition that man was born to make things, sell things, and to accumulate money, that nothing mattered but money. And Tod was getting himself tangled up in the system just as thousands of other young men get entangled in it and come to believe that modern commercialism has been and is and will be for ever.

“Carnarvon” drove him daily into the scuffle with little half-petulant, half-wheedling cries.

“Oh, Toddy, we must have a Chesterfield sofa.”Or “Clara Brown has got one of those new musquash coats.”

Display had meant expansion, and expansion had brought Tod into the presence of the economic god—Competition.

Mr. Julian Burgetrode could have lectured Tod on the subject of competition. He would have said that it was part of Nature, a heritage inherent in man, even wrapped up in his subconscious soul. It was emulation, virile pride, the spirit of adventure, the beads and feathers of the amorous savage, the jewel in the hilt of the sword of the hero. He would have told Tod that the wrong sort of people had got hold of the competition spirit. In other days there had been the Competition of Prophets, the Competition of Knights, the Competition of Poets, Painters and Scholars, the Competition of Cities over something finer than a herd of swine, the Competition of Builders in the soaring spires of their churches. These were noble emulations, emulations that the commercial man had trodden into the hog-wash of his sty. Mr. Julian Burgetrode had a ferocious contempt for the purely commercial man, and for the spirit of industrialism of nineteenth-century England. He had fought his way to the summit of things, he was a great captain of industry, he had explored the great barrack from cellar to roof. And that is what he called it: “A barrack.” “It won’t last,” he wrote to an American friend, “and if we try to make it last, it will come down and bury us. And I’m not so sure that I shall not be one of the first to get at the rotten brickwork with a pick.”

There were other sayings of Mr. Burgetrode’s that might have reminded Tod of his own father.“We have created another sphinx and another riddle. The industrial revolution and our concentration on purely material things made us overpopulate ourselves with a swarm of slaves. The slaves are waking up, only to find that we are still the slaves of the god we have created. Too many millions of people—and food to be got for all! Our materialism is a chain round our necks. No wonder the Sphinx grins like a cynic.”

“Competition, yes—it’s necessary—as things are. It may be brutal and sordid, but it is the plain man’s only defence against greedy commercialists. These gentlemen have to use their knives on each other. Thanks to competition, they cannot give all their attention to cutting their pounds of flesh off the poor ass.”

“Trusts and Combines. There should be no trust or combine that is not state-owned for the benefit of everybody. I would have all trust magnates treated as highwaymen or pirates.”

“The joy in the job. We have got to get back to that, or perish.”

Tod Western was just beginning to butt his head blindly against all these problems, like a young man who was conscious of obstacles—and rivals. Man does a lot of butting before he learns to stand still, to reflect and to think. Most men can do nothing but butt all their lives, till Death comes behind and kicks them into oblivion. And Tod was getting well down and into the thick of it; but he played fair; that was his great disadvantage.He was generous to his wife, and she wasted his money and worried him with her petty extravagances.

He trusted Love his mechanic, and Love robbed him in ingenious little ways that Tod was too big to notice.

He trusted his two new drivers, and they too robbed him, filching petrol, tools and oil, for Tod was an “employer,” and there is a certain sort of new citizen who thinks that it is his right to perpetrate any meanness upon the man who employs him.

These were minor matters. Tod did not notice them, for his consciousness was absorbed in sensing a vague obstruction that baffled the expansion of his business.

He began to remark all sorts of queer things.

None of the leading hotels ever ’phoned to Western’s garage for a car.

He had been unable to get himself appointed as agent by any of the bigger manufacturing firms, and he inferred that old Pumpelly had sucked that melon dry.

Nor was Tod satisfied with the work his taxis did at the station. So dissatisfied was he during the second summer that he played private investigator, and made the curious discovery that the porters appeared to hail a Pumpelly taxi before one of Western’s.His own drivers loafed in their seats, and did not seem concerned over the possible loss of fares and tips, but Tod saw that it was bad business if Pumpelly did three journeys to his two. And Pumpelly’s cars were more smartly kept than Tod’s; there was no doubt about it all.

Tod tackled his men. He was generous and hated accusing other men of slackness, but he had yet to learn that there is a type of man that responds only to necessity and fear.

“Look here, you chaps, I want you to be brisker. What about that dirty radiator? Take out your cushions, Smith, and let’s have a look at them.”

Smith’s cushions were a disgrace, and he roused Tod’s bile by being insolent over it.

“Look here, Smith, if you’ve got no conscience, find one, or I’ll sack you.”

Smith found a lick-and-a-scrape apology for a conscience, but he made up his mind to do Tod down. He was that sort of man.

Tod happened to be out on the London Road one evening, and he saw a big new boarding standing on a bank above the road. It warned tourists to Shaston that Pumpelly’s Garage was the best, and the only garage usable by a gentleman who respected his car. In fact Mr. Pumpelly lifted his leg over Tod and any other rival in the town. He was the leading agent for everything; he kept expert mechanics, even a consulting engineer. And Tod was annoyed.He had an even larger board erected higher up the London Road, so that Western’s should be read before Pumpelly’s.

About that time Tod had his first quarrel with his wife, and like most first quarrels it was an absurd affair. Millie had indulged in some particular bit of selfish extravagance, and the arrival of the bill coincided with the rise in the level of Tod’s financial worries.

“It isn’t sport,” he told her hotly. “Haven’t I enough worry without this gorgeous waste of money?”

Millie passed from sulkiness to a fine, ranting fury, and from fury to tears, and the fool man was beaten when that dear dew ran down into her red mouth. It was the first time she had used the device against Tod, and he surrendered instantly on that occasion.

There was a reconciliation of sorts, and they sat on the sofa with Tod’s arm round his wife’s waist, and he tried to explain to her that he was puzzled and worried. And Millie was vaguely alarmed. She had regarded the business as booming, and Tod had never hinted at the malign influence that appeared to be checking its expansion.

“Of course it’s old Pumpelly,” she said.

“But there is plenty of room for both of us. I can’t see myself going out to spoil another man’s pitch.”Tod’s wife had far more cunning than her man, and far more of the suspicious business spirit, the spirit that applauds honesty when honesty happens to pay.

“It’s business,” she told him; “any fool could tell you that. I’ll find out.”

She was as good as her word, and she discovered more in a week than Tod would have discovered in a year. Intuition helped her, the intuition of a young woman who had no false ideas on public morality, and who could put a plump forefinger unerringly on the sore that was eating into Western’s prosperity. Moreover, she had certain gossips and friends, and among them the wives of the local station-master and of a manager of one of the smaller hotels. They told her just what she had expected them to tell her. It was all so simple and so obvious that she thought Tod a fool for not having used the same methods of persuasion, and she was amazed with him for his lack of “smartness.”

“You are a kid,” she said a little contemptuously; “you have got a lot to learn, Toddy.”

“What’s wrong?” he asked good-temperedly.

“You don’t look after your own interests. Suppose you’ve gone round the hotels?”

“I sent them my card.”

She tittered, a vulgar little expression of scorn.“Your card! You ought to have gone and seen all the hotel porters.”

“What for?”

“Who rings up for taxis? Why, the hotel porters, of course! Old Pumpelly tips them all, and that’s why he gets the orders.”

“I don’t believe it,” said Tod.

“What d’you mean?” said Tod, with the battle-light in his blue eyes.

Tod flared up as his father had flared up before him.“Go round and bribe people! No, thank you. I’m hanged if I’ll do it.”

“You’re a fool,” she said tartly; “it’s money that counts.”

Mr. Charlie Britt, the nasty, dark, dapper little fellow, now junior partner of the firm of Pumpelly & Britt, had set out to smash Western’s Garage. He had no spite against Tod as Tod, but Britt was combative and greedy, and he belonged to the cut-throat school of business men who believed in showing no mercy, and in tolerating no rival, if that rival could be eliminated. Charles Britt’s methods were the methods of the garrotter. Even political economists have admitted that business is non-moral and that the man who sets out to be nothing but a trader has logic on his side when he shuts up his Sunday conscience in his safe and uses his wits as a wrestler uses his cunning. But Britt was one of those men who work in the dark. He met Tod once or twice and was particularly pleasant and polite to him, and Tod’s nature was too generous and open for him to suspect of deliberately fouling him.

Western’s Garage had a run of bad luck. Two of Tod’s taxis were out of action during the summer’s busiest month, one with a seized engine, the other with a badly broken back axle. Smith was the offender in the matter of the seized engine, a deliberate offender perhaps, and Tod sacked him, but the mischief was done. Then Tod had trouble with a private owner whose car was said to have lost a couple of electric headlights in Western’s garage.The owner was so positive and so combative that Tod paid him the value of the headlights, but the incident worried him more than the loss of the money. He hated suspecting people, especially men who worked with and about him every day of the week. He spoke to Love about it, and Love’s self-assurance frothed like beer.

“I have an idea where they went all right. No, it wasn’t Sid Smith. D’you remember a tourin’ car with a dirty little Belgian chofer—stayed ’ere two nights? I guess the Belgian had ’em.”

Tod had to be satisfied with that.

The hoped-for expansion did not develop, and Tod began to realise that he was living beyond his means. He had his monthly payments to make to the Semite of Wardour Street, and these payments competed with the demands of “Carnarvon.” Tod saw that the obvious and sensible thing to do was to cut down his domestic expenses, concentrate everything on the business, and to bide his time.

He walked home one evening, having made up his mind that Millie would have to economise, and for the first time since his marriage he found himself driven into a critical attitude towards his wife. Something within him foresaw a “scene,” and Tod hated scenes, and in hating them, asked himself why they should be necessary. Surely it was his wife’s duty to back him up, and not to pout those red lips of hers whenever he tried to talk about money?And suddenly he became aware of the stranger in his wife, a woman whom he did not know. Tod had been very much the slave of the business during the past year; he had met Millie across the table, behaved as the natural man to the natural woman, and yet he felt that they had drifted apart. He had glimpses of Millie as a golden-haired young woman floating about in a blue or cerise-coloured knitted coat, playing tennis, eating chocolates, reading cheap novels, gadding about to other women’s houses, and receiving other women into hers. She was out a very great deal, and knew people whom Tod had never met. “Same as me. I can’t keep away from the women, old chap; they’re so gorgeous. And Millie Western is just like me—with a difference. She can’t keep away from the men, the men who can bite. But she’s a liar, I’m not a liar.”

Arthur Mossup looked worried, and worry had tended to become a fixed factor in his life during the last six months. Like Tod, he was struggling in the world of unbridled competition, and the little business was like a half stifled plant making a pathetic struggle to get at the light and the public. Mossup was hard up, desperately hard up, and Olive was expecting a baby. The good fellow was not only a much harassed draper but a household drudge, and his wife adored him.

“Arthur’s such a dear,” she told Twine. “If only we could have a little bit of luck!”

“You deserve it,” he said, and added savagely, “some day I’ll go round with a red flag and a hatchet.”He left half a sovereign in Mossup’s pocket after they had come near quarrelling over that piece of gold.

“Take it, you silly blighter. What do I want it for? It would only be thrown away.”

“You’re a good chap, Twine; you don’t do yourself justice.”

“Don’t I? You go and ask some of the girls.”

Now poor Mossup’s worries culminated in a crisis that caught his pale pride shivering like a half starved saint in a winter wind. Olive had given birth to a boy; she had been very near death in the anguish of that night, and her husband had about five shillings in his cashbox. They were in debt, too, to the tradesmen, and Olive wanted coal and delicacies. Poor Mossup’s heart was sore.

Tod was smoking his pipe in his den at “Carnarvon” and reading a trade journal, when he heard the door-bell ring. It was the maid’s evening out, and Millie was posing on the sofa with one of her headaches, so Tod went to the front door. He found Arthur Mossup standing there, a melancholy figure in the raw murk of a November evening, his spectacles wet with rain and his seedy old black mackintosh flapping open at the throat.

“Hallo, Arthur! Come in.”

Mossup was nervous and confidential.
