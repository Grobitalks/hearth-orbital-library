Yet this plan of the great man was overset by circumstances that were stronger than Mr.Burgetrode, and he—like Tod—was to be but a fierce striving figure in the huge fresco of a world upheaval, a figure out of the brain of Michael Angelo the great. Julian Burgetrode met the inevitable, and smiled at it with some of the pride of a father. The man he had chosen could but prove himself a man.

In the sunlight of an August morning these two met on the white steps of the hotel; Tod with a deep light in his eyes and the tense fire of youth touched with a new heroism; Mr. Burgetrode with the day’s paper in his hand, and a kind of grimness about his mouth.

He looked at Tod, and he knew at once what Tod had come to say.

“Well, young Ajax?”

“I must go,” said Tod; “I wanted to come and tell you, sir. It’s right, isn’t it, even if I have to throw up everything? I am going round to the drill hall to sign on.”

Mr. Burgetrode held out a hand to Tod.

“Ajax couldn’t do anything else, boy. You’re right, utterly right. But when you come back, I shall want you to come to me.”

“I’ll come,” said Tod, much moved; “but there’s just one thing.”

“Out with it, boy. What is worrying you?”“My wife,” said Tod. “I have some money left, but it’s capital; and army pay won’t keep a wife. If I handed you the money, sir?”

Mr. Burgetrode’s eyes had lost all their fierceness.

“I’ll see to your wife, Tod; she shan’t suffer.”

“God bless you, sir,” said Tod; “I don’t suppose I shall have much trouble with the doctor.”

“But the Germans will with you,” said Mr. Burgetrode to himself. “Ave triumphans, little Great Heart—and may thousands of the same breed go with you. Why didn’t this happen when I was young!”

PART TWO<b>Chapter Thirty</b>

The village of Myonville, like many of the old Picardy villages in what had once been Ponthieu, hid itself in trees. You rode between the flat fields and the orchards, as young Seymour, the billeting officer, rode that May morning, and saw ahead of you a mass of towering elms and beeches, with a bit of white plastered wall showing here and there like a patch of light sky at the end of a wood. Even the church steeple was invisible, when the leaves were out, and the roads leading into the village were great green tunnels.

“It’s good,” said young Seymour to himself, “It’s damned good.”

And so it was.

For Myonville was a virgin village; troops had rarely been quartered there during the three and a half years of the war. It had had its sorrows, but they, like the village, were secret and beautiful, part of the low, white-walled and red-roofed houses; part of its orchards and bits of garden and great pensive trees. And the spring was in Myonville, an indescribable greenness, a glimpse of lush blue-grass under the pink and white of the apple trees, perhaps a clump of daffodils—goblin gold under the blue sky.

Young Seymour found the secretary of Monsieur le Maire. He was the schoolmaster, big, stately, with the baggy black clothes of a French civilian, shabby, but rather proud.“Billets? Yes. For a battalion, Monsieur le Capitaine?”

“Eight officers and seventy-three men and three horses,” said Seymour.

The schoolmaster raised his eyebrows.

“It is very small.”

“Well, you see,” said Seymour, “during the retreat the Boches were so loving that there wasn’t much left of us. Now, where am I to put the colonel?”

The placing of the colonel seemed to be the most sacred act in Seymour’s ritual.

“A big house, perhaps, Monsieur le Capitaine?”

“The colonel does not love big houses. A little house—with a garden.”

“There is the Café de la Paix.”

“Noisy?”

“Very quiet, monsieur.”

“Any children? The colonel loves children.”“There are plenty of children in Myonville,” said the schoolmaster with an amused twinkle.

So young Seymour went off with his list, and called first at the Café de la Paix. The choosing of the colonel’s billet appeared to be a very serious business, but as a matter of fact, it was a labour of love.

“Madame Lorellard? Bonjour, madame. I want to place Monsieur le Colonel in your house, with your good permission.”

Seymour was a pink and white boy, and very polite.

“Josephine,” called the old lady, “ici,” and she smiled like a red apple upon Seymour.

Josephine, the black, handsome Josephine, Josephine the talkative, the temperamental, came gliding out of the inner room.

“My daughter, monsieur. She manages everything.”

Seymour paid the colonel’s court to Josephine. She was put out, annoyed, haughty, yet polite. Strange officers were a nuisance. Besides, André, her brother, was expected home on leave, and she wanted the room for him.

“It is a very small room, monsieur.”“The colonel loves a small room mademoiselle. May I see it?”

Josephine took him upstairs, and was yet more annoyed when Seymour looked at the sheets.

“Monsieur, you insult me!”

“Oh, mademoiselle, I do not; I was admiring the linen.”

Someone had shown some sense in selecting Seymour as billeting officer.

He chalked the outer door and went about the rest of his business. Josephine was walking up and down the kitchen, talking in her excitable way to her mother and Cousin Claire. Claire taught in the school. She had been a solemn child, addicted to wild bouts of elfin mischief, a child with brown gold hair and great grey eyes.

“A colonel! Oh, la, la, he will be old and fussy, and he will want hot water at all hours, and baths, and he will stamp about the clean floors and scold. I know all about English colonels. Gabrielle had one at Doullens, cross and red as a lobster.”

“He can’t eat us,” said Claire, thrusting her needle into one of madame’s stockings she was darning.“No, but he will be cross, and he will be rude, and he will always be calling for red wine.”

“Josephine, the stew is boiling over,” said Madame Lorellard.

Josephine dealt with the stew, and Claire’s eyes had a little mischievous glimmer of light in them.

“What a monster you expect! But then, if he is old he will not be a nuisance in other ways.”

“Tiens!” said the black girl. “They are worse than the young ones sometimes. Old pigs!”

About three o’clock that afternoon Claire was washing lace in the little backyard and splashing soapsuds from her tub on to the leaves of the old pear tree trained against the wall. Madame had gone out into the fields to help old Lorellard with his hoeing; Josephine was in the garden picking herbs. Claire heard someone open the door of the café and walk in, and then an English voice called: “Madame!”

Claire went through the kitchen with a ball of wet lace in her two hands. The glass door leading into the café room was open. She saw an officer standing there.

“Monsieur?”He turned, smiled and saluted. Claire was strangely conscious of his eyes; they were blue eyes, rather sad, rather tired, but brave, very brave and so honest.

“I am billeted here, mam’selle.”

He spoke quite good French. She looked at his sleeve and Tod Western looked at her arms. Somehow he saw something very beautiful and peaceful and good in those plump arms of hers, the two strong little hands holding the ball of wet lace.

“But monsieur is so young!”

And Tod laughed.

“Mam’selle, did you expect a grandfather?”

“But we thought—and the young officer was so careful.”

Tod was still smiling, and she understood of a sudden why the tired but smiling eyes had brought an atmosphere into the house. As a woman she was quick to feel, though very slow to like. She noticed that he was wearing two ribbons, and that his left arm looked stiff.

“Monsieur would like to see his room?”She was pressing the ball of lace between her two hands; a drop of water fell on the floor.

“If you please,” said Tod, as though he were listening to some soft voice speaking inside him.

She put down the lace and dried her hands.

“It is a very small room, monsieur.”

Tod was absorbing all the pleasant homeliness of the place, the table scrubbed white as hollywood, the red-tiled floor, the black stove standing out into the room with the inevitable coffee-pot warming on it, the bright pots and jars, the plain wooden chairs. It was simple, austere, exquisitely clean. The doorway opening on to the yard showed Tod a garden and a great green orchard beyond it, and in the orchard a blackbird was singing.

His silence made Claire glance around at him as she dried her hands. He was looking out into the orchard and smiling, and his blue eyes were full of light. And again she understood; this was a man who loved simple things, children, dogs, a green tree in leaf, the singing of a bird, a field of corn rippling in the wind, red apples on a bough, a warm place by the fire.

“I beg your pardon, mam’selle,” said Tod abruptly, “but all this is so very good.”She crossed the kitchen and raised the latch of the door that opened on the stairs.

“Monsieur has suffered,” she said.

She ran up the steep stairs, a sturdy, graceful little figure, full throated, her old shoes making a merry clatter. Tod followed her, noticing the cleanness of that carpetless house, a row of shoes and sabots lined up on a shelf, a couple of mauve linen bonnets hanging on a nail, a table with clean linen laid out on it on the landing above. He had a glimpse of a room, a woman’s room with clothes hanging under a curtain and a red <i>duvet</i> on the bed. Claire had opened a door, and she went before him into the room.

“It is a comfortable bed, monsieur, and the room is quiet, for, as you see, the window looks out on the orchard.”

She was quite natural and unembarrassed, without a trace of coquettish false modesty.

“It is a very nice room,” said Tod.

“Is there anything that monsieur desires?”

“A table—a table to write on, if it will cause you no trouble. My servant will carry it in.”“We will find you a table, and it is no trouble.”

She left him and met Tod’s servant in the kitchen, a shy, rosy-faced boy playing at Atlas with the colonel’s brown valise on his back. She pointed him up the stairs, picked up the lace, and went back to her washing. She could hear the voices of the two men in the room above, and a sound of devoted activity on the part of the boy with the rosy face.

“You’ll have some hot water, sir?”

“If you can get it, Cane. Don’t worry the people.”

“I’ve got out a clean pair of pyjamas, sir. And your slacks.”

“They have promised me another table, Cane. I shall want it to-night.”

“It will be here, sir,” said the boy.

He spoke the truth, for Tod was a man who was loved with a very great love by all men who had faced death with him.

There were other voices now in the kitchen. Josephine had returned, and so had the old people, for Père Lorellard had hoed all the weeds.

“He is quite young,” said Claire calmly, drying her hands on a towel.“Oh, la la, the young ones can be a nuisance.”

Her cousin said nothing.

And then Tod descended upon them, a quiet man, very much at his ease, a man without guile.

He bowed to <i>grand’mère</i>.

“It is very good of you to take me in, madame. I shall give you no trouble.”

He bowed to Josephine, but a little more stiffly.

“My room is charming.”

That mercurial and handsome young woman became instantly the coquette. Would Monsieur le Colonel take a cup of coffee or a glass of red wine? Yes, the war, the terrible war! How much longer was it going to last, and would the Germans ever come to Myonville? Claire was busy hanging her lace on a little line near the stove. She appeared to be absorbed in what she was doing, and she took no part in the conversation.

Tod sat down and drank a cup of coffee, and talked to Josephine, whose elbows were on the table. She was leaning forward, her handsome and expressive face upturned, her black eyes looking straight into Tod’s blue ones.She liked homage, did Josephine; she liked to have a man <i>impressione</i>, interested in her and in her alone.

Tod gave it as his opinion that the Germans would never come to Myonville, and that they would go on fighting till the Germans were beaten. Josephine became voluble and eloquent on what France had suffered, how her brother André had been wounded three times, and how it was impossible to buy new clothes. Claire went on hanging up her lace, and saying nothing.

Tod looked at Josephine because he was a gentleman, but he looked now and again at Claire because he wanted to. A ray of sunlight was stealing through the window, and it fell upon her brown gold hair and white neck and plain black dress. She was a little woman, <i>la petite</i> Claire, but Tod had a feeling that there was nothing little about her. He liked her calmness, her quietude, those enigmatically frank grey eyes of hers, and the way she went on doing things.

“The aeroplanes, we hear them each night now,” said the dark girl, “and the bombs! Oh, la la! And Abbeville is being destroyed. I cannot sleep. I walk up and down, or run into the fields. It is so much safer in the fields.”

Claire spoke for the first time.

“If I am to be killed at night, I will be killed in my bed,” she said; “it will just happen so.”

Tod looked at her.“You will not be killed,” he said, “but you are very wise.”

Tod had work to do, a fatherly care for this battered remnant of his battalion, part of the battered remnant of a division. No one knew what they were doing in Myonville; Tod did not know, nor did the red men at D.H.Q. They had been pushed about the country, thrust into any odd corner, left on the rubbish heap so to speak, while over yonder men fought with their backs to the sea. Rumour had it that they were to train Americans; but neither the war, nor the Americans, nor the great spring offensive concern this tale—this green season in the village of Myonville in Ponthieu by the sea.

Tod spent an hour in the orderly-room, went round the men’s billets, inspected the kitchen, and was taken by Seymour to the officer’s mess.

“Is your billet all right, sir?”

“Just what I wanted, Jack. By Jove, aren’t those beech trees fine?”

“Gorgeous!” said the boy.

Tod wandered out after dinner into the lanes of Myonville. He had two letters in his pocket, but he did not want to read them yet. For Tod was sad, sad with the brave sadness of a strong man who had seen defeat, and whose heart was full of the thought of the men—his men—who were dead. He was sick of the war, soul-sick and very weary; there seemed no end to the bloody business, no end to the hopes that were dashed.“We’ve got to beat them,” was all that Tod would ever say; “we’ll go on till we do it.”

He was not a man of rhetoric, a man of phrases or of elaborate theories, one of your clever little people who sit in chairs in London and spill ink in wrangling over things they do not understand. Tod had no use for the war news of the journalists.

“Just tosh for the old women.”

He left it at that.

But these beech trees and orchards—these hills and little old houses and these rich fields! How good they were! Such gentle lights on the green grass, such mystery in the dim alleys of the orchards, such a smell of growth, such human tenderness! Tod’s heart had been made bigger by the war. The change in him could be summed up in those few words; while over in England! What had those damned “leaves” of his given him? Nothing, nothing at all.

It was growing dark when he returned to the Rue de l’Eglise and the Café de la Paix. Peasants were sitting at their doors, and Tod found the Lorellards grouped in the doorway of the café. It was a plain old brick house standing on the street, next door to the schoolhouse where Claire Vignard taught her children.

The whole family rose.“Please don’t get up,” said Tod.

He was introduced to Père Lorellard, a ruddy old man who kept an everlasting chuckle under his waistcoat, and whose blue eyes were full of mischief.

Tod sat down between Josephine and Claire. There was no embarrassment, no English awkwardness about these people. They accepted Tod, treated him with the exquisite and intelligent politeness of the French peasant, found their chairs just as comfortable although he had joined them. And Tod felt happily at home, the simplest of men chatting to two girls and two old people.

They praised his French.

“I’m half a Frenchman,” he laughed, passing his cigarette-case to old Lorellard.

Josephine was the ready coquette, making her dark eyes mysterious and mischievous in the dark.

“Monsieur will stay in France after the war, perhaps?”

“I’m married,” said Tod, glancing instinctively at Claire.

The fair girl did not look at him, but she had noticed a quick change in his voice.“And how many children have you?” madame wanted to know.

“None,” said Tod, like a man rebutting an accusation.

“Oh, mon Dieu!”—this was Josephine—“but the lines on my hand say that I am to have seven. Seven children, monsieur! Oh, la la!”

Little Claire broke in in that queer, abrupt way of hers.

“Why work if you are to have no children? What is the good of it—just for yourself?”

Her eyes met Tod’s in the dark, but she turned her head away almost immediately, and a silence fell upon Tod. A vague sadness seemed to steal in at the open doorway; and Tod rose and said “Good night.”

“I have letters,” he said.

Josephine lit him a candle, and waited for him to smile at her, but Tod did not smile.

“He is thinking of his home,” she said, when Tod had gone upstairs. “He is not happy.”

But Claire had a feeling that Tod was not happy because of his home.The colonel was sitting at his table, and he had pulled those two letters out of his pocket. One was from Mr. Burgetrode; the other from his wife. He read Mr. Burgetrode’s who had grown even bigger with the war, a creative giant labouring to beat the Boche. It was a long letter, an intimate letter, almost fatherly in some of its touches. Tod spent half of each leave with Mr. Julian Burgetrode; they had grown fond of each other for there was the same bigness in them both.

His wife’s letter brought a touch of grimness to Tod’s face. He was happy with his men, but miserable with those other memories. Millie had a little flat in town, and even a lieutenant-colonel’s pay was inadequate to explain her palpable opulence. Those “leaves” of his had worried Tod, sent him back to France with intolerance and suspicion in his heart. He suspected her of slyness, and of more than slyness.

“Have her watched,” some very merciless Stoic had said to him.

“I can’t,” Tod answered. “How can I? I’m up against death out here. That changes a man. Set some sneak to spy on her? Damn it—I can’t do it! I couldn’t do it—somehow—even if I felt sure——”

A woman would have said that Tod was right.<b>Chapter Thirty-one</b>

The Lorellards owned a little shop, the café, and a farm. The shop was next to the café, and you entered it by a separate glass-panelled door. Tod had to pass through the public room every time he left or entered his billet; it was a biggish room with a queer little bar, half a dozen clean white tables, a set of shelves almost barren of bottles, a dozen chairs, and a bracket clock. This room was nearly always empty. Sometimes Tod saw two or three old Frenchmen sitting at one of the tables; his own men never came there, for the Lorellards had no need to be very enthusiastic about the café. The shop was a more serious proposition, but the child of their hearts was the farm.

It lay beyond the orchard and a row of tall beeches, and Tod discovered it in company with Madame Lorellard. There were two little meadows surrounded with willows, a broad strip of arable land green with young wheat, a small wood of poplars, and a little field beyond the wood where old Lorellard grew potatoes and Indian corn. The barn and his stables were near the house.

Old Lorellard was always working. The back of his neck was all seamed with wrinkles, and the colour of leather. His hands were knotty and strong. In his old blue breeches and striped shirt, with a hoe in his hands, he was the very soul of France, shrewd, cheerful, ready with a mischievous gleam of the eyes under their badger grey eyebrows. He was a philosopher, and in his way—a thinker.

Old Lorellard astonished Tod. He could talk unexpectedly about so many unexpected things.“Socialism, monsieur? We do not need socialism in Myonville. Look, you, we all work here; there are no rich in Myonville.”

This was true.

“Moreover, there are no factories. It is the factories that make the trouble.”

And then he asked Tod a question.

“Is it true, monsieur, that in your war factories you pay the workers, even girls and boys, very much money?”

“That’s so,” said Tod.

“Then you will have much trouble after the war,” said old Lorellard; “no one will know the value of money or the value of good sweat. But in France we shall not have so much trouble.”

“Why?” Tod asked him.

Old Lorellard pointed to his crops.

“We grow things. We live close to Nature, and we know that she does not give if we do not work. And work is good. They say we peasants are misanthropes; sometimes it may be true. Look you, this land is mine; there are thousands of little farms like mine in France.We work; we do not waste money on things that do not matter. Paris is not France, monsieur.”

“Are you happy?”

Old Lorellard gave him one of his shrewd, smiling looks.

“Well, what would you say, monsieur?”

“I hope I shall be as happy at your age,” said Tod.

Madame was like her man, only she went to church and he did not. Her old sun-bonnet shaded a contented face, the face of a woman who had borne strong children, and who had a right to be proud. Even the war had not shaken her. Her boy’s three wounds were like red badges of honour over her heart.

She talked quite frankly to Tod, sensing him as a man without guile.

“My other girl is married, monsieur. They are good girls, both of them, though Josephine is a coquette. We do not spoil our girls, monsieur. They understand what is expected of a woman.”

“And your niece?” Tod asked with a certain shyness.

Madame’s eyes lit up. Her heart was very warm towards Claire.“A good girl, a very good girl, and so wise! She is an orphan, monsieur; her father was a railway official; she had a good education.”

“And she is not—not engaged?” Tod asked.

“Oh, no, monsieur. It is unfortunate, but her dot will be very small. Not but that men have asked for her, but she is very hard to please, is Claire. She looks with those grey eyes of hers and waits and listens. A good girl, so very strong yet gentle, and warm of heart, though she seems so quiet. Such a wife for some man.”

And Tod wandered off into the orchard with a sad and very thoughtful face. Why hadn’t he had the luck to find such a mate? There was something mysterious and fine and compelling about Claire.

The staircase of the house led down into the kitchen, and Tod had to pass through the kitchen whenever he went in and out. The Lorellards made but little use of the sitting-room. Tod would always be passing Claire. The school took but five days of her week, nor were the hours long, and she sat by the kitchen window with her workbox on the window-sill, and some work or other in her lap. She wore a rather shabby black skirt, a white blouse with a faint blue stripe on it, and cut low at the throat. On Sundays she wore a blouse with a pattern of little mauve flowers, and a black cotton apron covered with blue pansies.

Sometimes she smiled at Tod, sometimes she looked at him with a baffling seriousness and did not smile at all.She was not talkative; and though her fingers were always busy, she had an air of pleasant repose.

“Always working, mam’selle,” Tod would say to her.

Her replies were never obvious.

“I think while I work,” or

“A woman is not all fingers.”

“Do you ever go out?” he asked her.

She laughed.

“Has not Monsieur le Colonel seen me sitting in the orchard? And I walk every evening. There are always the children to walk with.”

“I wish——” said Tod, and then smothered the wish.

She guessed that he had wanted children.

Sometimes her face would glow with sudden light, and she would grow animated. She had a very charming and expressive mouth, and the most perfect white teeth; and when her eyes lit up Tod thought her beautiful, beautiful in a way that was new to him. There seemed so much behind those eyes.Yet she baffled him, this daintily shabby, silent, children-teaching Claire. She had such a sturdy poise of her own, the soft mystery of woodlands that do not rush to you but wait for you to come to them, and still remain mysterious. Tod found her serene, practical, strangely quick to understand him, ready to listen, slow to speak. She seemed to hold him at a distance. She was always a little way off.

He did not understand it then, that it was her strength, the strength of a Frenchwoman who knew the passion latent within her, not the Frenchwoman of the English idea, a woman far finer and more humanly wise than that. She had something sacred within her, a passionate spirit that no man should touch—until—— Until! That was the soft aloofness, Tod saw in her eyes.

Each evening Tod became part of the family. Sometimes they sat in the doorway, sometimes in the kitchen, sometimes in the orchard. Tod liked the orchard best. He lay on the grass and watched the last sunlight on the green boughs of the old trees and upon Claire’s face and hair. Josephine was the talker, and Tod could fly a gaiety that would match hers, but he did not look at Josephine as he looked at Claire.

Josephine made him talk.

“Were you always an officer, Monsieur le Colonel?”

Tod explained that he had begun the war as a private.“And such a boy! And what are the ribbons for?”

He told them, looking at Claire as he spoke.

“Just luck, you know.”

“And in England I suppose you are rich? I should like to be rich. I should have breakfast in bed each morning and in the winter I should go to Nice.”

“And you would be none the happier,” said Claire, with one of her quick flashes of aggressive truthfulness. “Too much money is like having too many aprons; you never know which one to wear.”

Old Lorellard chuckled.

“Did you ever hear such wisdom? Quite a female Solomon.”

Claire blushed.

Tod fought all through the summer months; in the autumn he fought at Le Cateau and Landrecies, and even before he had reached that October phase he was made to realise that he was not going to be killed.
