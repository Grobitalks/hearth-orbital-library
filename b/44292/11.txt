An important question now suggests itself, but no one has so far raised it: did the levies in these full numbers turn up? They are allotted in round figures: what proportion was actually furnished? That there would be some trouble in securing the conscripts is anticipated and provided for in severe measures for the contumacious.[88] This was usual, and even the strong hand of Edward I. could not prevent men from deserting after they had received their wages.[89] Here we have, also, a sufficient basis for an estimate. On May 12, 1301, Edward I. summoned for midsummer 12,000 men from nine of the counties included in the Bannockburn levy--York, as in that case, being assessed at 4,000.[90] On July 12 we have the numbers from these counties as they appear on the pay-roll, when it is stated that they had contributed in proportions which give only 5,501 all told; York having sent only 1,193, and Northumberland, assessed at 2,700, providing the largest proportion--2,019.[91] The numbers vary slightly on other days, but seem never to have exceeded, if they reached, 50 per cent.of the nominal levy. Mr. Morris works out the same result for the Caerlaverock Campaign of 1300.[92] There are no grounds for assuming that things went differently in 1314, and thus over 21,540 men are reduced by about half. It is quite a fair conclusion that not more than 12,000 English foot--which exceeds the proportion above--were actually present at Bannockburn.

I would suggest, therefore, for the English army the following round numbers: 3,000 to 4,000 horse of all sorts, 12,000 English and Welsh foot, 3,000 (?) Irish, 1,500 (?) foreigners, or, in a lump sum, 20,000 men of all arms, to which must be added a crowd of non-combatants--servants, traders, and camp-followers generally. Bain (as cited) proposes 50,000; Round, 30,000; Oman, 60,000 to 70,000. I consider 18,000 to 20,000 the most probable range. With even the lower of these numbers, the English commanders in organization and commissariat would have rather more than they could manage.

Barbour’s figure for the Scottish army must be similarly reduced. More than 30,000 would be a huge proportion of the Scottish population of that time, especially as the whole does not seem to have been drawn upon, and of that, as Barbour insists, a good many were still hostile.[98] William the Lion was credited in 1173 with a national host of 1,000 armoured horsemen, and 30,000 unarmoured footmen,[99] and the latter unit is surely over the score. At Halidon Hill, 1333, the Scots are said to have had 1,174 knights and men-at-arms and 13,500 light-armed men or foot;[100] and this chronicler consistently exaggerates. Yet these figures represent a united kingdom.Forty thousand at Bannockburn is the estimate for the Scots of the <i>Vita Edw.</i> writer, but the English writers, on their side, grossly overstate the numbers of the enemy, as witness what is said of Hemingburgh above. Bain’s figure of 15,000 to 16,000 is no doubt nearer the mark; “perhaps twenty-five thousand men in all” is Mr. Oman’s conjecture.[101] Possibly 6,000 to 7,000 is as near as we can go, adopting Barbour’s ratio, which gives a proportion of 1 to 3 of the English army. The non-combatants here, too, would be numerous. Up to this time Bruce’s men in the field could be numbered only in hundreds, so that as many thousands would represent a very special effort. And note that after Murray’s success over Clifford nearly the whole Scots army gathered round him to see him and do him honour--a fact which is suggestive[102] as to its size.

APPENDIX E

THE “ALEXANDER” AND THE “BRUCE”

<i>The Buik of the Most Noble and Vailyeand Conquerour, Alexander the Great</i> is an anonymous Scots translation of three French romances in the Alexander cycle, dated, in a rhyming colophon, 1438, and published for the Bannatyne Club in 1831. Between this translation and the <i>Bruce</i> there is a remarkably intimate and undisguised connection, not only in spirit and method, but in “the diction as a whole, the choice of words and the arrangement of the sentences, (and) the abundant use of alliteration,” to such an extent that “in reading the <i>Buik of Alexander</i> one would often think that he discerned the singer of the <i>Bruce</i>.”[125] A few examples have been given in the notes, but for a full survey of this literary phenomenon the reader must go to the dissertation quoted from above, or to Mr. J. T. T. Brown’s <i>The Wallace and the Bruce Restudied</i>, pp. 100-112 (Bonn, 1900), or Mr.Neilson’s <i>John Barbour, Poet and Translator</i> (London, 1900), which is devoted to the subject; or, for the parallels in the Bannockburn account, to Mr. Neilson’s article on Barbour in Chambers’s <i>Cyclopædia of English Literature</i>, vol. i.

[125] <i>Untersuchungen über das schottische Alexanderbuch.</i> Albert Hermann, Halle, 1893, pp. 26, 27.

On the facts there is no dispute; for explanation three hypotheses have been put forward. Hermann, accepting the 1438 date, concludes that the translator of the <i>Alexander</i> was so familiar with the language of the <i>Bruce</i>--“here and there, indeed, knew it by heart” (<i>stellenweise es wohl auswendig wusste</i>)--that his translation was necessarily strongly influenced thereby.[126] This is inadmissible; the French poems are earlier than the <i>Bruce</i>, and to these the links of connection ultimately go back. The relationship is really deeper than the mere language of the translation, as Hermann himself indicates. Mr. Neilson, accordingly, in a detailed and forcible argument, claims Barbour himself as the translator of the <i>Alexander</i>, arguing that, the literary proofs being so conclusive, the date given must be an error, “scribal or printer’s.”[127] Given Roman numerals to begin with, such a slip is not in the least unlikely; variations of this sort occur in the <i>Bruce</i> itself,[128] and 1438 may have been a misreading of 1338, or the date may be that of the scribe’s copy, not of the actual work. Mr. Neilson has an ingenious section on the wayward fortunes of dates.[129] Thus, reversing Hermann’s thesis, he holds that “Barbour’s mind and memory had been steeped in the <i>Alexander</i> when he wrote the <i>Bruce</i>.”[130] Mr. Neilson’s argument and conclusion are vigorously contested by Mr.Brown in a <i>Postscript</i> to the work cited. His more elaborate hypothesis is that David Rate translated the <i>Alexander</i> in 1437, and that “John Ramsay, Sir John the Ross, wishful to improve the plain song of John Barbour, used the translation of the <i>Alexander</i> extensively, taking freely whatever he required.”[131] Mr. Brown’s negative criticism is independent of this proposition which is involved in his wider theory regarding the construction of the <i>Bruce</i>. The eclectic conclusion of the writer in the <i>Cambridge History of English Literature</i>, vol. ii., is: “Either the book (<i>i.e.</i>, the <i>Alexander</i>) is the work of Barbour preserved in a somewhat later form, or the author was saturated with Barbour’s diction, so that he continually repeats his phrases.”[132]

[126] As cited, p. 35.

[127] As cited, p. 45.

[128] <i>Cf.</i> p. 292.

[129] Pp. 43-47.

MR. BROWN’S “SOURCES” FOR THE “BRUCE.”

In pursuance of his “hypothesis of fifteenth-century redaction” of the <i>Bruce</i>, Mr. Brown applies what he claims to be “fair and ordinary tests” to six “selected examples,” in order to show that his hypothesis “has a basis in demonstrable fact.”[134] I shall notice such of these very briefly, premising that I do not consider Mr. Brown’s use of his tests either “fair” or “ordinary.” So much, I think, will appear.

1. <i>The Trojan War</i>, <i>Alexander the Great</i>, <i>Julius Cæsar</i>, and <i>King Arthur</i>.(<i>a</i>) The only thing urged against the <i>Trojan War</i> passage[135] is that it is in the suspicious company of the others, and these, Mr. Brown suggests, are derived from Chaucer’s <i>Monk’s Tale</i>, from which he produces a selection of lines to parallel those in the <i>Bruce</i>. It may be urged,[136] at the outset, that two contemporary poets dealing with the same set of historical events are very likely to display similarities. As Chaucer himself begins by saying--

“The storie of Alisaundre is so commune,
That every wight that hath discrecioune
Hath herd somwhat or al of his fortune.”

[136] <i>Bruce</i>, I. 529-548; <i>Monk’s Tale</i>, Group B., 3821-3908.

Chaucer says nothing of “Babilony’s tour”; “his awyne hows” is no parallel to “of thyne owne folk”; and “Bot, ar he deit, his land delt he” has no equivalent whatsoever in the <i>Monk’s Tale</i>. (<i>b</i>) Mr. Brown argues that the <i>Julius Cæsar</i> parallels are “not less remarkable for significant agreement, as regards the sequence of the narrative,”[137] and that “so far as concerns the diction it (the <i>Bruce</i> passage) approaches even nearer the Chaucerian original.”[138] But where Chaucer speaks of Cæsar conquering “thoccident” and “the orient,” Barbour enumerates the countries. Is this similarity of diction? According to Mr. Brown, he is giving “simply an expansion of the Chaucerian phrases.”[139] Elsewhere, in such a case, Barbour is convicted of “summarising” or “assimilating,” here of “expanding.” This is Mr. Brown’s “fork” from which no author could escape. In twelve lines Barbour comments on Cæsar’s conquests, his imperial position, and his death, and in forty-eight Chaucer gives a detailed biography introducing Pompey, of whom Barbour says nothing, and Barbour is thereupon charged with following “the sequence of the narrative”--as if he could avoid doing so! That Cæsar by both poets is styled “Emperor” goes for nothing; that was the medieval way; as also was the statement that he was killed in “the Capitol,” as Shakespeare, too, believed. But the most striking note of difference remains. Barbour says of Cæsar--

“Hys eyn with his hand closit he,
For to dey with mar honeste.”[140]

APPENDIX G

LANGUAGE AND ORTHOGRAPHY

The language of the <i>Bruce</i> is Northern English, the dialect spoken north of the Humber. Barbour himself calls it “Inglis” (Bk. IV. 253), and Scottish writers down to the sixteenth century do the same. The name “Scots” is therefore a term of pure convenience, signifying the English spoken within the political borders of Scotland, which continued to be an independent literary medium after the Northern English of England had ceased to be such, and had yielded place to the standard dialect of Chaucer and his successors. But the language of the Aberdeen Barbour is substantially that of the Yorkshire Richard Rolle.

The most obvious characteristic of the northern tongue is that in development it was far ahead of its southern contemporary, in so far as it had absolutely got rid of inflections, not even retaining the final <i>e</i> which casts its shadow over Chaucer. Where the final <i>e</i> occurs in the <i>Bruce</i> it is non-significant and unsounded. Like the variable spelling, it is the result of the writing of English by scribes accustomed to the sounds and orthography of contemporary French, which was rich in final <i>e’s</i>[179]--a process which had seriously complicated the straightforward phonetic spelling of Old English.A peculiar Scots fashion, however, was the representation of the long vowels by a combination with <i>y</i> or <i>i</i>, sometimes in addition to a final <i>e</i>, which came to be regarded as the sign of a long vowel. Thus we have such groups as <i>mar</i>, <i>mair</i>, <i>mayr</i>: <i>done</i>, <i>doyn</i>, <i>doyne</i>. In these and similar cases <i>y</i> is simply an alternative form of <i>i</i>; at first it would be written before letters like <i>m</i>, <i>n</i>, <i>u</i>, where <i>i</i>, having no dot, might be mistaken for a part of the succeeding letter; then in practice the two would become interchangeable according to the caprice of the writer. In this matter the scribe of C is rather more regular than that of E. Now, such a diphthong as <i>ai</i> was in time reduced to a simple sound, when the <i>i</i> became superfluous, and came to be looked upon as a sign of length. With this function it was afterwards, at the pleasure of the writer, transferred to the company of other long vowels where there was no original diphthong. Thus we have the long vowel sound represented in three ways--simple, with following <i>i</i> (<i>y</i>), or with terminal <i>e</i>. In cases like <i>tais</i>, <i>gais</i>, however, the <i>i</i> is part of the termination of the present tense of the verb, of which Barbour is particularly fond.[180]

In general, these follow the common nouns in their forms, but note:

1. Two names ending in <i>s</i> have no inflection for the genitive: <i>King Adrastus men</i> (II. 529), <i>Thomas prophecy</i> (II. 86). This occurs also in Chaucer.

2. <i>Douglas</i> has both flectionless and inflected forms: <i>the Douglas men</i> (X. 398), <i>the Lord Douglassis men</i> (XX. 481).

1. There is no distinction in the <i>Bruce</i> between strong and weak adjectives, the latter in Chaucer being indicated by a final <i>e</i>, and used after the definite article, demonstrative and possessive pronouns, etc., nor is the plural similarly distinguished from the singular. In all these cases the Scots adjective is invariable; the final <i>e</i>, which often occurs, is of no grammatical significance. There is no difference between <i>the gud Erll</i> (X. 686) and <i>the gude Erll</i> (XX. 603), while the plural is <i>gud werriours</i> (XX. 416).

2. The only example of inflection is the form <i>aller</i>, genitive plural (<i>er</i> = <i>-ra</i>, A.S. <i>eal-ra</i>). It occurs once: <i>throuch thar aller hale assent</i> (I. 137).

3. The comparative and superlative are formed by adding <i>ar</i> or <i>er</i> (seldom <i>ir</i>) and <i>est</i> or <i>ast</i>. In words of two and more syllables <i>mar</i> (<i>mair</i>) and <i>mast</i> (<i>maist</i>) also occur. The same rule applies to adverbs. The final consonant of a monosyllable may be doubled: <i>thikkest</i> (VIII. 81), but also <i>thikast</i> (XVII. 156).

1. <i>Ane</i> before a consonant sometimes becomes <i>a</i>: <i>in a nycht and a day</i> (III. 429). <i>The tane</i> and <i>the ta</i> are compounds of this numeral, with the old neuter <i>thaet</i> (<i>thet</i>) of the definite article, really <i>thet-ane</i>, <i>thet-a</i>. The former is a substantive, the latter an adjective: <i>the tane suld be kyng</i> (VI. 186), <i>the ta part</i> (III. 239). Similarly <i>the tothir</i> = <i>thet othir</i> (I. 7, etc.), which is throughout used for the ordinal “the second,” as <i>formast</i> is sometimes used for “the first”: <i>Fule-hardyment the formast is, And the tothir is cowardis</i> (VI. 337, 338).

2. Note <i>thresum</i>, <i>fiffsum</i>, <i>sex sum</i>, where “sum denotes conjunction” (Jamieson’s <i>Dictionary</i>), three, etc., together; <i>half deill</i> (A.S. <i>dæl</i>, a part); <i>twa part</i> = two parts; <i>yneuch</i>, singular (usually adverb), used for quantity, and <i>ynew</i> (<i>enew</i>), plural, for number. <i>Cf.</i> XX. 337, and I. 558.

N.B.--For demonstrative, etc., adjectives, see also Pronouns, Demonstrative, etc. Note <i>sam(m)yn</i> (same) as adjective and adverb (V. 72, XII. 49, etc.). Distinguish <i>ilk(e)</i> = same (A.S. <i>ylc</i>) from the distributive <i>ilk</i> (A.S. _ æle_) = each. Others are <i>sik</i> (<i>syk</i>), <i>swilc</i>, etc.

PRONOUNS.<i>Personal.</i>--1. In MS. E besides <i>I</i> is found the strong form <i>Ik</i> or <i>Ic</i> (A.S. <i>Ic</i>). The latter is entirely wanting in C, which has everywhere <i>I</i>.

2. The third person feminine is <i>scho</i> (A.S. <i>sēo</i>), once spelled <i>sche</i> (XIII. 635), a Southern effect, where, however, E has <i>scho</i>.

3. Note the gen. sing. mas. and gen. plur. in <i>magre his</i>, in spite of him, and <i>magre thairis</i>, in spite of them.

<i>Possessive.</i>--1. The genitive cases of the personal pronouns are most often adjectives. Before words beginning with a vowel they are <i>mine</i>, <i>thine</i>; before words beginning with a consonant they become <i>my</i>, <i>thi</i>; words beginning with <i>h</i> may have either form.

2. The forms used absolutely are <i>myn</i>, <i>his</i>, <i>ours</i>, <i>yhouris</i>, <i>thaires</i>: <i>That suld be myn</i> (V. 223), etc.

<i>Reflexive.</i>--1. The personal pronouns serve as reflexives: <i>I will me spied</i> (XI. 638); <i>And went hyr hame</i> (V. 177).

2. There is also the form in <i>self</i>, more seldom <i>selvyn</i>, <i>selvin</i>. When the subject is a personal pronoun this may drop out: <i>that himself suld wele Kepe the entre</i> (XI. 445).

3. <i>Self</i> is both singular and plural: <i>That thai mycht help thameself</i> (X. 619).<i>Demonstrative.</i>--1. The Northern plural of <i>this</i> is <i>thir</i>, which supplanted <i>thas</i> as <i>thise</i> did in the Midland. Its origin is obscure.[185]

[185] <i>Murray</i>, p. 185.

2. The plural of <i>that</i> is <i>tha(i)</i>. Distinguish from <i>thai</i> (they), 3rd pers. plur.

3. <i>Yhon(e)</i>, more often an adjective, has also a substantive use in three cases only (IV. 502, 506; XIV. 280).

<i>Relative and Interrogative.</i>--1. The relative pronoun is <i>that</i> or <i>at</i>, never <i>quha</i>. <i>At</i> is a purely Northern form. <i>Quhais</i> and <i>quham</i> occur occasionally as oblique cases. In <i>quhom</i>, as in words like <i>so</i>, <i>mor</i>, etc., there is evidence of Southern influence. <i>Quha</i> is used as an interrogative. The simple <i>quhilk</i> occurs but once (XVIII. 225, see note), where E has a different reading; otherwise it is in the form <i>the quilk that</i>.

2. The relative in the oblique cases is often omitted: <i>the small folk thai had thar</i> (IX. 261). This is a marked feature of the <i>Wallace</i>.

<i>Indefinite.</i>--1. <i>None</i> for <i>nane</i> occurs only once (IX. 485); and so, too, <i>ilkone</i> for <i>ilkane</i>, rhyming with a proper name (XI. 303). <i>Nane</i> is used both as substantive and as adjective. As adjective it comes before a word beginning with a vowel. Before consonants and <i>h</i>, <i>na</i> or <i>no</i> is used. In two cases <i>no</i> precedes a vowel: XIII. 145; XVI. 249.2. <i>Othir</i>, often = “each other,” as <i>Thai dang on othir</i> (X. 680).

3. The plural of <i>man</i> has a pronominal use = <i>one</i>, German “man”: <i>as men in the Bibill seys</i> (I. 466); <i>men mycht se</i> (X. 678), etc.

VERBS.

1. Note the periphrastic form with <i>gan</i> in E (<i>can</i> in a few cases), <i>can</i> always in C: <i>all the land gan occupy</i> (I. 184); <i>all can thai cry</i> (XII. 200). The past tense of <i>can</i> is <i>couth</i>: <i>thar vittale all fast couth fale</i> (VIII. 460): whence, by analogy, the false form <i>begouth</i> for past of <i>gan</i>, itself the past of <i>ginnen</i>, to begin.

2. Weak verbs form their past tense and past participle in <i>it</i> or <i>yt</i>, the latter being more common in E. In certain cases the vowel drops out, as in <i>dwelt</i>. After <i>r</i>, <i>l</i> (<i>ll</i>), <i>n</i>, the ending is often in <i>d</i>, as <i>herd</i>, <i>ansuerd</i>, etc.

NOTE.--<i>Felt</i> for the ordinary <i>feld</i> occurs once, in III. 119. <i>Cald</i>, too, for <i>callit</i>, is forced to a rhyme (XIII. 61). The proper past of <i>send</i> is <i>send</i>.

3. The present tense indicative is, <i>I spek</i>, <i>thou spekis</i>, <i>he spekis</i>, <i>we spek</i>, etc. But when the personal pronoun is separated from the verb, or when the subject is a noun or relative pronoun or other form, the ending is in <i>is</i> or <i>ys</i> throughout. <i>Cf.</i> <i>yhow that takis</i> (XVI.592); <i>yhe that this redis</i> (VI. 269); <i>all men fleis the did</i> (IX. 90), etc.

4. The imperative plural is in <i>is</i> (<i>ys</i>): <i>departis us</i> (VI. 543). It is more rare without the ending (XI 309; XII. 227, etc). This, however, is the rule when the imperative is followed by its pronoun: <i>luk yhe</i> (XII. 217).

5. The present participle ends in <i>and</i>. Doubling of the consonant may occur after a short vowel--<i>e.g.</i>, <i>wonnand</i> (X. 160).

6. The verbal noun (so-called) ends in <i>yng</i> or <i>ing</i>. In many examples the <i>g</i> is dropped, as the tendency was and is in Scots, and we have a form in <i>yn(e)</i>. <i>Cf.</i> <i>fechting</i> (XII. 119) and <i>fechtyn</i> (III. 241). And these may rhyme, which shows that in sound the difference was not apparent (<i>cf.</i> IX. 120). Sometimes the particular form depends on the MS.--<i>e.g.</i>, VI. 520, C <i>supposing</i>: E <i>supposyn</i>. The same thing is found in proper names with a similar termination.

7. The past participle of strong verbs has ending <i>yn</i> or <i>in</i>, seldom <i>en</i>. <i>Clymen</i> in X. 648, where the correct form, <i>clummyn</i>, is given in 606, is either really a surviving or intruded infinitive, or has been assimilated to it.

N.B.--VII. 524, C <i>cum</i>: E <i>cummyn</i>; X. 506, C <i>won</i>: E <i>wonnyn</i>.

8. The infinitive has dropped all trace of an ending. The <i>e</i> which sometimes appears is silent. It is formed by prefixing <i>to</i> or <i>till</i>, in certain cases with <i>for</i> in addition.For one case we have a reduced infinitive with <i>at</i>: <i>a-do</i> (X. 348).

ADVERBS.

1. <i>him allane</i> is a strengthened form of <i>him ane</i> (VI. 272, 320), and is the more frequent of the two; equivalent to German “allein” and analogous in construction to the pronoun with <i>self</i>. In Early Scots <i>alane</i> is attached to the dative, in later and modern Scots to the possessive, <i>his ane</i>, etc.: the fuller form, from a confusion with <i>lone</i>, is now <i>his lane</i>, etc.

2. The surviving phrase, <i>the morn</i> (XIV. 478) occurs once for the older <i>to-morn</i>. Mr. Gregory Smith says that the former “begins to appear in M. Sc.” (Middle Scots, 1450-1600),[186] and MS. E indeed reads <i>to-morn</i> in the passage above.

3. Note the compounds with <i>gat</i> (a way), <i>thusgat</i>, <i>swagat</i>, etc.: in XIX. 253; C <i>swagatis</i>; E <i>swagat</i>: also <i>howgatis</i>, etc., preserving Genitive ending.

4. The forms <i>on liff</i> (XVIII. 154), <i>on slepe</i> (VII. 192), <i>on stray</i> (XIII. 195), etc., are peculiarly Northern forms for “alive,” “asleep,” etc. <i>Cf.</i> also <i>on fer</i>, <i>on flot</i>.

PREPOSITIONS.1. <i>Till</i> = <i>to</i>, the former being a Northern fashion. C frequently extends to <i>on-to</i>, <i>on-till</i>, as in VI. 622, XVII. 29, etc.

2. Note the verbal preposition <i>that I of tell</i>, etc.

3. <i>Be</i> and <i>by</i> are common to both MSS., C more frequently the latter, probably a Southern influence. Strictly in Scots <i>be</i> alone denotes agency; <i>by</i> usually = beyond, as in <i>by ordinar</i>, etc.

2. <i>the-quhethir</i> = <i>thohquhether</i> = <i>thohquether</i>, “nevertheless,” unknown to the Southern dialects.[187]
